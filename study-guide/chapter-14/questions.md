# Chapter 14: Introduction to Modern Portfolio Theory — 100 MCQ Question Bank

> Three tiers — 🟢 Easy (Recall), 🟡 Medium (Application), 🔴 Hard (Numerical & Scenario). **The two-security risk formula appears again and again — and the un-square-rooted variance is almost always one of the distractors.** Remember the **25% negative marking**.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy: Recall (Q1–Q35)

**Q1.** Modern Portfolio Theory was developed by:
A) William Sharpe  B) Harry Markowitz  C) Eugene Fama  D) Merton Miller
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Harry Markowitz**, in his paper *"Portfolio Selection"*.
A) Sharpe developed the CAPM and the Sharpe ratio. C) Fama is associated with the Efficient Market Hypothesis. D) Miller is known for the Modigliani-Miller capital structure propositions. Sharpe and Miller shared the 1990 Nobel Prize *with* Markowitz, but did not create MPT.
</details>

**Q2.** In which year was the paper "Portfolio Selection" published?
A) 1934  B) 1952  C) 1964  D) 1990
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Published in the **Journal of Finance in 1952**.
A) 1934 is the year of Graham and Dodd's *Security Analysis*. C) 1964 is when Sharpe's CAPM appeared. D) 1990 is the year Markowitz received the Nobel Prize, not the year of publication.
</details>

**Q3.** Modern Portfolio Theory is also commonly known as:
A) Technical analysis  B) Fundamental analysis  C) Mean-variance analysis  D) Behavioural finance
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Because portfolios are described by only two statistics, the **mean (expected return) and the variance (risk)**.
A) Studies price and volume patterns. B) Values securities from financial statements. D) Studies investor psychology and biases — in many ways the opposite of MPT's rational-investor assumption.
</details>

**Q4.** Under MPT, the risk of a security is measured by:
A) The standard deviation (or variance) of its returns  B) Its price  C) Its dividend yield  D) Its market capitalisation
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Risk is defined as the **variability of returns**, captured by variance and its square root, the standard deviation.
B) Price level says nothing about volatility. C) Dividend yield is an income measure. D) Market capitalisation is a size measure; large companies can still be volatile.
</details>

**Q5.** The expected return of a portfolio is:
A) The weighted average of the securities' expected returns  B) The highest security's return  C) Always lower than the weighted average  D) The lowest security's return
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Return combines in a **straight line**: E(R_p) = Σ wᵢE(Rᵢ).
B) Ignores the other holdings entirely. C) Confuses return with risk — it is **risk**, not return, that falls below the weighted average. D) Also ignores the rest of the portfolio.
</details>

**Q6.** The risk of a portfolio is normally:
A) Less than the weighted average of the securities' risks  B) Equal to the weighted average of the securities' risks  C) Greater than the weighted average  D) Always zero
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Whenever correlation is below +1, σ_p falls **below** the weighted average. This is the whole point of diversification.
B) Holds only in the special case ρ = +1. C) Portfolio risk can never exceed the weighted average. D) Zero risk requires ρ = −1 and precise weights.
</details>

**Q7.** The correlation coefficient always lies between:
A) −1 and +1  B) 0 and 1  C) −100 and +100  D) 0 and infinity
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Dividing covariance by both standard deviations bounds the result to **−1 to +1**.
B) Excludes negative correlation, which is central to diversification. C) and D) describe no correlation scale; covariance, not correlation, is unbounded.
</details>

**Q8.** If two securities have a correlation of exactly +1, the diversification benefit is:
A) Maximum  B) Zero  C) Moderate  D) Negative
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — At ρ = +1 the formula collapses to σ_p = w₁σ₁ + w₂σ₂, exactly the **weighted average**. No risk is removed.
A) Maximum benefit occurs at ρ = −1. C) Moderate benefit needs ρ meaningfully below +1. D) Diversification never increases risk above the weighted average.
</details>

**Q9.** The maximum diversification benefit occurs when correlation is:
A) +1  B) 0  C) −1  D) +0.5
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — At **ρ = −1** the two securities are exact mirror images and risk can be reduced to **zero** with the right weights.
A) Gives no benefit at all. B) Gives substantial benefit but not the maximum. D) Gives only a modest benefit.
</details>

**Q10.** A risk-averse investor is one who:
A) Requires additional expected return to accept additional risk  B) Avoids all investments  C) Is indifferent to risk  D) Prefers a gamble to a certain sum of equal value
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Risk aversion means demanding a **risk premium** for bearing risk.
B) Risk-averse investors do invest in risky assets — at the right price. C) Describes a risk-neutral investor. D) Describes a risk-seeking investor.
</details>

**Q11.** A risk-neutral investor makes decisions based on:
A) Risk alone  B) Expected return alone  C) Both risk and return equally  D) Neither
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Risk simply does not enter a risk-neutral investor's decision; only the **expected value** matters.
A) Reverses the definition. C) Describes a risk-averse investor. D) An investor with no criterion could not choose at all.
</details>

**Q12.** A risk-seeking investor offered a certain ₹5,00,000 or a 50-50 gamble paying ₹10,00,000 or nothing would:
A) Take the gamble  B) Take the certain ₹5,00,000  C) Be indifferent  D) Refuse both
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Both have an expected value of ₹5,00,000, but a **risk-seeker prefers the uncertainty**.
B) Is the risk-averse choice. C) Is the risk-neutral position. D) No investor type refuses a non-negative-value opportunity.
</details>

**Q13.** Covariance measures:
A) The average return of two securities  B) The dividend correlation  C) The total risk of one security  D) The extent to which two securities move together
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — It captures the **co-movement** of two securities around their own means.
A) Averages are computed separately as expected returns. B) Covariance is computed on returns, not dividends specifically. C) That is variance, which involves one security only.
</details>

**Q14.** The correlation coefficient between securities A and B is calculated as:
A) Cov(A,B) × σ_A × σ_B  B) Cov(A,B) / (σ_A × σ_B)  C) σ_A / σ_B  D) Cov(A,B) + σ_A + σ_B
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Covariance **divided by** the product of the two standard deviations standardises it.
A) Reverses the operation and would leave an unbounded number. C) A ratio of standard deviations says nothing about co-movement. D) Adding quantities in different units is meaningless.
</details>

**Q15.** Covariance can be recovered from correlation as:
A) ρ + σ_A + σ_B  B) ρ / (σ_A σ_B)  C) ρ × (σ_A + σ_B)  D) ρ × σ_A × σ_B
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Simply rearranging ρ = Cov/(σ_Aσ_B) gives **Cov = ρ σ_A σ_B**.
A) Adds incompatible quantities. B) Inverts the relationship. C) Uses a sum where a product is required.
</details>

**Q16.** The variance of a two-security portfolio equals:
A) w₁σ₁ + w₂σ₂  B) w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂²  C) w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂σ₁σ₂ρ  D) σ₁² + σ₂²
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Two variance terms plus the **interaction (covariance) term**.
A) Is a weighted average of standard deviations, not a variance. B) Omits the covariance term, which is precisely where diversification comes from. D) Ignores the weights altogether.
</details>

**Q17.** The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios that:
A) Have the lowest cost  B) Have zero risk  C) Contain the most securities  D) Offer the highest return for a given level of risk
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Equivalently, the **lowest risk for a given level of return**.
A) Cost is not a variable in MPT. B) With risky assets alone, no frontier portfolio has zero risk. C) The number of holdings is irrelevant; correlation is what matters.
</details>

**Q18.** A portfolio lying below the efficient frontier is:
A) Efficient  B) Inefficient — another portfolio offers more return for the same risk  C) Unattainable  D) Riskless
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — It is **dominated**: you can do better on risk, return or both.
A) Only points on the frontier are efficient. C) Points below the frontier are perfectly attainable — just not sensible. D) Position below the frontier says nothing about being riskless.
</details>

**Q19.** A portfolio plotted above the efficient frontier is:
A) The best portfolio  B) Unattainable with the given assets  C) The minimum variance portfolio  D) Inefficient
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The frontier is the **boundary of what is possible**; nothing exists beyond it.
A) It cannot be "best" because it cannot be built. C) The MVP is the leftmost point **on** the frontier. D) Inefficient portfolios lie **below** the frontier.
</details>

**Q20.** The Global Minimum Variance Portfolio is:
A) The highest-return portfolio  B) A portfolio with zero risk  C) The portfolio with the lowest possible risk from the asset set  D) The equally weighted portfolio
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — It is the **leftmost point** of the frontier.
A) That is the top-right end of the frontier. B) It has the lowest risk available, which is rarely zero. D) Equal weighting has no special optimality property.
</details>

**Q21.** The shape of the efficient frontier is:
A) A straight line  B) A circle  C) Convex, bending down  D) Concave, bending up and to the left
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Concavity follows from the diversification benefit at ρ below +1.
A) A straight line would arise only if all assets were perfectly positively correlated. B) The frontier is an open curve, not a closed shape. C) A convex frontier is impossible — combinations of two frontier points would beat it.
</details>

**Q22.** Under MPT, investors base their decisions on:
A) Return, risk, taxes and liquidity  B) Expected return and risk only  C) Past prices alone  D) Broker recommendations
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Utility is assumed to be a function of **E(R) and σ only**.
A) Taxes and liquidity are explicitly assumed away in the basic model — one of its practical weaknesses. C) MPT is forward-looking, based on expected distributions. D) No model assumes this.
</details>

**Q23.** MPT assumes an investment horizon of:
A) Ten years  B) Infinite  C) A single holding period  D) One day
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — The model is **static and single-period**, which is one of its criticisms.
A) Ten years is a specific horizon the model never assumes. B) An infinite horizon would require a multi-period model, which classical MPT is not. D) One day is likewise never assumed.
</details>

**Q24.** "Diminishing marginal utility of wealth" means:
A) Wealth falls over time  B) Each additional rupee of wealth adds less satisfaction than the one before  C) Utility is negative  D) Returns fall as wealth rises
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — It is why the utility curve is **concave** and why investors are risk-averse.
A) Describes wealth destruction, not utility. C) Utility of positive wealth is positive. D) Confuses the utility of wealth with investment returns.
</details>

**Q25.** Diversification under MPT eliminates:
A) All risk  B) Inflation risk  C) Systematic (market) risk  D) Unsystematic (security-specific) risk
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Only the **diversifiable, security-specific** portion can be removed.
A) Risk can never be fully eliminated with risky assets. B) Inflation risk affects the whole economy and is not diversifiable. C) Market risk affects every security and cannot be diversified away.
</details>

**Q26.** How many covariance terms appear in a three-security portfolio variance?
A) One  B) Three  C) Two  D) Six
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The pairs are **AB, AC and BC** — n(n−1)/2 = 3(2)/2 = 3.
A) One pair is the two-security case. C) Understates by one pair. D) Six is the **total** number of terms (3 variances + 3 covariances), not the covariance count.
</details>

**Q27.** In total, the variance formula for a three-security portfolio contains:
A) Three terms  B) Four terms  C) Nine terms  D) Six terms
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — **Three variance terms plus three covariance terms**.
A) Counts only the variance terms. B) Corresponds to no meaningful split. C) Nine is the number of cells in the full 3×3 covariance matrix, where the off-diagonal cells are counted twice.
</details>

**Q28.** Markowitz received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in:
A) 1952  B) 1976  C) 2003  D) 1990
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — **1990**, shared with Merton Miller and William Sharpe.
A) Is the year of the original paper. B) and C) are unrelated years.
</details>

**Q29.** "Garbage in, garbage out" in the MPT context refers to:
A) The output being only as reliable as the estimated inputs  B) Poor record-keeping  C) Transaction costs  D) Bad securities
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — The optimiser's mathematics is exact, but **expected returns, variances and covariances are forecasts**.
B) Refers to operations, not modelling. C) Costs are a separate omission of the model. D) The phrase concerns the input estimates, not the quality of the securities themselves.
</details>

**Q30.** The utility-of-wealth curve of a risk-averse investor is:
A) Concave  B) Convex  C) A straight line  D) Vertical
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Concavity reflects **diminishing marginal utility of wealth**.
B) Convex curves belong to risk-seeking investors. C) A straight line describes a risk-neutral investor. D) A vertical curve has no economic meaning.
</details>

**Q31.** Which term in the two-security variance formula creates the diversification benefit?
A) The covariance term 2w₁w₂σ₁σ₂ρ  B) w₂²σ₂²  C) w₁²σ₁²  D) The square root
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — It is the **only term containing ρ** and the **only term that can be negative**.
B) and C) are squared quantities and are always positive, so they can never reduce total variance. D) The square root converts variance to standard deviation; it does not create the benefit.
</details>

**Q32.** Correlation between two securities affects a portfolio's:
A) Expected return only  B) Risk only  C) Both return and risk  D) Neither
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Portfolio return is always the plain weighted average, whatever ρ is.
A) Reverses the truth. C) A very common error — correlation appears nowhere in the return formula. D) Correlation is the single most important driver of portfolio risk.
</details>

**Q33.** In portfolio optimisation, the "budget constraint" means:
A) Costs must be minimised  B) The client must have ₹50 lakh  C) The portfolio weights must sum to 1  D) Returns must be positive
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — **Σwᵢ = 1**: the whole portfolio, and only the whole portfolio, is invested.
A) Cost is not modelled in basic MPT. B) That is the PMS minimum, unrelated to optimisation. D) The optimiser places no sign restriction on returns.
</details>

**Q34.** The indifference curves of a risk-averse investor in expected-return / risk space are:
A) Downward sloping  B) Horizontal  C) Upward sloping and convex  D) Vertical
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — More risk must be compensated by more return, so the curves rise; they are convex because compensation is demanded at an increasing rate.
A) Downward sloping curves belong to a risk-seeking investor. B) Horizontal curves describe a risk-neutral investor. D) Vertical curves have no meaning here.
</details>

**Q35.** Owning thirty stocks from the same sector is best described as:
A) Markowitz diversification  B) Naive diversification with little real risk reduction  C) An efficient portfolio  D) The minimum variance portfolio
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The holdings are highly correlated, so the covariance terms stay large. **Diversification is about difference, not count.**
A) Markowitz diversification explicitly selects on low correlation. C) A sector-concentrated portfolio is almost certainly dominated. D) The MVP is defined by the optimiser, not by holding many similar stocks.
</details>

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium: Application (Q36–Q70)

**Q36.** A portfolio holds ₹6,00,000 in a fund expected to return 15% and ₹4,00,000 in a fund expected to return 8%. Its expected return is:
A) 11.50%  B) 11.00%  C) 13.00%  D) 12.20%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Weights are 0.60 and 0.40. E(R_p) = 0.60(15) + 0.40(8) = 9.0 + 3.2 = **12.20%**.
A) Is the simple average of 15 and 8, ignoring the weights. B) Understates both contributions. C) Overweights the equity fund.
</details>

**Q37.** ₹12,00,000 sits in equity expected to return 18% and ₹8,00,000 in debt expected to return 7%. The expected portfolio return is:
A) 12.50%  B) 11.20%  C) 14.30%  D) 13.60%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Weights 0.60 / 0.40. E(R_p) = 0.60(18) + 0.40(7) = 10.8 + 2.8 = **13.60%**, i.e. **₹2,72,000** on ₹20 lakh.
A) Is the unweighted average of 18 and 7. B) Reverses the weights (0.40 equity, 0.60 debt). C) Overweights equity.
</details>

**Q38.** A ₹10,00,000 portfolio holds ₹5,00,000 equity (15%), ₹3,00,000 debt (8%) and ₹2,00,000 gold (10%). Expected return is:
A) 11.00%  B) 12.50%  C) 11.90%  D) 11.30%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — 0.50(15) + 0.30(8) + 0.20(10) = 7.5 + 2.4 + 2.0 = **11.90%**.
A) Is the unweighted average of the three returns. B) Overstates the equity weight. D) Miscomputes the gold contribution.
</details>

**Q39.** A stock returns +30% with probability 0.25, +12% with probability 0.50 and −8% with probability 0.25. Its expected return is:
A) 11.33%  B) 10.00%  C) 12.00%  D) 11.50%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — 0.25(30) + 0.50(12) + 0.25(−8) = 7.5 + 6.0 − 2.0 = **11.50%**.
A) Is the simple average of the three returns, ignoring probabilities. B) Understates the boom contribution. C) Ignores the recession scenario's drag.
</details>

**Q40.** A stock returns +20% or 0%, each with probability 0.50. Its standard deviation is:
A) 0%  B) 10%  C) 5%  D) 20%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — E(R) = 10%. Deviations are ±10, so σ² = 0.5(100) + 0.5(100) = **100**, and σ = **10%**.
A) A two-outcome distribution is not riskless. C) Halves the deviation incorrectly. D) Is the range of outcomes, not the standard deviation.
</details>

**Q41.** Two funds have σ of 20% and 5%, weights 0.60 and 0.40, and ρ = +1. The portfolio standard deviation is:
A) 14.00%  B) 12.74%  C) 12.17%  D) 10.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — At ρ = +1, σ_p is simply the weighted average: 0.60(20) + 0.40(5) = 12 + 2 = **14.00%**. (Check: 144 + 4 + 48 = 196; √196 = 14.)
B) Is the answer for ρ = 0.30. C) Is the answer for ρ = 0. D) Is the answer for ρ = −1.
</details>

**Q42.** The same two funds, now with ρ = 0. The portfolio standard deviation is:
A) 14.00%  B) 12.74%  C) 12.17%  D) 148.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — σ_p² = 0.36(400) + 0.16(25) + 0 = 144 + 4 = **148**; σ_p = √148 = **12.17%**.
A) Is the weighted average, valid only at ρ = +1. B) Is the answer for ρ = 0.30. D) Is the variance, not the standard deviation — the square root was not taken.
</details>

**Q43.** The same two funds, now with ρ = −1. The portfolio standard deviation is:
A) 10.00%  B) 0%  C) 12.17%  D) 14.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — σ_p² = 144 + 4 − 48 = **100**; σ_p = **10.00%**.
B) Zero risk at ρ = −1 requires the specific weights 0.20 / 0.80, not 0.60 / 0.40. C) Is the ρ = 0 answer. D) Is the ρ = +1 answer.
</details>

**Q44.** Cov(A,B) = 90, σ_A = 18% and σ_B = 10%. The correlation is:
A) 0.50  B) 0.30  C) 0.90  D) 1.80
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — ρ = 90 / (18 × 10) = 90/180 = **0.50**.
B) Divides by the wrong product. C) Uses the covariance figure directly as if it were a correlation. D) Exceeds +1 and is therefore impossible for any correlation.
</details>

**Q45.** ρ = 0.40, σ_A = 20% and σ_B = 15%. The covariance is:
A) 0.40  B) 12  C) 300  D) 120
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Cov = ρσ_Aσ_B = 0.40 × 20 × 15 = **120**.
A) Is the correlation itself. B) Divides the product by 25 for no reason. C) Is σ_Aσ_B without applying the correlation.
</details>

**Q46.** Cov(A,B) = −60, σ_A = 25% and σ_B = 8%. The correlation is:
A) −0.60  B) −0.30  C) −3.00  D) +0.30
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — ρ = −60 / (25 × 8) = −60/200 = **−0.30**.
A) Misreads the covariance as a percentage. C) Lies outside the −1 to +1 range and is impossible. D) Loses the negative sign, reversing the economic meaning entirely.
</details>

**Q47.** Two assets each have σ = 20%, held 50/50 with ρ = 0. Portfolio risk is:
A) 20.00%  B) 10.00%  C) 14.14%  D) 0%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — σ_p² = 0.25(400) + 0.25(400) = **200**; σ_p = √200 = **14.14%**.
A) Is the weighted average, applicable only at ρ = +1. B) Would require a far more negative correlation. D) Zero risk needs ρ = −1.
</details>

**Q48.** Two funds with σ of 30% and 10%, held 50/50 with ρ = 0.50. Portfolio risk is:
A) 20.00%  B) 18.03%  C) 15.81%  D) 13.23%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — σ_p² = 0.25(900) + 0.25(100) + 2(0.5)(0.5)(30)(10)(0.5) = 225 + 25 + 75 = **325**; σ_p = **18.03%**.
A) Is the weighted average (ρ = +1). C) Is the ρ = 0 answer. D) Is the ρ = −0.5 answer.
</details>

**Q49.** Which of these pairs would give an adviser the greatest diversification benefit?
A) Two large-cap equity funds, ρ = 0.95  B) Two IT sector stocks, ρ = 0.88  C) An equity fund and a gilt fund, ρ = 0.10  D) A Nifty index fund and a Sensex index fund, ρ = 0.99
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — The **lowest correlation** delivers the largest reduction in the covariance term.

</details>

**Q50.** Portfolio P returns 12% with σ 15%; portfolio Q returns 12% with σ 18%. Which statement is true?

**Q51.** Portfolio R returns 10% with σ 15%; portfolio P returns 12% with σ 15%. Which is true?
A) R dominates P  B) P dominates R — same risk, higher return  C) Both are efficient  D) R has less risk
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — With identical risk, the **higher-return portfolio dominates**.
A) Reverses the dominance. C) R is dominated, so it cannot be on the efficient frontier. D) Both carry σ of 15% — the risks are identical.
</details>

**Q52.** For 50 securities, the number of distinct covariance pairs that must be estimated is:

**Correct: C)** — n(n−1)/2 = 50 × 49 / 2 = **1,225**.

**Q53.** For 50 securities, the total number of inputs (expected returns, variances and covariances) is:

**Q54.** Why does the efficient frontier stop at the Global Minimum Variance Portfolio at its left end?

**Q55.** Two clients look at the same efficient frontier and choose different portfolios. This is because:

### Case: Mrs Deshmukh's ₹50,00,000 Portfolio

Mrs Deshmukh, 48, has **₹50,00,000** to invest for a 12-year goal. Her SEBI-registered investment adviser proposes a two-fund portfolio:

| Fund | Amount | Weight | Expected return | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Diversified equity fund** | ₹30,00,000 | **0.60** | **14%** | **22%** |
| **Short-duration debt fund** | ₹20,00,000 | **0.40** | **7%** | **6%** |

The adviser estimates the **correlation between the two funds at ρ = 0.25**.

---

**QC1.** What is the expected return of Mrs Deshmukh's portfolio, in percent and in rupees?
A) 10.50%; ₹5,25,000  B) 11.20%; ₹5,60,000  C) 12.00%; ₹6,00,000  D) 11.20%; ₹4,48,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — E(R_p) = 0.60(14) + 0.40(7) = 8.4 + 2.8 = **11.20%**. In rupees: 11.20% × ₹50,00,000 = **₹5,60,000**.
A) Is the unweighted average of 14 and 7. C) Overweights the equity fund. D) Has the right percentage but applies it to ₹40 lakh instead of ₹50 lakh.
</details>

**QC2.** What is the portfolio's standard deviation?
A) 15.60%  B) 12.10%  C) 195.84%  D) 13.99%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — First the covariance term: 2(0.60)(0.40)(22)(6)(0.25) = 0.48 × 132 × 0.25 = **15.84**.
σ_p² = 0.36(484) + 0.16(36) + 15.84 = 174.24 + 5.76 + 15.84 = **195.84**.
**σ_p = √195.84 = 13.99%.**
A) Is the weighted average of the standard deviations, 0.60(22) + 0.40(6), which holds only at ρ = +1. B) Is the answer to QC4, after gold is added. C) Is the variance — the square root was not taken, the single most common error on this formula.
</details>

**QC3.** How much risk did diversification save her, and what would her risk have been at ρ = +1?
A) Nothing; 13.99%  B) 1.61 percentage points; 15.60% at ρ = +1  C) 8.00 percentage points; 22.00%  D) 1.61 percentage points; 13.99%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — At **ρ = +1** the formula collapses to the weighted average: 0.60(22) + 0.40(6) = **15.60%**. Her actual σ_p is **13.99%**, so diversification saved **15.60 − 13.99 = 1.61 percentage points**. Note her **expected return of 11.20% is unchanged** by the correlation — the saving is pure gain.
A) A benefit always exists below ρ = +1. C) 22% is the equity fund's own risk, not any portfolio outcome. D) Quotes the actual σ_p as though it were the ρ = +1 figure.
</details>

**QC4.** The adviser proposes restructuring to **50% equity, 35% debt and 15% in a gold ETF** (gold: E(R) 9%, σ 14%; ρ with equity 0.10, ρ with debt 0.05). The new risk and return are:
A) σ 13.99%; E(R) 11.20%  B) σ 14.50%; E(R) 11.50%  C) σ 12.10%; E(R) 10.80%  D) σ 12.10%; E(R) 11.20%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — **Return:** 0.50(14) + 0.35(7) + 0.15(9) = 7.00 + 2.45 + 1.35 = **10.80%**.
**Risk:** variances 0.25(484) = 121.00, 0.1225(36) = 4.41, 0.0225(196) = 4.41. Covariance terms: 2(0.5)(0.35)(22)(6)(0.25) = 11.55; 2(0.5)(0.15)(22)(14)(0.10) = 4.62; 2(0.35)(0.15)(6)(14)(0.05) = 0.44. Total σ_p² = **146.43**; **σ_p = 12.10%**.
A) Is the original two-fund portfolio, before gold was added. B) Adding a low-correlation asset lowers risk; it does not raise it. D) Pairs the new risk with the old return, ignoring that gold's 9% pulls the average down.
</details>

**QC5.** Six months later a global shock pushes the equity-debt correlation from 0.25 to 0.75. On her ORIGINAL 60/40 portfolio, what happens, and what is the lesson?
A) Nothing — the weights are unchanged  B) Her expected return falls to 9%  C) Risk rises from 13.99% to about 15.08%, wiping out most of the diversification benefit — correlations are estimates, and they rise in a crisis  D) The portfolio becomes riskless
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Only the covariance term changes: 0.48 × 132 × 0.75 = **47.52**, against 15.84 before.
σ_p² = 174.24 + 5.76 + 47.52 = **227.52**; **σ_p = √227.52 = 15.08%**.
Her risk rises from **13.99% to 15.08%**, and the benefit against the 15.60% weighted average collapses from **1.61 pp to just 0.52 pp**.

**The lesson — this is the whole of "estimation issues" in one number.** The formula never changed and the weights never changed; only one *estimated input* moved. Correlations are the least stable input in the model and they **rise towards +1 in exactly the conditions that make diversification matter most**. That is why an adviser should (i) present optimiser output as a range rather than a precise figure, (ii) **stress-test the portfolio with correlations pushed to +1**, and (iii) set the client's expectations on the stressed number, not the comfortable one.

A) Unchanged weights do not mean unchanged risk — risk depends on correlation too. B) Correlation never affects expected return; it stays at 11.20%. D) Rising correlation increases risk; it can never remove it.
</details>

# Chapter 4: Debt Management and Loans — Short Notes

> **Module 1 · Personal Financial Planning (37 marks).**
> ⭐ Heavily **numerical**. EMI calculations, amortisation, and the "prepay vs invest" decision are caselet staples. This chapter is Chapter 2's TVM formulas applied to borrowing.

---

## 4.1 Why Borrow at All?

Debt is a **tool**, not a moral failing. It lets a household:

- **Buy an appreciating asset earlier** than savings alone would allow (a house).
- **Invest in earning capacity** (education).
- **Smooth consumption** across a temporary income gap.
- **Handle emergencies** when liquid assets fall short.

The planner's job is not to eliminate debt but to make sure it is the **right purpose, at the right cost, over the right maturity, in an amount the household can service.**

---

## 4.2 Debt in Cash Flow Management

### 4.2.1 Consumption vs Investment expenditure

| | **Investment expenditure** | **Consumption expenditure** |
|---|---|---|
| **Creates** | An appreciating asset or future income | No lasting value |
| **Examples** | Home loan, education loan, business loan | Holiday, wedding, gadgets, dining on a credit card |
| **Borrowing verdict** | Generally **justified** | Generally **avoid** |

> 🧠 **The test:** *will the thing I'm buying still be worth something when the loan ends?* A 20-year home loan on an appreciating house passes. A 3-year personal loan for a 10-day holiday fails.

### 4.2.2 Identifying holes in the household budget
Persistent reliance on credit cards or overdrafts to reach month-end signals a **structural deficit**, not a temporary one. More debt cannot fix a deficit — only raising income or cutting expenses can.

### 4.2.3 Allocation across expense categories
Debt servicing competes with savings and living costs. Every rupee of EMI is a rupee unavailable for goals — which is why the **debt-to-income ratio** matters so much (Chapter 3).

### 4.2.4 Windfalls
Bonuses, inheritances and maturity proceeds should be **pre-allocated**: typically clear the costliest debt, then top up the emergency fund, then fund goals. Unallocated windfalls get absorbed into consumption.

---

## 4.3 Leverage and Debt Counselling

**Leverage** = using borrowed money to control a larger asset. It magnifies **both** gains and losses.

The four questions of debt counselling:

### 4.3.1 Purpose
Is it investment or consumption expenditure? (See above.)

### 4.3.2 Cost
The **interest rate**, plus processing fees, insurance bundled with the loan and prepayment charges. Compare on **effective** cost, not the headline rate.

**Typical cost ladder (highest to lowest):**
Credit card revolving (36–48%) → Personal loan (11–18%) → Gold loan / LAS (9–14%) → Vehicle loan (9–12%) → Home loan (8–10%) → Education loan (8–12%)

### 4.3.3 Maturity
Match the loan tenure to the **useful life of the asset**. Never take a 7-year loan for a phone. A longer tenure lowers the EMI but sharply raises **total interest**.

### 4.3.4 Debt rescheduling
When a borrower cannot service existing debt, options include extending the tenure, reducing the rate, converting to a lower-cost loan (**refinancing**), a **moratorium**, or a formal restructuring. All reduce immediate pressure but usually **increase total interest paid**.

---

## 4.4 Debt Servicing and Credit Scores

**Debt servicing** = the periodic obligation (EMIs) the borrower must meet.

$$\text{Debt-to-Income Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total EMIs}}{\text{Gross Income}}$$

Keep below **35–40%**. Lenders use it directly to decide eligibility.

### 4.4.1 Credit bureaus and the credit score

- **Credit Information Companies (CICs)** in India: **CIBIL (TransUnion CIBIL), Experian, Equifax, CRIF High Mark** — regulated by the **RBI** under the **Credit Information Companies (Regulation) Act, 2005**.
- The score typically ranges **300–900**; **750+** is generally considered good.
- **What drives it:** repayment history (the biggest factor), credit utilisation, length of credit history, credit mix, and the number of recent hard enquiries.
- **RBI rule:** every individual is entitled to **one free full credit report per calendar year** from each CIC.
- Checking your own score is a **soft enquiry** and does **not** hurt the score; a lender's **hard enquiry** can.

## 4.5 Responsible Borrowing

1. Borrow for **investment**, not consumption.
2. Keep debt servicing **within 35–40%** of income.
3. Match **tenure to asset life**.
4. Read the **effective** cost including fees.
5. Maintain an **emergency fund** so a shock doesn't create new debt.
6. Never borrow to **service existing debt** (the debt spiral).
7. Protect big loans with **term insurance** so dependants don't inherit the liability.

## 4.6 Secured vs Unsecured Loans

| | **Secured** | **Unsecured** |
|---|---|---|
| **Collateral** | Yes — asset pledged | No |
| **Interest rate** | **Lower** (lender's risk is lower) | **Higher** |
| **Amount available** | Larger | Smaller |
| **Examples** | Home loan, car loan, gold loan, loan against securities/property | Personal loan, credit card, most education loans (small ticket) |
| **On default** | Lender can seize and sell the security | Lender must pursue legal recovery |

---

## 4.7 Loan Terminology — Learn Every One

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Fixed rate loan** | Rate locked for the tenure (or a stated period). Certainty, usually a higher starting rate |
| **Variable/floating rate loan** | Rate moves with a benchmark (repo rate / MCLR / external benchmark). Cheaper initially, but rate risk sits with the borrower |
| **Home equity loan** | Borrowing against the equity built up in a home already owned |
| **Hire purchase** | Possession and use now; **ownership transfers only on the final instalment** |
| **Leasing** | Right to *use* an asset for a rental; **ownership never transfers** to the user |
| **Amortisation** | Repaying principal + interest through equal instalments over the tenure |
| **EMI** | Equated Monthly Instalment — the constant monthly payment |
| **Refinancing** | Replacing an existing loan with a new one, usually at a lower rate (also "balance transfer") |
| **Prepayment** | Paying more than the scheduled amount, reducing principal |
| **Pre-EMI interest** | On a partly-disbursed loan (e.g. under-construction property), the borrower pays **interest only** on the amount disbursed; principal repayment starts after full disbursement |
| **Moratorium** | An agreed period of no repayment (or interest-only). **Interest usually still accrues** |
| **Mortgage** | Security created over **immovable** property |
| **Pledge** | Security over **movable** property where **possession passes** to the lender (e.g. gold loan) |
| **Hypothecation** | Security over **movable** property where **possession stays with the borrower** (e.g. car loan) |

> ⚠️ **Guaranteed exam question:** **Mortgage = immovable. Pledge = movable, lender holds it. Hypothecation = movable, borrower keeps it.** A gold loan is a *pledge*; a car loan is a *hypothecation*; a home loan is a *mortgage*.

## 4.8 Types of Borrowing

| Loan | Security | Typical use / notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Home loan** | Mortgage of the property | Longest tenure (up to 30 years), lowest rates, tax benefits |
| **Education loan** | Often unsecured up to a limit; collateral above it | Usually a **moratorium** during the course plus 6–12 months |
| **Vehicle loan** | Hypothecation of the vehicle | 3–7 years; the asset **depreciates** |
| **Business loan** | Secured or unsecured | For working capital or expansion |
| **Personal loan** | Unsecured | Highest-cost mainstream loan; no end-use restriction |
| **Credit card debt** | Unsecured | **The most expensive** — 36–48% annualised on revolving balances |
| **Overdraft** | Against a deposit/security or clean | Interest charged only on the amount used, only for the days used |
| **Loan against securities (LAS)** | Pledge of shares/MF units | Quick liquidity without selling; subject to **margin calls** if values fall |
| **Loan against insurance policy** | Assignment of the policy | Available once a surrender value exists |
| **Loan against property (LAP)** | Mortgage of property | Larger amounts, lower rate than a personal loan |
| **Gold loan** | Pledge of gold | Fast disbursal, moderate rates |
| **P2P lending** | Usually unsecured | RBI-regulated NBFC-P2P platforms match individual lenders and borrowers |

> 💡 **Credit card mechanics:** paying the "minimum due" avoids a late fee but **not** interest — the remaining balance revolves at 3–4% *per month*. And once a balance revolves, the interest-free period on *new* purchases is typically lost.

## 4.9 ⭐ Loan Calculations

### The EMI formula

$$\boxed{EMI = P \times \frac{r(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n - 1}}$$

where **P** = principal, **r** = **monthly** rate (annual ÷ 12), **n** = number of **months**.

**Example:** ₹40,00,000 at 9% for 20 years.
r = 0.09 ÷ 12 = **0.0075**; n = **240**; (1.0075)²⁴⁰ = **6.00915**.
EMI = 40,00,000 × (0.0075 × 6.00915) ÷ (6.00915 − 1) = 40,00,000 × 0.04506862 ÷ 5.00915 = **₹35,989**.

Excel: `=PMT(9%/12, 240, -4000000)`

### Total interest

$$\text{Total Interest} = (EMI \times n) - P$$

Above: (35,989 × 240) − 40,00,000 = 86,37,360 − 40,00,000 = **₹46,37,360** — more than the loan itself.

### 4.7.6 The amortisation schedule

Each EMI splits into interest and principal:

- **Interest for the month** = Outstanding balance × monthly rate
- **Principal for the month** = EMI − Interest
- **New balance** = Old balance − Principal repaid

**First three months of the loan above:**

| Month | Opening balance | EMI | Interest | Principal | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40,00,000 | 35,989 | 30,000 | 5,989 | 39,94,011 |
| 2 | 39,94,011 | 35,989 | 29,955 | 6,034 | 39,87,977 |
| 3 | 39,87,977 | 35,989 | 29,910 | 6,079 | 39,81,898 |

> 🧠 **The critical insight:** early EMIs are **almost entirely interest**. In month 1, ₹30,000 of a ₹35,989 EMI (83%) is interest and only ₹5,989 reduces the debt. The principal share rises slowly and only crosses 50% around the **two-thirds** point of the tenure.
>
> **Two consequences:** (1) prepayments made **early** save dramatically more interest than the same amount later; (2) after paying for 8 of 20 years, the borrower has repaid far less principal than they assume.

## 4.11 Repayment with Varying Interest Rates

On a **floating rate** loan, when the benchmark moves the lender must adjust something. Two choices:

| | **Keep EMI, change tenure** | **Keep tenure, change EMI** |
|---|---|---|
| **Rate rises** | Tenure extends | EMI rises |
| **Rate falls** | Tenure shortens | EMI falls |
| **Cash flow impact** | None immediately | Immediate |
| **Total interest** | Higher when rates rise | Lower |

**Default practice** in India is usually to hold the EMI and change the tenure — but if the rate rises far enough, the EMI may no longer cover even the interest, forcing an EMI increase.

### 4.13 Should you change the EMI or the tenure when rates change?

| Client situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Cash flow is tight | **Keep the EMI**, let the tenure extend |
| Cash flow is comfortable | **Keep the tenure** (raise the EMI) — far less total interest |
| Rates have **fallen** and the client can afford it | **Keep the EMI** and let the tenure shorten — the single most powerful interest saver |

> 🔑 **When rates fall, the best move is to keep paying the same EMI.** The extra goes entirely to principal, cutting years off the loan.

## 4.10 Loan Restructuring — Present Value of Future Payments

The outstanding balance of a loan at any moment is simply the **present value of all remaining EMIs**, discounted at the loan rate:

$$\text{Outstanding} = EMI \times \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-m}}{r}$$

where **m** = remaining months. This is how a lender computes a foreclosure amount, and how you compare restructuring options.

---

## 4.12 Evaluating Loans — What to Compare

1. **Effective interest rate** including processing fees and bundled insurance
2. **Fixed vs floating** and the reset frequency
3. **Prepayment/foreclosure charges** — floating-rate home loans to individuals have **no prepayment penalty** under RBI rules; fixed-rate loans may carry one
4. **Tenure flexibility**
5. **Total interest outgo**, not just the EMI
6. **Tax treatment** where applicable

> ⚠️ **The EMI illusion:** a lower EMI usually means a longer tenure and **more** total interest. Always compare **total interest**, not the monthly figure.

---

## 4.14 Should You Invest or Repay the Loan?

**The rule:**

$$\textbf{Compare the loan rate with the expected POST-TAX return on the investment.}$$

- If **loan rate > post-tax expected return** → **repay the loan**. Repayment is a **risk-free, tax-free return equal to the interest rate.**
- If **post-tax expected return > loan rate**, with a comfortable margin for risk → investing may be better.

**Worked comparison:** a client has ₹5,00,000 spare.

| | Repay a 14% personal loan | Invest at an expected 11% |
|---|---|---|
| Return | **14% certain, tax-free** | 11% expected, **uncertain**, taxable |
| Risk | Zero | Market risk |
| **Verdict** | ✅ **Repay** | ❌ |

But against a **8.5% home loan** with a 20-year equity horizon, investing may win — the expected equity return exceeds 8.5% and the horizon allows volatility to average out.

> ⚠️ Two caveats: (1) **never** deplete the emergency fund to prepay; (2) a **certain** 9% beats an *expected* 11% for a risk-averse client or a short horizon.

## 4.15 ⭐ Strategies to Get Rid of Debt Faster

Three named methods — an exam favourite:

### Avalanche
Repay the **highest interest rate first** (while paying minimums on the rest).
✅ **Mathematically optimal** — minimises total interest paid.
❌ If the costliest loan is also the largest, motivation can fade before the first win.

### Snowball
Repay the **smallest balance first**, regardless of rate.
✅ **Psychologically powerful** — quick wins build momentum.
❌ Costs more in total interest than the avalanche.

### Blizzard
A **hybrid**: clear one or two small balances first for momentum (snowball), then switch to attacking the highest rate (avalanche).
✅ Balances motivation and mathematics.

> 🧠 **Memory hook:** **Ava**lanche → **A** for **A**PR (rate). **Snow**ball → **S** for **S**mallest balance. **Blizzard** → a mix of snow and avalanche.

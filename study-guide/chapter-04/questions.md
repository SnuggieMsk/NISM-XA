## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy: Recall (Q1–Q35)

**Q1.** The EMI formula is:
A) P × r × n  B) P × r(1+r)ⁿ ÷ [(1+r)ⁿ − 1]  C) P ÷ n  D) P × (1+r)ⁿ
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The EMI is the payment whose present value equals the loan: **P × r(1+r)ⁿ ÷ [(1+r)ⁿ − 1]**, with r monthly and n in months.
A) Computes simple interest. C) Repays principal only, with no interest. D) Is the future value of a lump sum.
</details>

**Q2.** In the EMI formula, "r" must be:
A) The annual interest rate  B) The monthly interest rate (annual ÷ 12)  C) The effective annual rate  D) The repo rate
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — For monthly instalments, **r and n must both be monthly**: r = annual ÷ 12, n = years × 12.
A) Mixing an annual rate with monthly periods is the single most common error. C) EAR is used for comparing deposits, not in the EMI formula. D) The repo rate is a benchmark, not the borrower's rate.
</details>

**Q3.** A mortgage is security created over:

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — A **mortgage** is over **immovable** property (land, buildings).
A) Describes a **pledge**. B) Describes **hypothecation**. D) A deposit-backed loan is a lien, not a mortgage.
</details>

**Q4.** In a pledge:

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium: Application (Q36–Q70)

**Q36.** A ₹30,00,000 home loan at 8.5% for 20 years. What is the EMI?
A) ₹21,250  B) ₹26,035  C) ₹12,500  D) ₹32,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — r = 0.085 ÷ 12 = **0.0070833**; n = 240; (1.0070833)²⁴⁰ = **5.44127**. EMI = 30,00,000 × (0.0070833 × 5.44127) ÷ (5.44127 − 1) = 30,00,000 × 0.0385424 ÷ 4.44127 = **₹26,035**.
A) ₹21,250 is the **first month's interest** (30,00,000 × 0.0070833). C) ₹12,500 is principal ÷ 240. D) ₹32,000 overstates it. Excel: `=PMT(8.5%/12,240,-3000000)`.
</details>

**Q37.** For the Q36 loan, what is the interest component of the FIRST EMI?
A) ₹26,035  B) ₹21,250  C) ₹4,785  D) ₹12,500
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Interest = outstanding × monthly rate = 30,00,000 × 0.0070833 = **₹21,250**.
A) Is the full EMI. C) ₹4,785 is the **principal** component (26,035 − 21,250). D) Is principal ÷ n. **Note: 82% of the first EMI is interest.**

**Q38.** Continuing Q36–Q37, what is the outstanding balance after the first EMI?

**Correct: A)** — r = 0.12 ÷ 12 = **0.01**; n = 96; (1.01)⁹⁶ = **2.59927**. EMI = 18,00,000 × (0.01 × 2.59927) ÷ (2.59927 − 1) = 18,00,000 × 0.0259927 ÷ 1.59927 = **₹29,255**.
B) ₹18,750 is principal ÷ 96 (18,00,000 ÷ 96), ignoring interest entirely. C) ₹18,000 is the **first month's interest** (18,00,000 × 0.01). D) ₹35,000 overstates it. Excel: `=PMT(12%/12,96,-1800000)`.
</details>

**Q92.** A client with a floating-rate home loan sees rates fall from 9.5% to 8.5%. She has comfortable cash flow. Comparing the two options on a ₹40,00,000 loan with 18 years remaining:
A) Reducing the EMI saves more interest  B) Keeping the EMI constant shortens the tenure by roughly 2–3 years and saves several lakhs of interest  C) There is no difference  D) She should refinance to a fixed rate immediately
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — At 8.5% the required EMI falls, so continuing to pay the **old, higher EMI** sends the surplus entirely to principal. On a loan of this size the tenure typically shortens by **2–3 years**, saving several lakh rupees.
A) A lower EMI stretches the loan and saves the least. C) The difference is material. D) Switching to fixed just as rates fall locks in the *higher* environment — usually the wrong moment.
</details>

**Q93.** A borrower has ₹4,50,000 available. Debts: card ₹1,80,000 at 40%, personal loan ₹2,00,000 at 15%, car loan ₹3,00,000 at 10%. He also has no emergency fund and monthly expenses of ₹60,000. What is the best allocation?
A) Clear all of the car loan  B) Clear the card (₹1,80,000), clear the personal loan (₹2,00,000), and keep ₹70,000 as the start of an emergency buffer  C) Put everything into equity  D) Clear the card only and spend the rest
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Avalanche order attacks **40% then 15%**, saving roughly ₹72,000 + ₹30,000 = **₹1,02,000 of interest in year one**. The remaining ₹70,000 begins the buffer (about 1.2 months) so a future shock doesn't recreate card debt.
A) The car loan at 10% is the cheapest of the three. C) No investment reliably beats 40%. D) Leaves 15% debt outstanding while spending. **Note the balance struck: clear expensive debt, but do not end up with zero liquidity.**
</details>

**Q94.** Which statement about the relationship between EMI, tenure and total interest is correct?
A) Doubling the tenure roughly halves the total interest  B) Doubling the tenure lowers the EMI but can more than double the total interest  C) Tenure affects only the EMI  D) Total interest is fixed regardless of tenure
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — On the ₹60,00,000 at 9% example: 20 years costs ₹69.56 lakh of interest; 25 years costs ₹91.05 lakh. Extending further compounds the effect sharply.
A) Reverses the relationship entirely. C) Tenure drives **both** the EMI and total interest. D) Total interest is highly sensitive to tenure.
</details>

**Q95.** A client wants to borrow ₹3,00,000 for her daughter's wedding. Which is the soundest advice?
A) Use a credit card for the flexibility  B) This is consumption expenditure — prefer saving in advance; if borrowing is unavoidable, use the cheapest secured option (e.g. gold loan or LAS) over a personal loan or card  C) Take a 10-year personal loan for a low EMI  D) Borrow ₹10,00,000 to have a buffer
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A wedding creates **no lasting asset**, so it is consumption expenditure. Where borrowing cannot be avoided, minimise the cost by using **secured** credit.
A) Cards at 36–48% are the worst possible choice. C) A long tenure on consumption debt multiplies total interest. D) Borrowing more than needed is straightforwardly value-destructive.
</details>

**Q96.** A ₹10,00,000 loan at 10% has an EMI of ₹21,247 over 5 years. In month 1, how is the EMI split?
A) Interest ₹8,333; principal ₹12,914  B) Interest ₹12,914; principal ₹8,333  C) Interest ₹21,247; principal nil  D) Interest ₹1,00,000; principal nil
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Interest = 10,00,000 × (0.10 ÷ 12) = 10,00,000 × 0.008333 = **₹8,333**. Principal = 21,247 − 8,333 = **₹12,914**.
B) Reverses the two. C) Some principal is always repaid on an amortising loan. D) ₹1,00,000 is a full year's interest. **Contrast with the 20-year home loan where interest was 82% of the first EMI — on a 5-year loan, principal already dominates at 61%.**
</details>

**Q97.** Which action most directly reduces a client's leverage ratio?
A) Taking a top-up loan  B) Using surplus cash to prepay principal  C) Revaluing the house upward  D) Extending the loan tenure
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Leverage = liabilities ÷ assets. Prepaying reduces **liabilities** (and cash), lowering the ratio.
A) Raises liabilities. C) Raising asset values lowers the ratio arithmetically but is a **paper** change, not a real reduction in debt. D) Changes timing, not the outstanding balance.
</details>

**Q98.** A client is 5 years into a 20-year, ₹50,00,000 home loan at 9% (EMI ₹44,986) and is offered refinancing at 8% with a ₹40,000 fee. Should she refinance?
A) No, fees always outweigh the benefit  B) Yes — a 1 percentage-point saving on a large outstanding balance over 15 remaining years far exceeds ₹40,000  C) Only if the tenure is extended  D) Only if she switches to a fixed rate
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Outstanding after 5 years ≈ **₹44.3 lakh**. Saving 1 percentage point on roughly that balance is on the order of **₹40,000 in the first year alone**, and the benefit continues for 15 more years — a total saving in the lakhs against a one-time ₹40,000.
A) Fees matter but must be weighed against the saving. C) Extending the tenure would erode the benefit. D) The rate type is a separate decision. **Rule of thumb: refinancing is usually worthwhile when the rate gap is ≥0.5pp and substantial tenure remains.**
</details>

**Q99.** A household's debt profile: home loan ₹35,00,000 at 8.5% (EMI ₹30,400), car loan ₹4,00,000 at 11% (EMI ₹10,400), personal loan ₹3,00,000 at 17% (EMI ₹10,700). Gross income ₹1,60,000/month. What is the priority action?
A) Prepay the home loan  B) Debt-to-income is 32% (acceptable); direct any surplus at the 17% personal loan first  C) Take another loan to consolidate at 20%  D) Do nothing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Total EMIs = 30,400 + 10,400 + 10,700 = **₹51,500**; ratio = 51,500 ÷ 1,60,000 = **32%**, inside the healthy range. So the household is not distressed — the optimisation is simply to attack the **costliest debt (17%)** first.
A) The home loan at 8.5% is the cheapest. C) Consolidating at a *higher* rate destroys value. D) Leaving 17% debt outstanding while having surplus is a missed guaranteed return.

**Q100.** Which single principle best summarises sound debt management?
A) Avoid all debt at any cost  B) Borrow for assets that outlast the loan, at the lowest cost, over a tenure matched to the asset, within 35–40% of income  C) Take the loan with the lowest EMI  D) Always choose the longest tenure
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

## 🧩 Worked Caselet — Exam-Style Practice

> **Attempt all five sub-questions before checking any answer.** Convert every rate to **monthly** and every tenure to **months** before computing.

### Case: The Menon Family's Debt Review

Arjun Menon, 38, earns **₹2,60,000 a month** gross. His wife earns **₹90,000 a month**.

**Their loans:**

| Loan | Outstanding | Rate | EMI | Remaining tenure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Home loan (floating) | ₹52,00,000 | 9.0% | ₹49,000 | 15 years |
| Car loan | ₹5,20,000 | 10.5% | ₹13,400 | 4 years |
| Personal loan | ₹4,00,000 | 16.5% | ₹14,200 | 3 years |
| Credit card (revolving) | ₹2,10,000 | 42.0% | ₹10,500 (minimum) | — |

**Monthly household expenses excluding EMIs:** ₹1,40,000.
**Liquid assets:** ₹2,80,000. **Other investments:** ₹16,00,000 in equity mutual funds.

Arjun has just received an annual bonus of **₹7,00,000**.

---

**QC1.** What is the Menons' debt-to-income ratio?
A) 21.9%  B) 24.9%  C) 33.5%  D) 45.0%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Combined gross income** = 2,60,000 + 90,000 = **₹3,50,000**. **Total monthly debt servicing** = home ₹49,000 + car ₹13,400 + personal ₹14,200 + card minimum ₹10,500 = **₹87,100**. Ratio = 87,100 ÷ 3,50,000 = **24.9%**.
A) 21.9% omits the credit card minimum — a common slip, since the card has no formal "EMI" but its minimum due is a real monthly obligation. C) 33.5% wrongly uses only Arjun's ₹2,60,000 income. D) 45% does not follow. **Verdict: comfortably inside the 35–40% guideline — the Menons' problem is not the *amount* of debt but its *cost*, with 42% card debt outstanding.**
</details>

**QC2.** Under the avalanche method, how should the ₹7,00,000 bonus be applied?
A) Prepay the home loan  B) Clear the card (₹2,10,000 at 42%), then the personal loan (₹4,00,000 at 16.5%), leaving ₹90,000  C) Clear the car loan first  D) Invest it all in equity funds
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Avalanche orders strictly by **rate**: 42% → 16.5% → 10.5% → 9.0%. The ₹7,00,000 clears the **card (₹2,10,000)** and the **personal loan (₹4,00,000)**, leaving **₹90,000**.
A) The home loan at 9% is the **cheapest** debt and the last to prepay. C) The car loan at 10.5% is cheaper than both the card and the personal loan. D) No equity return reliably beats 42%.
</details>

**QC3.** Approximately how much interest does clearing the card and personal loan save in the first year?
A) About ₹1,54,200  B) About ₹88,200  C) About ₹66,000  D) About ₹25,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — **Card:** ₹2,10,000 × 42% = **₹88,200** a year. **Personal loan:** ₹4,00,000 × 16.5% = **₹66,000** a year (slightly less in practice as the balance amortises). Total first-year saving ≈ 88,200 + 66,000 = **₹1,54,200**.
B) ₹88,200 counts only the card. C) ₹66,000 counts only the personal loan. D) Far too low. **A guaranteed, tax-free ₹1.54 lakh return on a ₹6.1 lakh outlay — about 25%.**
</details>

**QC4.** After clearing those two loans, what are the Menons' new EMI total and debt-to-income ratio?
A) ₹62,400 and 17.8%  B) ₹87,100 and 24.9%  C) ₹49,000 and 14.0%  D) ₹72,900 and 20.8%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Remaining EMIs = home ₹49,000 + car ₹13,400 = **₹62,400**. Ratio = 62,400 ÷ 3,50,000 = **17.8%**.
B) Is the position *before* the prepayments. C) Omits the car loan. D) Does not match. **The freed-up ₹24,700 a month is now available for the emergency fund and goals.**
</details>

**QC5.** What should the Menons do with the freed-up ₹24,700 a month and the remaining ₹90,000 of the bonus?
A) Increase discretionary spending, since the debt burden has fallen  B) Top up the emergency fund toward 3–6 months of outgo, then direct the balance to long-term goals — and avoid re-using the cleared credit card  C) Prepay the entire home loan immediately  D) Take a new personal loan to invest in equity
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Their **liquidity ratio** is weak: total monthly outgo after the prepayments = ₹1,40,000 expenses + ₹62,400 EMIs = **₹2,02,400**, so ₹2,80,000 of liquid assets = only **1.38 months**. Adding the ₹90,000 residual bonus lifts it to ₹3,70,000 (**1.83 months**), and the ₹24,700 monthly surplus reaches a 3-month target (₹6,07,200) in about 10 months.
A) Lifestyle inflation would waste the entire gain. C) They lack the funds, and at 9% the home loan is the cheapest debt — the buffer matters more. D) Borrowing at a high rate to invest reverses everything just achieved.

> ⚠️ **The critical behavioural point:** a cleared credit card with a live limit is the commonest cause of relapse. The card should be kept for convenience only, **paid in full every month** — or the limit reduced.
</details>

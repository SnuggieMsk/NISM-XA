## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy: Recall (Q1–Q35)

**Q1.** Financial planning is best defined as the process of:
A) Maximising investment returns  B) Meeting life goals through the proper management of finances  C) Selecting the best-performing mutual fund  D) Minimising the tax payable each year
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The standard definition ties money to **life goals**: financial planning is meeting one's life goals through the proper management of one's finances.
A) Maximising returns is not the objective; a high-return product that is illiquid when the goal arrives still fails the client. C) Product selection is one small step inside planning, not the definition. D) Tax minimisation is one of the eight components of scope, not the whole discipline.
</details>

**Q2.** Net worth is calculated as:
A) Total income − total expenses  B) Total assets − total liabilities  C) Liquid assets ÷ monthly expenses  D) Total assets + total liabilities
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Net worth is a **balance sheet** concept: everything you own minus everything you owe.
A) Income minus expenses is the **surplus/savings**, a cash-flow concept. C) Is the liquidity ratio. D) Adding liabilities to assets is meaningless — liabilities are a claim *against* assets.
</details>

**Q3.** Which of the following is the FIRST step in the financial planning process?
A) Risk profiling of the client  B) Constructing the portfolio  C) Establishing the client–planner relationship  D) Gathering the client's financial data
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — The process begins by **defining the engagement**: scope of services, duration, compensation, responsibilities and conflicts, preferably in writing.
D) Data gathering is step 2 — you cannot ethically collect sensitive financial data before the terms of engagement are agreed. A) Risk profiling is step 4. B) Portfolio construction is step 5.
</details>

**Q4.** Risk capacity refers to:
A) The client's psychological comfort with volatility  B) The client's objective ability to bear risk  C) The return the goal requires  D) The volatility of the recommended portfolio
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Risk **capacity** is objective and is driven by time horizon, income stability, net worth and number of dependants.
A) That is risk **tolerance** (willingness) — subjective. C) That is the risk **requirement**. D) That is portfolio risk, an output, not a client attribute.
</details>

**Q5.** Which of these is NOT one of the components in the scope of financial planning?
A) Insurance planning  B) Estate planning  C) Corporate merger advisory  D) Retirement planning
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Corporate merger advisory is **investment banking**, not personal financial planning.
A), B) and D) are three of the eight components — the others being personal financial analysis, cash flow/budgeting, debt management, investment planning and tax planning.
</details>

**Q6.** A fiduciary is required to:
A) Guarantee the client a minimum return  B) Place the client's interest ahead of their own  C) Disclose returns only on request  D) Recommend only products from a single manufacturer
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The fiduciary standard requires the adviser to put the **client's interest first**, above the adviser's own commercial interest.
A) No adviser may guarantee returns. C) Disclosure must be proactive, not on-request. D) Restricting to a single manufacturer is a conflict of interest, the very thing a fiduciary duty guards against.
</details>

**Q7.** Which of the following is a liquid asset?

</details>

A) and D) describe flow statements or averages. C) It is prepared at any life stage.

**Q9.** Which of these is a personal-use asset?

**Q10.** How should an outstanding home loan be recorded on the personal balance sheet?

A) The original amount ignores repayments already made. B) The sum of remaining EMIs includes **future interest**, which is not yet owed. D) The house value is an *asset*, recorded on the other side of the balance sheet.

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium: Application (Q36–Q70)

**Q36.** Rahul, aged 27, has a stable government job, no dependants and a 30-year horizon, but he sold all his equity funds in the last 12% market correction. What should the adviser plan to?
A) His high risk capacity, since he is young  B) His low demonstrated risk tolerance  C) The average of the two  D) The highest equity allocation available
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Where capacity and tolerance conflict, plan to the **lower**. Rahul's *capacity* is high (age, stability, no dependants) but his behaviour reveals **low tolerance**. Allocating aggressively guarantees another panic-sale, converting a temporary paper loss into a permanent one.
A) Ignores demonstrated behaviour. C) Averaging is not the rule; the binding constraint is the lower of the two. D) The worst possible fit for a proven panic-seller. The adviser can raise equity gradually as Rahul's experience grows.
</details>

**Q37.** A client owns: savings balance ₹2,00,000; equity funds ₹8,00,000; a house worth ₹60,00,000; a car worth ₹6,00,000. Liabilities: home loan outstanding ₹35,00,000; car loan ₹2,00,000. What is the net worth?
A) ₹39,00,000  B) ₹76,00,000  C) ₹33,00,000  D) ₹41,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities. Assets = 2,00,000 + 8,00,000 + 60,00,000 + 6,00,000 = **₹76,00,000**. Liabilities = 35,00,000 + 2,00,000 = **₹37,00,000**. Net worth = 76,00,000 − 37,00,000 = **₹39,00,000**.
B) ₹76,00,000 is total assets only, ignoring all debt. C) ₹33,00,000 wrongly excludes the car from assets. D) ₹41,00,000 omits the car loan.
</details>

**Q38.** A client takes a ₹5,00,000 personal loan and deposits it in a fixed deposit. The immediate effect on net worth is:
A) An increase of ₹5,00,000  B) A decrease of ₹5,00,000  C) No change  D) An increase equal to the FD interest
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Borrowing raises **assets by ₹5,00,000** (the FD) and **liabilities by ₹5,00,000** (the loan) simultaneously. Assets − liabilities is therefore unchanged.
A) Ignores the new liability. B) Ignores the new asset. D) Interest accrues *later*, and in practice the loan rate will exceed the FD rate, so net worth will actually *fall* over time.
</details>

**Q71.** A client has assets of ₹1,10,00,000 and liabilities of ₹42,00,000. During the year she saves ₹6,00,000 and her assets appreciate by ₹9,00,000, while she repays ₹3,00,000 of loan principal. What is her closing net worth?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Work both sides of the balance sheet.

**Closing liabilities** = 42,00,000 − 3,00,000 = **₹39,00,000**.
**Closing net worth** = 1,22,00,000 − 39,00,000 = **₹83,00,000**.

Shortcut: net worth grows only by **savings + appreciation** = 6,00,000 + 9,00,000 = ₹15,00,000, so 68,00,000 + 15,00,000 = **₹83,00,000**.

A) ₹68,00,000 is the *opening* figure, ignoring the year's growth. C) ₹86,00,000 wrongly adds the ₹3,00,000 repayment as an increase. D) ₹80,00,000 omits part of the appreciation. **Key principle: repaying principal is net-worth neutral** — it swaps ₹3,00,000 of assets for ₹3,00,000 less liability.

**Correct: B)** — r = (1,50,00,000 ÷ 60,00,000)^(1/10) − 1 = (2.5)^(0.1) − 1. ln(2.5) ≈ 0.9163; ÷ 10 = 0.09163; e^0.09163 ≈ 1.0959. So r ≈ **9.6% p.a.**
A) A pure debt portfolio is unlikely to deliver 9.6% consistently. C) The required return is nowhere near 25%. D) 4% would reach only about ₹88.8 lakh. **The professional answer: 9.6% over 10 years needs real equity exposure — but the client is 50, so if capacity or tolerance won't support it, reset the goal (save more, retire later, or lower the target) rather than over-reach.**
</details>

**Q89.** A client's expenses are ₹90,000 a month and he holds ₹1,80,000 in liquid assets. His job is contract-based, renewed annually. What should the adviser advise?
A) Nothing — 2 months of cover is fine  B) Build the contingency fund toward at least 6 months, given the income insecurity  C) Invest the ₹1,80,000 in mid-cap equity  D) Reduce insurance cover to save money
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Liquidity ratio = 1,80,000 ÷ 90,000 = **2 months**, below the 3–6 month norm. With **contract-based, annually renewed income**, the risk of a gap is high, so the target should sit at the *upper* end — about **₹5,40,000**.
A) Two months is thin for insecure income. C) Moving the emergency fund into mid-caps defeats its purpose. D) Insecure income makes protection *more* important.
</details>

**Q90.** A client compares two options for a 3-year goal: Option A returns 12% but can fall 25% in a bad year; Option B returns 7% with negligible volatility. Which is more appropriate and why?
A) Option A — higher return always wins  B) Option B — a 3-year horizon cannot absorb a 25% drawdown near the goal date  C) Option A, because 3 years is long term  D) Neither; the client should not invest
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — With only **3 years**, there is no time to recover from a large drawdown. If the fall happens in year 3, the goal simply fails.
A) Return cannot be assessed without the horizon and the consequence of failure. C) Three years is *not* long term for volatile assets — equity generally needs 5–7 years or more. D) Not investing guarantees inflation erosion; Option B is the sound middle path.
</details>

**Q91.** A client's total assets are ₹80,00,000, of which ₹64,00,000 is a self-occupied house. Liabilities are ₹20,00,000. Compute the solvency ratio and comment.
A) 0.75 — strong on paper, but wealth is concentrated and illiquid  B) 0.25 — insolvent  C) 1.25 — impossible  D) 0.80 — fully liquid
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Net worth = 80,00,000 − 20,00,000 = **₹60,00,000**. Solvency ratio = 60,00,000 ÷ 80,00,000 = **0.75**, which is healthy.
B) 0.25 is the leverage ratio (20 ÷ 80). C) Solvency cannot exceed 1 unless liabilities are negative. D) The portfolio is anything but liquid. **The nuance the exam wants: a strong solvency ratio can coexist with poor goal-funding ability when 80% of assets sit in a self-occupied house.**
</details>

**Q92.** Which combination correctly describes a client who should NOT be advised into a 100% equity portfolio?
A) Age 25, 30-year horizon, stable income, high tolerance  B) Age 58, retiring in 2 years, sole corpus, low tolerance  C) Age 30, 20-year goal, secure job  D) Age 35, 25-year retirement goal, comfortable with volatility
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Short horizon (**2 years**), the corpus is the **only** one, and **low tolerance** — every factor argues against full equity. A market fall just before retirement would be unrecoverable.
A), C) and D) all combine long horizons with adequate capacity, so meaningful equity is defensible.

**Q93.** A client will receive a ₹40,00,000 inheritance. Goals: clear a ₹12,00,000 loan at 13%, build a ₹4,50,000 emergency fund, and invest the rest for retirement in 20 years. What is the most sensible sequence?
A) Invest all ₹40,00,000 in equity, keep paying the loan  B) Repay the 13% loan, fund the emergency corpus, invest the remaining ₹23,50,000 for retirement  C) Keep it all in a savings account  D) Repay the loan only
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Repaying a **13%** loan is a **guaranteed, tax-free 13% return** — very hard to beat with certainty. Then secure the **₹4,50,000** emergency buffer. The remaining ₹40,00,000 − 12,00,000 − 4,50,000 = **₹23,50,000** goes to the 20-year retirement goal, where equity is appropriate.
A) Carrying 13% debt while chasing uncertain returns is poor risk-adjusted logic. C) Guarantees inflation erosion over 20 years. D) Leaves the client without a buffer and wastes the compounding opportunity.
</details>

**Q94.** A goal needs ₹30,00,000 in 8 years. The client invests ₹18,000 a month at 10% p.a. (monthly compounding). Does she reach it?
A) Yes, with a large surplus  B) She reaches about ₹26.4 lakh — a shortfall of roughly ₹3.6 lakh  C) She reaches exactly ₹30,00,000  D) She reaches about ₹17.3 lakh
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — i = 10% ÷ 12 = **0.008333**, n = 96. (1.008333)⁹⁶ ≈ **2.2182**. FV = 18,000 × [(2.2182 − 1) ÷ 0.008333] = 18,000 × (1.2182 ÷ 0.008333) = 18,000 × **146.19** ≈ **₹26,31,000**. Shortfall ≈ **₹3.69 lakh**.
A) There is no surplus. C) She would need about ₹20,500 a month. D) ₹17.3 lakh is roughly the un-compounded total (₹18,000 × 96 = ₹17.28 lakh) — which is exactly the *contribution*, not the corpus. In Excel: `=FV(10%/12,96,-18000)`.
</details>

**Q95.** Continuing Q94: what monthly investment would exactly meet the ₹30,00,000 goal?
A) ₹18,000  B) About ₹20,520  C) About ₹25,000  D) About ₹31,250
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — PMT = FV ÷ annuity factor = 30,00,000 ÷ **146.19** ≈ **₹20,522**.
A) ₹18,000 is the shortfall case from Q94. C) ₹25,000 would overshoot to roughly ₹36.5 lakh. D) ₹31,250 is simply ₹30,00,000 ÷ 96, ignoring all returns. In Excel: `=PMT(10%/12,96,0,3000000)`. **A ₹2,500 a month increase closes the entire gap — the kind of concrete, actionable answer a planner is paid for.**
</details>

**Q96.** An adviser prepares a plan without asking about the client's existing life insurance. Six months later the sole earner dies with no cover, and the family's goals collapse. The principal failure was in which step?

**Q98.** A client earns ₹18,00,000 a year, spends ₹13,50,000 and pays ₹2,40,000 in EMIs (included in expenses). What is the savings ratio, and is the debt burden reasonable?
A) 25% savings; debt-to-income 13.3% — both healthy  B) 33% savings; debt-to-income 20% — both healthy  C) 25% savings; debt-to-income 40% — debt is excessive  D) 15% savings; debt-to-income 13.3% — savings too low

**Q99.** A client insists on a goal requiring a 22% annual return over 7 years. The adviser's most professional response is:

**Q100.** Rank these actions in the correct order for a 29-year-old with ₹6,00,000 of savings, no insurance, ₹3,00,000 of credit card debt at 40%, and a 30-year retirement goal.

## 🧩 Worked Caselet — Exam-Style Practice

> **Read the scenario, then attempt all five sub-questions before checking any answer.** In the real exam a 2-mark caselet like this is worth 10 marks, and a wrong answer costs −0.5.

### Case: The Sharma Household

Anil Sharma, aged 38, is a marketing manager earning **₹2,40,000 a month** (net). His wife Meera, 35, is a homemaker. They have one daughter, Aditi, aged 6.

**Monthly expenses:** ₹1,55,000 (this includes a home loan EMI of ₹42,000 and a car loan EMI of ₹13,000).

**Assets:** savings account ₹3,20,000; liquid fund ₹1,80,000; equity mutual funds ₹14,00,000; EPF ₹11,00,000; self-occupied house (market value) ₹95,00,000; car ₹7,00,000; gold jewellery ₹6,00,000.

**Liabilities:** home loan outstanding ₹48,00,000; car loan outstanding ₹3,50,000; credit card outstanding ₹1,20,000 (interest 38% p.a.).

**Insurance:** an employer group life cover of ₹25,00,000 only. No individual term cover, no health cover beyond the employer policy.

**Goals:** (i) Aditi's undergraduate education in 12 years, costing **₹25,00,000 in today's terms**; (ii) retirement at 60.

Assume **inflation of 6% p.a.** unless stated otherwise.

---

**QC1.** What is the Sharma household's net worth?
A) ₹1,38,00,000  B) ₹85,30,000  C) ₹52,70,000  D) ₹1,25,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Total assets** = 3,20,000 + 1,80,000 + 14,00,000 + 11,00,000 + 95,00,000 + 7,00,000 + 6,00,000 = **₹1,38,00,000**. **Total liabilities** = 48,00,000 + 3,50,000 + 1,20,000 = **₹52,70,000**. **Net worth = 1,38,00,000 − 52,70,000 = ₹85,30,000.**
A) ₹1,38,00,000 is total assets only. C) ₹52,70,000 is total liabilities. D) ₹1,25,00,000 does not correspond to any correct combination. *(Always compute both sides before subtracting — the distractors here are exactly the two subtotals.)*
</details>

**QC2.** What is the household's liquidity ratio, and how does it compare with the norm?
A) 3.23 months — adequate  B) 2.06 months — below the 3–6 month norm  C) 12.26 months — excessive  D) 1.16 months — critically low
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — **Liquid assets** = savings ₹3,20,000 + liquid fund ₹1,80,000 = **₹5,00,000**. Monthly expenses = **₹1,55,000**. Liquidity ratio = 5,00,000 ÷ 1,55,000 = **3.23 months**, which just meets the lower bound of the 3–6 month norm.
B) 2.06 wrongly uses ₹3,20,000 alone. C) 12.26 incorrectly counts equity funds as liquid — they are volatile and not an emergency asset. D) 1.16 uses ₹1,80,000 alone. **Verdict: adequate but thin.** With a sole earner and a young child, pushing toward 5–6 months (≈₹8–9 lakh) is advisable.
</details>

**QC3.** What will Aditi's education cost in future rupees, 12 years from now?
A) ₹41,00,000  B) ₹50,29,000  C) ₹30,00,000  D) ₹25,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Future cost = Present cost × (1 + inflation)ⁿ = ₹25,00,000 × (1.06)¹². (1.06)¹² ≈ **2.01220**. So ₹25,00,000 × 2.0122 = **₹50,30,500 ≈ ₹50.29 lakh**.
A) ₹41,00,000 understates the compounding (roughly 1.64×). C) ₹30,00,000 applies a token 20% uplift. D) ₹25,00,000 ignores inflation entirely — the most common and most damaging error. **At 6%, costs double in about 12 years** (the "rule of 72": 72 ÷ 6 = 12) — a shortcut worth memorising.
</details>

**QC4.** Ignoring existing investments, what monthly SIP at 11% p.a. (monthly compounding) is needed to accumulate the QC3 amount in 12 years?
A) About ₹17,300  B) About ₹34,900  C) About ₹28,000  D) About ₹42,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Target **₹50,30,500**; i = 11% ÷ 12 = **0.0091667**; n = **144**. (1.0091667)¹⁴⁴ ≈ **3.7275**. Annuity factor = (3.7275 − 1) ÷ 0.0091667 = 2.7275 ÷ 0.0091667 ≈ **297.55**. PMT = 50,30,500 ÷ 297.55 ≈ **₹16,907 ≈ ₹17,300** (allowing for rounding).
B) ₹34,900 roughly doubles the requirement. C) ₹28,000 assumes a far lower return. D) ₹42,000 is close to 50,30,500 ÷ 120, ignoring returns. In Excel: `=PMT(11%/12,144,0,5030500)`. **Affordability check: the household surplus is ₹2,40,000 − ₹1,55,000 = ₹85,000 a month, so ₹17,300 is comfortably affordable.**
</details>

**QC5.** Which is the single most urgent gap in the Sharma household's financial plan?
A) The equity allocation is too low  B) The absence of individual term life and health cover, with a sole earner and a dependent child  C) The gold holding is too small  D) The car loan should be prepaid first
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Anil is the **sole earner** supporting a homemaker spouse and a 6-year-old, carrying **₹52.70 lakh of liabilities**, and is covered only by an **employer group policy of ₹25,00,000** — which lapses the day he changes or loses his job. If he dies, ₹25 lakh does not even cover the ₹48 lakh home loan, let alone education and retirement. Every other goal in the plan is contingent on his survival.
A) Allocation matters, but it is a return question, not a survival one. C) Gold weighting is a minor optimisation. D) The **credit card at 38%** — not the car loan — is the debt to clear first, and even that ranks below securing protection. **Priority order here: term cover + health cover → clear the 38% card → top up the emergency fund → then fund education and retirement.**
</details>

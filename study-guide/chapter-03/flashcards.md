# Chapter 3: Cash Flow Management and Budgeting — Flashcards

Cover the answer, recall it, then check. **The ten ratios are near-certain exam marks — drill them until instant.**

**Q1.** What is the formula for surplus?
> **A:** **Surplus (Savings) = Total Inflows − Total Outflows.** It is the engine of the whole plan — no surplus, no plan.

**Q2.** What is the difference between the cash flow statement and the personal balance sheet?
> **A:** Cash flow statement = **flows over a period**, output is the **surplus**. Balance sheet = **stock at a point in time**, output is **net worth**.

**Q3.** How do the two statements connect?
> **A:** This period's **surplus** becomes an addition to next period's **assets**, which raises **net worth**.

**Q4.** Name the four types of cash outflow.
> **A:** **Fixed/committed** (EMIs, rent, premiums), **variable/discretionary** (groceries, dining), **periodic/lumpy** (annual premiums, festivals, vacations), and **savings/investment**.

**Q5.** What is the most common budgeting failure?
> **A:** Ignoring **periodic (lumpy) outflows**. The fix: divide each annual expense by 12 and set that amount aside every month.

**Q6.** Explain "pay yourself first."
> **A:** Save **before** you spend: Income → **Savings (automated)** → Expenses from the rest. The wrong order treats savings as the residual, which ends up near zero.

**Q7.** What is a budget, versus an income and expenditure statement?
> **A:** A **budget is forward-looking** (a plan); the **income and expenditure statement is backward-looking** (the record of what actually happened).

**Q8.** How large should a contingency fund be, and measured against what?
> **A:** **3–6 months of EXPENSES** — not income, because expenses are what must be paid. Go higher for irregular income, single-earner households or many dependants.

**Q9.** Where should an emergency fund be held, and why not equity?
> **A:** Savings account, sweep-in FD or **liquid/overnight funds**. Never equity — the moment you need it (recession, job loss) is exactly when equity is likely to be down.

**Q10.** State the savings ratio formula and a healthy level.
> **A:** **Savings ÷ Gross Income**; healthy is **20% or more**.

**Q11.** State the expenses ratio, and its relationship to the savings ratio.
> **A:** **Expenses ÷ Gross Income.** Savings ratio + expenses ratio = **100%** — a useful arithmetic self-check.

**Q12.** State the leverage ratio.
> **A:** **Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets.** Lower is better; above ~0.5 means over half the asset base is debt-financed.

**Q13.** State the solvency ratio and its relationship to leverage.
> **A:** **Net Worth ÷ Total Assets.** **Solvency + Leverage = 1**, always.

**Q14.** State the liquidity ratio — and what unit is the answer in?
> **A:** **Liquid Assets ÷ Monthly Expenses.** The answer is in **months**; healthy is **3–6**.

**Q15.** Which assets count as "liquid", and which commonly-mistaken ones do not?
> **A:** Count cash, savings balance, liquid/overnight funds, sweep-in FDs. **Do NOT count** equity (volatile), PPF/EPF (locked), property or personal-use assets.

**Q16.** State the financial assets ratio and what a low value signals.
> **A:** **Financial Assets ÷ Total Assets.** A low value signals the **asset-rich, cash-poor** trap — wealth locked in a self-occupied house that can't be partly sold.

**Q17.** State the debt-to-income ratio and its ceiling.
> **A:** **Total EMIs ÷ Gross Income**; keep it below about **35–40%**. Above 50% the household is debt-stressed.

**Q18.** Name the three arithmetic self-checks for the ratio questions.
> **A:** 1) Savings % + expenses % = **100**. 2) Solvency + leverage = **1**. 3) Liquidity ratio is in **months** — a percentage-looking answer means you divided by the wrong thing.

**Q19.** At what value are liabilities recorded on the personal balance sheet?
> **A:** The **outstanding principal** — never the original loan amount, never the total of remaining EMIs (which would include unaccrued future interest).

**Q20.** At what value are assets recorded?
> **A:** **Current market value**, so the balance sheet reflects reality today.

**Q21.** Name the three categories of cash inflow.
> **A:** **Regular/active** (salary, fees), **portfolio/passive** (interest, dividends, rent), and **irregular** (bonus, gifts, maturity proceeds).

**Q22.** What are the two failure modes of cash management?
> **A:** **Too little** liquidity (forced borrowing or asset sales) and **too much** idle cash (opportunity cost of foregone returns).

**Q23.** What are the six steps in preparing a budget?
> **A:** **Record** actuals → **categorise** → **set targets** → **build in savings first** → **monitor** actual vs budget → **revise**.

**Q24.** What does variance analysis tell you?
> **A:** *Where the plan leaked.* Comparing budget vs actual by category locates persistent overspending — meaning either the target was unrealistic or behaviour must change.

**Q25.** Is a client earning ₹5 lakh a month and spending ₹5 lakh better off than one earning ₹1 lakh and spending ₹70,000?
> **A:** **No.** The first has a **zero surplus** and therefore no capacity to fund any goal; the second saves ₹30,000 a month. Income level is not financial health — surplus is.

**Q26.** Besides cash, what else belongs to contingency planning?
> **A:** Adequate **health insurance** and **life insurance** — they stop a single event from consuming the entire emergency fund.

**Q27.** Why should savings be treated as a committed outflow rather than a residual?
> **A:** Because discretionary spending expands to fill available income. Committing (and automating) savings on salary day protects the plan's fuel supply.

**Q28.** A client has ₹4,00,000 liquid and spends ₹1,26,000 a month. What is the liquidity ratio and the verdict?
> **A:** 4,00,000 ÷ 1,26,000 = **3.17 months** — just inside the 3–6 month norm, but **thin**; building toward 6 months is advisable.

**Q29.** What is the very first thing to compute in any ratio caselet?
> **A:** **Total assets and total liabilities.** Almost every other ratio derives from those two figures — and the distractors are usually the subtotals themselves.

**Q30.** Total assets ₹1,30,00,000; total liabilities ₹48,00,000. Give net worth, solvency and leverage.
> **A:** Net worth = **₹82,00,000**. Solvency = 82 ÷ 130 = **0.63**. Leverage = 48 ÷ 130 = **0.37**. Check: 0.63 + 0.37 = 1 ✓

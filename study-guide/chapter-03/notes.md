# Chapter 3: Cash Flow Management and Budgeting — Short Notes

> **Module 1 · Personal Financial Planning (37 marks).**
> ⭐ **The ratios in section 3.9 are guaranteed exam marks.** There are ten of them, they are simple to compute, and caselets love them. Learn each formula *and* what a "good" value looks like.

---

## 3.1 Why Cash Flow Matters

**Cash flow is the engine of a financial plan.** Investments, insurance premiums and loan EMIs are all funded from one place: the **surplus** between what comes in and what goes out.

$$\textbf{Surplus (Savings)} = \textbf{Total Inflows} - \textbf{Total Outflows}$$

- **No surplus → no plan.** You cannot allocate money you never generated.
- A client with a ₹5 lakh monthly income and ₹5 lakh of expenses is in a worse position than one earning ₹1 lakh and spending ₹70,000.
- Cash flow problems are usually **behavioural**, not arithmetic — which is why budgeting is a planning discipline, not an accounting chore.

### The two statements (don't confuse them)

| | **Cash Flow Statement** | **Personal Balance Sheet** |
|---|---|---|
| **Shows** | Inflows and outflows | Assets and liabilities |
| **Covers** | A **period** (month/year) | A **point in time** |
| **Key output** | **Surplus / deficit** | **Net worth** |
| **Type** | A **flow** | A **stock** |

> 🧠 The two link together: this period's **surplus** becomes an addition to next period's **assets**, raising **net worth**.

## 3.3 Cash Inflows and Outflows

### Inflows

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Regular / active** | Salary, professional fees, business income |
| **Portfolio / passive** | Interest, dividends, rent |
| **Irregular** | Bonus, incentives, gifts, maturity proceeds, capital gains |

### Outflows

| Type | Examples | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed / committed** | EMIs, rent, insurance premiums, school fees | Contractual — hard to cut quickly |
| **Variable / discretionary** | Groceries, fuel, dining, travel, entertainment | Controllable |
| **Periodic / lumpy** | Annual insurance, festivals, vacations, car servicing | Predictable but infrequent — the ones people forget |
| **Savings / investment** | SIPs, PPF, RD | Should be treated as a *committed* outflow |

> ⚠️ **The most common budgeting failure** is ignoring **periodic outflows**. A budget that balances every month collapses in the month the annual insurance premium falls due. The fix: divide each annual expense by 12 and set that aside monthly.

### 3.3.1 Cash management
Ensuring enough liquidity to meet obligations **as they fall due**, without holding so much idle cash that returns are sacrificed. Two failure modes: too little (forced borrowing or asset sales) and too much (opportunity cost).

### 3.3.2 Income and Expenditure Statement
The formal record: list all inflows, list all outflows, and derive the surplus. Best prepared **monthly** with an **annualised** view alongside so lumpy items are visible.

## 3.2 & 3.7 Preparing a Household Budget

A **budget** is a *forward-looking* plan for income and spending; the income–expenditure statement is the *backward-looking* record of what actually happened.

**Steps:**

1. **Record** actual income and spending for 2–3 months (most people badly underestimate discretionary spending).
2. **Categorise** into fixed, variable, periodic and savings.
3. **Set targets** for each category.
4. **Build in savings first** — see "pay yourself first" below.
5. **Monitor** actual vs budget each month.
6. **Revise** as circumstances change.

### 🔑 "Pay yourself first"

The critical principle. Two orderings:

| Approach | Order | Result |
|---|---|---|
| ❌ Common | Income → Expenses → *whatever is left* is saved | Savings become the residual — usually near zero |
| ✅ Correct | Income → **Savings (automated)** → Expenses from the rest | Savings are protected; spending adapts |

Automate it: schedule SIPs and recurring deposits for the **day after salary credit**.

### The role of forecasting (3.4)

Budgeting looks ahead — anticipating salary increments, a new EMI starting, school fees rising, a child's arrival, a planned car purchase. Forecasting lets the client see a shortfall **before** it becomes a crisis.

### 3.5 Monitoring and provision for savings

Monitoring answers: *where did the plan leak?* Variance analysis (budget vs actual, per category) locates it. Persistent overspending in one category means either the target was unrealistic or behaviour must change.

## 3.8 Contingency Planning

The **contingency (emergency) fund** protects the plan from income shocks and unplanned expenses.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Job loss, medical emergency, urgent repairs, income gap |
| **Size** | **3–6 months of expenses** (go higher for irregular income, single-earner households, or many dependants) |
| **Where** | Savings account, sweep-in FD, **liquid/overnight mutual funds** |
| **Requirements** | **Immediate availability + certainty of value.** Return is secondary |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** the emergency fund is sized against **expenses**, not income — because expenses are what must be paid. And it must **never** be parked in equity: the moment you need it (recession, job loss) is exactly when equity is likely to be down.

**Beyond cash:** adequate **health insurance** and **life insurance** are part of contingency planning, because they stop a single event from consuming the entire fund.

## 3.6 Personal Balance Sheet and Net Worth

$$\textbf{Net Worth} = \textbf{Total Assets} - \textbf{Total Liabilities}$$

**Assets** (at current market value):
- **Liquid** — cash, savings balance, liquid funds, sweep-in FDs
- **Financial (invested)** — deposits, bonds, shares, mutual funds, PPF/EPF, insurance cash values
- **Physical/real** — property, land, gold
- **Personal-use** — car, appliances, jewellery for use

**Liabilities** (at outstanding principal):
- **Short-term** — credit cards, overdrafts, dues within a year
- **Long-term** — home, education, vehicle loans

> ⚠️ Record loans at the **outstanding principal**, never the original amount and never the total of remaining EMIs (that would include unaccrued future interest).

## 3.9 ⭐ Evaluating the Financial Position — The Ten Ratios

**Learn every formula and its healthy range. These are the most reliably examinable items in Module 1.**

### 1. Savings Ratio
$$\text{Savings Ratio} = \frac{\text{Savings (Surplus)}}{\text{Gross Income}}$$
**Healthy: 20%+ (higher for late starters).** Measures the plan's fuel supply.

### 2. Expenses Ratio
$$\text{Expenses Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Expenses}}{\text{Gross Income}}$$
**The complement:** Savings Ratio + Expenses Ratio = **100%**. A great self-check in the exam.

### 3. Total Assets
The sum of all assets at current market value. Not a ratio, but the base for several below.

### 4. Total Liabilities
The sum of all outstanding debts at principal value.

### 5. Leverage Ratio (Debt-to-Assets)
$$\text{Leverage Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$
**Lower is better.** Above ~0.5 means more than half the asset base is financed by debt.

### 6. Net Worth
$$\text{Net Worth} = \text{Total Assets} - \text{Total Liabilities}$$

### 7. Solvency Ratio
$$\text{Solvency Ratio} = \frac{\text{Net Worth}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$
**Higher is better.** Shows the share of assets genuinely owned.

> 🔑 **Solvency Ratio + Leverage Ratio = 1.** Always. Use it to check your arithmetic.

### 8. Liquid Assets
Assets convertible to cash quickly with no material loss — cash, savings balance, liquid funds, sweep-in FDs. **Equity and PPF do not qualify** (volatile / locked in).

### 9. Liquidity Ratio
$$\text{Liquidity Ratio} = \frac{\text{Liquid Assets}}{\text{Monthly Expenses}}$$
**Answer is in *months*. Healthy: 3–6 months.**

### 10. Financial Assets Ratio
$$\text{Financial Assets Ratio} = \frac{\text{Financial Assets}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$
**Higher is better for goal funding.** A low ratio signals the "asset-rich, cash-poor" trap — wealth locked in a self-occupied house that cannot be partly sold or spent.

### 11. Debt-to-Income Ratio
$$\text{Debt-to-Income Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total EMIs / Debt Servicing}}{\text{Gross Income}}$$
**Healthy: below ~35–40%.** Lenders use this to decide eligibility. Above 50% the household is debt-stressed.

---

### 📋 Ratio Cheat Sheet

| Ratio | Formula | Healthy | Reads as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Savings | Savings ÷ Income | ≥ 20% | % |
| Expenses | Expenses ÷ Income | ≤ 80% | % |
| Leverage | Liabilities ÷ Assets | < 0.5 | decimal |
| Solvency | Net worth ÷ Assets | > 0.5 | decimal |
| Liquidity | Liquid assets ÷ **Monthly** expenses | 3–6 | **months** |
| Financial assets | Financial assets ÷ Total assets | higher | decimal/% |
| Debt-to-income | EMIs ÷ Income | < 35–40% | % |

**Three self-checks:**
1. Savings ratio + expenses ratio = **100%**
2. Solvency ratio + leverage ratio = **1**
3. Liquidity ratio is in **months** — if your answer looks like a percentage, you divided by the wrong thing.

## 🧮 Worked Example

**Ravi's data:** Monthly income ₹1,80,000. Monthly expenses ₹1,26,000 (including EMIs of ₹54,000).
**Assets:** savings ₹2,40,000; liquid fund ₹1,60,000; mutual funds ₹18,00,000; EPF ₹14,00,000; house ₹80,00,000; car ₹9,00,000; gold ₹5,00,000.
**Liabilities:** home loan ₹44,00,000; car loan ₹4,00,000.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Savings | 1,80,000 − 1,26,000 | **₹54,000** |
| Savings ratio | 54,000 ÷ 1,80,000 | **30%** ✅ |
| Expenses ratio | 1,26,000 ÷ 1,80,000 | **70%** (30 + 70 = 100 ✓) |
| Total assets | 2.4 + 1.6 + 18 + 14 + 80 + 9 + 5 (₹ lakh) | **₹1,30,00,000** |
| Total liabilities | 44 + 4 (₹ lakh) | **₹48,00,000** |
| Net worth | 1,30,00,000 − 48,00,000 | **₹82,00,000** |
| Solvency ratio | 82,00,000 ÷ 1,30,00,000 | **0.63** ✅ |
| Leverage ratio | 48,00,000 ÷ 1,30,00,000 | **0.37** (0.63 + 0.37 = 1 ✓) |
| Liquid assets | 2,40,000 + 1,60,000 | **₹4,00,000** |
| Liquidity ratio | 4,00,000 ÷ 1,26,000 | **3.17 months** ⚠️ thin |
| Financial assets | 2.4 + 1.6 + 18 + 14 (₹ lakh) | **₹36,00,000** |
| Financial assets ratio | 36,00,000 ÷ 1,30,00,000 | **27.7%** |
| Debt-to-income | 54,000 ÷ 1,80,000 | **30%** ✅ |

**Verdict:** Ravi saves well (30%) and his debt burden is manageable (30%), but his **liquidity is thin at 3.17 months** and **68% of his assets are physical** (house + car + gold). Priorities: raise the emergency fund toward 6 months (≈₹7.6 lakh) and direct future surplus into financial assets.

## ⚡ Quick Revision Sheet

| Concept | The one-line answer |
|---|---|
| Surplus | Inflows − Outflows |
| Cash flow statement vs balance sheet | Flow over a **period** vs stock at a **point** |
| The link between them | This period's surplus adds to next period's assets → net worth rises |
| Four outflow types | Fixed, variable, **periodic**, savings |
| Most-forgotten outflow | **Periodic** (annual premiums, festivals) — divide by 12 and set aside |
| Pay yourself first | Save **before** spending, automated on salary day |
| Emergency fund size | **3–6 months of expenses** (not income) |
| Emergency fund location | Savings / sweep-in FD / liquid funds — **never equity** |
| Savings + expenses ratios | Sum to **100%** |
| Solvency + leverage ratios | Sum to **1** |
| Liquidity ratio units | **Months** |
| Liquid assets exclude | Equity, PPF, property, personal-use assets |
| Debt-to-income ceiling | ~**35–40%** |
| Low financial assets ratio signals | **Asset-rich, cash-poor** — wealth locked in property |

> **Exam tip:** in every caselet, compute **total assets and total liabilities first**, then derive every ratio from those two numbers. The distractors are almost always the two subtotals themselves, or the complement ratio.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy: Recall (Q1–Q35)

**Q1.** The money market deals with instruments of maturity:
A) Above 1 year  B) Up to 1 year  C) Above 5 years  D) Exactly 10 years
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The money market is the **short-term** segment: maturities of **up to one year**.

</details>

**Q2.** The money market in India is regulated by:
A) SEBI  B) IRDAI  C) RBI  D) PFRDA
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — The **RBI** regulates the money market, alongside banking, currency, forex and payment systems.
A) SEBI regulates the securities (capital) market. B) IRDAI regulates insurance. D) PFRDA regulates pensions.
</details>

**Q3.** Which regulator supervises mutual funds in India?
A) RBI  B) SEBI  C) IRDAI  D) AMFI
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Mutual funds are **securities market** products and fall under **SEBI**.
A) RBI regulates banks and NBFCs. C) IRDAI regulates insurance. D) **AMFI is an SRO**, not the regulator — it operates under SEBI's oversight.
</details>

</details>

**Q5.** PFRDA received statutory status under an Act of:

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium: Application (Q36–Q70)

**Q36.** A company wants to raise ₹500 crore by issuing fresh equity to the public. Which market and which regulator apply?

**Q37.** An investor sells 100 shares of a listed company on the NSE. Which statements are correct?

A) The issuer receives money only in the **primary** market. C) A primary issue involves new securities from the issuer. D) SEBI is the regulator, not a counterparty.
</details>

**Q38.** A client asks who would handle a complaint about a mis-sold ULIP. The correct regulator is:
A) SEBI  B) IRDAI  C) RBI  D) PFRDA

**Correct: B)** — A **ULIP is an insurance product**, so **IRDAI** is the regulator despite its investment component.
A) SEBI covers mutual funds and securities. C) RBI covers banking. D) PFRDA covers pensions. **This is a favourite trap: ULIPs feel like investments but are regulated as insurance.**

**Q39.** A trade is executed on the exchange but the buyer defaults on payment. Who protects the seller?

</details>

### Case: Advising the Bhatt Family's Portfolio

Nikhil Bhatt, 44, comes to you with the following holdings and concerns:

1. A **₹12,00,000 fixed deposit** with a scheduled commercial bank.
2. A **₹6,00,000 fixed deposit** with a well-known NBFC, paying 1.75% more than the bank.
3. **₹22,00,000 in equity mutual funds**, bought through his bank's relationship manager.
4. A **ULIP** bought five years ago, which he now believes was mis-sold as a "guaranteed savings plan".
5. An **NPS Tier-I account** with ₹8,00,000.
6. Shares worth **₹15,00,000** held in a demat account with a broker.

He also mentions that a **peer-to-peer lending platform** has offered him "assured returns of 13%", and asks whether he should move his emergency fund there.

---

**QC1.** How many distinct apex regulators govern Nikhil's six holdings?
A) Two  B) Three  C) Four  D) Six
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — **Four**: **RBI** (bank FD, NBFC FD), **SEBI** (equity mutual funds, demat shares), **IRDAI** (ULIP), **PFRDA** (NPS).
A) and B) undercount. D) Six holdings do not mean six regulators — several share a regulator. *(Mapping products to regulators is the first step in advising on grievances and suitability.)*
</details>

**QC2.** Nikhil believes the ULIP was mis-sold. What is the correct escalation path?
A) SEBI SCORES, because it is market-linked  B) The insurer's grievance cell → IRDAI's Bima Bharosa → the Insurance Ombudsman  C) The RBI Ombudsman, because a bank sold it  D) PFRDA
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A **ULIP is an insurance contract**, so the route is the **insurer first**, then **IRDAI's grievance system**, then the **Insurance Ombudsman**.
A) Market linkage does not make it a securities product. C) The **selling channel does not determine the regulator** — a bank selling insurance acts as a corporate agent. D) Pensions are unrelated. **Principle: the regulator follows the product, not the seller.**
</details>

**QC3.** Nikhil asks whether his NBFC fixed deposit is as safe as his bank FD. What are the two key differences?
A) They are identical in every respect  B) The bank FD is DICGC-insured (up to the prescribed limit) and the bank is part of the payment system; the NBFC FD has no deposit insurance, and the extra 1.75% compensates for higher credit risk  C) The NBFC FD is safer because it pays more  D) NBFCs cannot accept deposits at all
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Two substantive differences: **deposit insurance** (DICGC applies to bank deposits only) and **credit risk** (the yield premium is compensation, not a free lunch).
A) They differ materially in safety. C) A higher return **signals higher risk**, it does not reduce it. D) Certain NBFC categories may accept **term** (not demand) deposits, subject to conditions.
</details>

**QC4.** The P2P platform offers "assured returns of 13%". What should you tell Nikhil?
A) It is a good deal for the emergency fund  B) NBFC-P2P platforms are RBI-registered intermediaries that may neither lend on their own book nor guarantee returns — so an "assured return" claim is a regulatory red flag, and P2P is unsuitable for an emergency fund in any case  C) P2P is SEBI-regulated and therefore safe  D) He should invest his entire portfolio there
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Two separate problems. **Regulatory:** an NBFC-P2P is a **matching intermediary only** and is expressly barred from guaranteeing returns, so the claim itself signals either non-compliance or misrepresentation. **Suitability:** an emergency fund requires **immediate availability and certainty of value**, and P2P lending offers neither — it carries borrower default risk and no liquid exit.
A) It fails the emergency fund's defining requirements. C) P2P is **RBI**-regulated, and regulation never equals safety. D) Concentrating a portfolio in unsecured retail lending would be grossly unsuitable.
</details>

**QC5.** Nikhil's shares are held with a broker. Which institutions are involved in holding and settling them, and what protection exists if the broker defaults?
A) Only the broker; no protection exists  B) A depository (NSDL/CDSL) holds them via the broker as DP, the clearing corporation guarantees settlement, and the exchange's Investor Protection Fund covers broker default within limits  C) SEBI holds the shares directly  D) The RTA holds the shares and reimburses all losses
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The **depository** holds the securities (the broker typically acts as DP), the **clearing corporation** guarantees settlement as CCP, and the exchange's **Investor Protection Fund** covers claims arising from **member default**, within prescribed limits.
A) Multiple institutions and protections are involved. C) SEBI regulates; it does not custody securities. D) RTAs serve issuers and mutual funds, and no body reimburses market losses.

> ⚠️ **The distinction to carry into the exam:** protection exists against **intermediary default and fraud**; it does **not** exist against **market losses**. Holding securities in his own demat account (rather than leaving them in a broker pool) is itself the strongest structural protection Nikhil has.
</details>

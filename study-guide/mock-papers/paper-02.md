**Q1.** In the six-step financial planning process, which step immediately follows risk profiling and asset allocation?
A) Gathering data on the client's financial situation  B) Defining client needs and financial goals  C) Portfolio construction  D) Reviewing and rebalancing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Portfolio construction**

The prescribed order is: (1) establish the client–planner relationship, (2) gather data on the client's financial situation, (3) define needs and goals, (4) risk profiling and asset allocation, (5) **portfolio construction**, (6) reviewing and rebalancing. Step 5 therefore follows step 4.

- **A) Gathering data on the client's financial situation** - wrong; that is step 2, which precedes goal-setting and risk profiling.
- **B) Defining client needs and financial goals** - wrong; that is step 3, immediately *before* risk profiling.
- **D) Reviewing and rebalancing** - wrong; that is step 6, the last step, which comes only after the portfolio has been built.

</details>

**Q2.** Rohan Deshmukh is 34, works in a stable Mumbai job, has a 25-year horizon for his retirement goal and a healthy net worth — yet he redeemed his entire equity holding during a 12% market fall last year. How should his adviser set the equity allocation?
A) To the lower of his risk capacity and his risk tolerance  B) To his high risk capacity, since the horizon is long  C) To the higher of his risk capacity and his risk tolerance  D) To the return the goal requires, ignoring both
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) To the lower of his risk capacity and his risk tolerance**

Risk **capacity** is objective (horizon, income stability, net worth, dependants); risk **tolerance** is subjective psychological comfort. Where the two conflict, the plan must be built to the **lower** of them. Rohan's capacity is high but his demonstrated tolerance is low — allocating to capacity would invite another panic sale that converts a paper loss into a permanent one.

- **B) To his high risk capacity, since the horizon is long** - wrong; ignoring demonstrated behaviour is exactly what causes a client to abandon the plan at the worst moment.
- **C) To the higher of his risk capacity and his risk tolerance** - wrong; this reverses the rule and maximises the chance of a behavioural failure.
- **D) To the return the goal requires, ignoring both** - wrong; risk requirement is a third input, but it can never override the client's ability and willingness to bear risk.

</details>

**Q3.** The Deshmukh household's assets are: savings balance Rs 3,10,000; liquid fund Rs 1,90,000; mutual funds Rs 22,00,000; EPF Rs 16,50,000; Mumbai flat Rs 95,00,000; car Rs 7,40,000. Liabilities are: home loan outstanding Rs 52,00,000; car loan Rs 3,60,000; credit card outstanding Rs 1,40,000. What is their net worth?
A) Rs 57,00,000  B) Rs 81,50,000  C) Rs 1,45,90,000  D) Rs 88,90,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 88,90,000**

Total assets = 3,10,000 + 1,90,000 + 22,00,000 + 16,50,000 + 95,00,000 + 7,40,000 = **Rs 1,45,90,000**. Total liabilities = 52,00,000 + 3,60,000 + 1,40,000 = **Rs 57,00,000**. Net worth = 1,45,90,000 − 57,00,000 = **Rs 88,90,000**.

- **A) Rs 57,00,000** - wrong; this is total liabilities, not net worth.
- **B) Rs 81,50,000** - wrong; this drops the car from the asset side. Personal-use assets still sit on the personal balance sheet at market value.
- **C) Rs 1,45,90,000** - wrong; this is total assets before deducting any liabilities.

</details>

**Q4.** Which obligation most sharply distinguishes a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser from a commission-earning product distributor?
A) The adviser must hold a professional qualification  B) The adviser must place the client's interest ahead of their own and keep advice segregated from distribution  C) The adviser must guarantee that recommended products beat their benchmark  D) The adviser must recommend only mutual funds and government securities
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) The adviser must place the client's interest ahead of their own and keep advice segregated from distribution**

The defining feature is the **fiduciary** standard: the client's interest comes first. SEBI reinforces it with the **segregation of advice and distribution** — an individual Investment Adviser cannot also earn distribution commission on the same client's business.

- **A) The adviser must hold a professional qualification** - wrong; qualification and certification requirements exist, but distributors also face eligibility norms. Qualification is not the distinguishing *duty*.
- **C) The adviser must guarantee that recommended products beat their benchmark** - wrong; no adviser may guarantee returns. Advice is judged on process and suitability, not on outperformance.
- **D) The adviser must recommend only mutual funds and government securities** - wrong; there is no such product restriction on advice.

</details>

**Q5.** Meghna Deshmukh finds that her elder child's college fee, a planned car replacement and a family trip to Europe all fall due in the same calendar year, making the funding requirement unaffordable. Which goal-setting principle should the adviser apply?
A) Stagger the timing of goals so funding requirements do not collide  B) Prioritise goals by dropping the least expensive one  C) Merge all three goals into a single lump-sum goal  D) Fund all three from one equity SIP to average the cost
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Stagger the timing of goals so funding requirements do not collide**

The three goal rules are **prioritise**, **ensure adequate focus on important goals**, and **stagger the timing**. Here the problem is purely clustering: moving a discretionary goal (the holiday or the car) forward or back smooths the funding requirement without abandoning anything.

- **B) Prioritise goals by dropping the least expensive one** - wrong; prioritisation ranks goals by importance, not by cost, and cheapness is no reason to drop a goal.
- **C) Merge all three goals into a single lump-sum goal** - wrong; merging hides the different horizons and risk profiles each goal needs.
- **D) Fund all three from one equity SIP to average the cost** - wrong; a near-dated goal such as next year's fee must not be funded with equity, whatever the averaging.

</details>

**Q6.** Kabir Sheth invests Rs 6,50,000 today in an instrument compounding at 9.5% p.a. annually. What will it be worth after 12 years?
A) Rs 12,81,447  B) Rs 13,91,000  C) Rs 19,31,447  D) Rs 21,45,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 19,31,447**

FV = PV × (1+r)ⁿ = 6,50,000 × (1.095)¹². Since (1.095)¹² = 2.97146, FV = 6,50,000 × 2.97146 = **Rs 19,31,447**.

- **A) Rs 12,81,447** - wrong; this is only the *interest* component (19,31,447 − 6,50,000), not the maturity value.
- **B) Rs 13,91,000** - wrong; this applies **simple** interest: 6,50,000 × (1 + 0.095 × 12) = 6,50,000 × 2.14.
- **D) Rs 21,45,000** - wrong; this assumes a crude 11% flat growth and does not follow from the compounding formula.

</details>

**Q7.** A portfolio Meghna started with Rs 7,50,000 is worth Rs 21,00,000 after 9 years. What is the CAGR?
A) 8.00%  B) 12.12%  C) 14.44%  D) 20.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 12.12%**

CAGR = (FV ÷ PV)^(1/n) − 1 = (21,00,000 ÷ 7,50,000)^(1/9) − 1 = (2.8)^0.1111 − 1 = 1.12120 − 1 = **12.12% p.a.**

- **A) 8.00%** - wrong; at 8% the corpus would reach only about Rs 14.99 lakh in 9 years.
- **C) 14.44%** - wrong; this overstates the rate — 14.44% would produce roughly Rs 25.4 lakh.
- **D) 20.00%** - wrong; this is simple-interest thinking: total gain 13,50,000 ÷ 7,50,000 = 180%, divided by 9 years. It ignores compounding entirely.

</details>

**Q8.** The Deshmukhs start a SIP of Rs 18,000 at the end of every month for 14 years, expecting 12% p.a. compounded monthly. What corpus do they accumulate?
A) Rs 30,24,000  B) Rs 47,53,746  C) Rs 78,55,523  D) Rs 77,77,746
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 77,77,746**

r = 0.12 ÷ 12 = 0.01; n = 14 × 12 = 168; (1.01)¹⁶⁸ = 5.32097.
FV = 18,000 × [(5.32097 − 1) ÷ 0.01] = 18,000 × 432.097 = **Rs 77,77,746**.

- **A) Rs 30,24,000** - wrong; this is the total *contributed* (18,000 × 168), ignoring all returns.
- **B) Rs 47,53,746** - wrong; this is only the return component (77,77,746 − 30,24,000), not the corpus.
- **C) Rs 78,55,523** - wrong; this treats the SIP as an annuity **due** (× 1.01), but the question states payments at the end of each month.

</details>

**Q9.** Nandita Rane wants Rs 1.40 crore in 18 years for retirement, expecting 10.8% p.a. compounded monthly. What investment at the end of each month is required?
A) Rs 19,138  B) Rs 25,486  C) Rs 21,262  D) Rs 64,815
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 21,262**

r = 0.108 ÷ 12 = 0.009; n = 216; (1.009)²¹⁶ = 6.92512.
PMT = FV × r ÷ [(1+r)ⁿ − 1] = (1,40,00,000 × 0.009) ÷ 5.92512 = 1,26,000 ÷ 5.92512 = **Rs 21,262 a month**.

- **A) Rs 19,138** - wrong; this understates the requirement and would leave the corpus roughly Rs 14 lakh short.
- **B) Rs 25,486** - wrong; this overstates the requirement, overshooting the target by about Rs 25 lakh.
- **D) Rs 64,815** - wrong; this is simply 1,40,00,000 ÷ 216 — the target divided by the number of months, assuming no return at all.

</details>

**Q10.** An investment is expected to deliver a nominal 10.75% p.a. while inflation runs at 6.25% p.a. What is the real rate of return?
A) 4.24%  B) 4.06%  C) 4.50%  D) 17.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 4.24%**

Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1 = (1.1075 ÷ 1.0625) − 1 = 1.042353 − 1 = **4.24%**.

- **B) 4.06%** - wrong; this mis-applies the deflator and understates the real return.
- **C) 4.50%** - wrong; this is the crude subtraction 10.75 − 6.25, which always **overstates** the real return.
- **D) 17.00%** - wrong; this adds the two rates instead of deflating one by the other.

</details>

**Q11.** A bank quotes a nominal 9.6% p.a. on a deposit, compounded quarterly. What is the effective annual rate?
A) 9.60%  B) 9.83%  C) 10.03%  D) 9.95%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) 9.95%**

EAR = (1 + r/m)^m − 1 = (1 + 0.096/4)⁴ − 1 = (1.024)⁴ − 1 = 1.09951 − 1 = **9.95%**.

- **A) 9.60%** - wrong; that is the **nominal** rate. The nominal rate never changes with frequency — only the effective rate rises.
- **B) 9.83%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **half-yearly** compounding: (1.048)² − 1.
- **C) 10.03%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **monthly** compounding: (1.008)¹² − 1.

</details>

**Q12.** Vikram Chitale invests Rs 25,000 at the **beginning** of every month for 10 years, earning 12% p.a. compounded monthly. What corpus does he accumulate?
A) Rs 30,00,000  B) Rs 58,08,477  C) Rs 56,94,027  D) Rs 57,50,967
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 58,08,477**

r = 0.01; n = 120; (1.01)¹²⁰ = 3.30039.
Ordinary annuity FV = 25,000 × [(3.30039 − 1) ÷ 0.01] = 25,000 × 230.0387 = Rs 57,50,967.
Payments at the **beginning** make this an annuity **due**: 57,50,967 × 1.01 = **Rs 58,08,477**.

- **A) Rs 30,00,000** - wrong; this is the total contributed (25,000 × 120), ignoring returns.
- **C) Rs 56,94,027** - wrong; this *divides* by 1.01 instead of multiplying, moving the value the wrong way.
- **D) Rs 57,50,967** - wrong; this is the **ordinary** annuity value. An annuity due is always worth more because each payment earns one extra month.

</details>

**Q13.** The Deshmukhs' combined monthly income is Rs 2,85,000 and their total monthly expenses, including EMIs, are Rs 1,96,650. What is their savings ratio?
A) 31.00%  B) 22.25%  C) 44.93%  D) 69.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 31.00%**

Savings = 2,85,000 − 1,96,650 = Rs 88,350. Savings ratio = 88,350 ÷ 2,85,000 = **31.00%**. Cross-check: the expenses ratio is 1,96,650 ÷ 2,85,000 = 69.00%, and 31 + 69 = 100 ✓.

- **B) 22.25%** - wrong; no combination of the given figures produces this.
- **C) 44.93%** - wrong; this divides savings by **expenses** (88,350 ÷ 1,96,650) instead of by income.
- **D) 69.00%** - wrong; that is the **expenses** ratio, the complement of the savings ratio.

</details>

**Q14.** Meghna holds a savings balance of Rs 2,80,000, a liquid fund of Rs 3,95,000, equity mutual funds of Rs 14,00,000 and PPF of Rs 8,60,000. Household expenses are Rs 1,12,500 a month. What is the liquidity ratio?

**Correct: C) 6.00 months**

Liquid assets = savings 2,80,000 + liquid fund 3,95,000 = **Rs 6,75,000**. Equity funds and PPF do **not** qualify — one is volatile, the other locked in. Liquidity ratio = 6,75,000 ÷ 1,12,500 = **6.00 months**, at the top of the healthy 3–6 month band.

- **A) 0.50 months** - wrong; this divides by **annual** expenses (Rs 13,50,000) instead of monthly.
- **B) 18.44 months** - wrong; this wrongly counts the equity mutual funds as liquid assets.

**Q15.** A household has total assets of Rs 1,62,00,000 and total liabilities of Rs 58,32,000. What is its solvency ratio?
A) 0.28  B) 0.64  C) 0.36  D) 1.56
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 0.64**

Net worth = 1,62,00,000 − 58,32,000 = Rs 1,03,68,000. Solvency ratio = Net worth ÷ Total assets = 1,03,68,000 ÷ 1,62,00,000 = **0.64**. Cross-check: leverage ratio = 58,32,000 ÷ 1,62,00,000 = 0.36, and 0.64 + 0.36 = 1 ✓.

- **A) 0.28** - wrong; this is the difference between the two ratios, not either of them.
- **C) 0.36** - wrong; that is the **leverage** ratio (liabilities ÷ assets), the complement of solvency.
- **D) 1.56** - wrong; this inverts the ratio by dividing total assets by net worth.

</details>

**Q16.** Which statement about a household contingency fund is correct?
A) It should be sized at 3–6 months of gross income  B) It should be invested in equity mutual funds so it beats inflation  C) It becomes unnecessary once adequate health insurance is in place  D) It should be sized at 3–6 months of expenses and held in cash-like assets
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) It should be sized at 3–6 months of expenses and held in cash-like assets**

The fund exists to meet obligations during a job loss or emergency, so it is sized against **expenses** — what must actually be paid — and parked where **immediate availability and certainty of value** are assured: savings account, sweep-in FD or liquid/overnight funds. Return is secondary.

- **A) It should be sized at 3–6 months of gross income** - wrong; income is not the benchmark. A high earner with modest expenses would over-provision, and the reverse case would under-provision.
- **B) It should be invested in equity mutual funds so it beats inflation** - wrong; the moment the fund is needed (recession, job loss) is precisely when equity is likely to be down.
- **C) It becomes unnecessary once adequate health insurance is in place** - wrong; insurance and the emergency fund are complements. Insurance does not cover job loss, deductibles or cash-flow gaps.

</details>

**Q17.** A Mumbai couple's monthly budget balances every month yet collapses in March and October each year. Which category of outflow has most likely been omitted?
A) Fixed committed outflows  B) Variable discretionary outflows  C) Periodic lumpy outflows  D) Savings and investment outflows
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Periodic lumpy outflows**

Annual insurance premiums, school admission fees, festival spending, vacations and vehicle servicing are **periodic** — predictable but infrequent. They are the most commonly forgotten category, and their absence is exactly what makes an otherwise balanced budget fail in specific months. The fix is to divide each annual item by 12 and set that amount aside monthly.

- **A) Fixed committed outflows** - wrong; EMIs, rent and school fees recur every month and are the hardest items to overlook.
- **B) Variable discretionary outflows** - wrong; these are often underestimated but they occur every month, so they would not cause a failure concentrated in two specific months.
- **D) Savings and investment outflows** - wrong; omitting SIPs would make the budget look *better*, not create a shortfall.

</details>

**Q18.** The Deshmukhs take a home loan of Rs 62,00,000 at 8.75% p.a. for 22 years. What is the EMI?
A) Rs 52,993  B) Rs 23,485  C) Rs 45,208  D) Rs 58,142
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Rs 52,993**

r = 0.0875 ÷ 12 = 0.00729167; n = 22 × 12 = 264; (1.00729167)²⁶⁴ = 6.83303.
EMI = 62,00,000 × (0.00729167 × 6.83303) ÷ (6.83303 − 1) = 62,00,000 × 0.0498242 ÷ 5.83303 = **Rs 52,993**.

- **B) Rs 23,485** - wrong; this is principal ÷ number of months (62,00,000 ÷ 264), which charges no interest at all.
- **C) Rs 45,208** - wrong; this is the **first month's interest** (62,00,000 × 0.00729167), the classic near-miss distractor.
- **D) Rs 58,142** - wrong; this overstates the EMI and does not follow from the formula.

</details>

**Q19.** On the same Rs 62,00,000 loan at 8.75% for 22 years with an EMI of Rs 52,993, how much of the **first** EMI reduces the principal?
A) Rs 23,485  B) Rs 45,208  C) Rs 52,993  D) Rs 7,785
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 7,785**

Interest for month 1 = outstanding × monthly rate = 62,00,000 × 0.00729167 = Rs 45,208. Principal repaid = EMI − interest = 52,993 − 45,208 = **Rs 7,785**. Only about 15% of the first instalment touches the debt, which is why early prepayment saves so much interest.

- **A) Rs 23,485** - wrong; this is principal ÷ 264, an even principal instalment that an amortising loan does not follow.
- **B) Rs 45,208** - wrong; that is the **interest** portion, not the principal portion.
- **C) Rs 52,993** - wrong; that is the whole EMI, most of which is interest in month 1.

</details>

**Q21.** Devendra Nayak pledges his family gold with a bank to raise a loan, and the bank keeps the gold in its vault. What is the correct legal description of this security?
A) Pledge  B) Mortgage  C) Hypothecation  D) Assignment
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Pledge**

A **pledge** is security over **movable** property where **possession passes to the lender**. A gold loan is the standard Indian example.

- **B) Mortgage** - wrong; a mortgage is security over **immovable** property, such as the home securing a home loan.
- **C) Hypothecation** - wrong; hypothecation is security over movable property where **possession stays with the borrower** — a car loan is the standard example.
- **D) Assignment** - wrong; assignment transfers rights under a contract, as in a loan against an insurance policy.

</details>

**Q22.** A borrower carries a Rs 40,000 credit card balance at 42% p.a., a Rs 3,20,000 personal loan at 15% p.a. and a Rs 9,00,000 vehicle loan at 10% p.a. Which repayment strategy minimises the **total interest** paid?
A) Snowball — repay the smallest balance first  B) Blizzard — clear two small balances, then attack by rate  C) Avalanche — repay the highest interest rate first  D) Pro-rata — pay extra on all three in proportion to their balances
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Avalanche — repay the highest interest rate first**

The **avalanche** method directs every spare rupee at the costliest debt, here the 42% card, while paying minimums elsewhere. It is **mathematically optimal** and always minimises total interest.

- **A) Snowball — repay the smallest balance first** - wrong; the snowball is psychologically motivating but costs more in total interest because the rate is ignored.
- **B) Blizzard — clear two small balances, then attack by rate** - wrong; the blizzard is a compromise between motivation and mathematics, so it costs more than a pure avalanche.
- **D) Pro-rata — pay extra on all three in proportion to their balances** - wrong; spreading the surplus leaves the highest-rate balance outstanding longer, raising total interest.

</details>

**Q23.** Which of the following intermediaries is regulated by the **RBI** rather than by SEBI?
A) Portfolio managers  B) Account Aggregators  C) Alternative Investment Funds  D) Credit rating agencies
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Account Aggregators**

Account Aggregators are registered as **NBFC-AA** with the **RBI**. With the customer's explicit consent they retrieve and share financial data across institutions, and they are "data blind" — they cannot read or store the data.

- **A) Portfolio managers** - wrong; regulated by SEBI under the SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, 2020.
- **C) Alternative Investment Funds** - wrong; regulated by SEBI under the SEBI (AIF) Regulations, 2012.

</details>

**Q24.** Which market infrastructure institution acts as the **central counterparty**, guaranteeing that a trade settles even if one member defaults?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) The clearing corporation**

The **clearing corporation** interposes itself between buyer and seller as the **central counterparty (CCP)**, manages margins and operates the settlement guarantee fund, so no participant bears the credit risk of the other side.

- **A) The stock exchange** - wrong; the exchange provides the platform and **matches** trades. It does not guarantee settlement.

**Q28.** Purnima Iyer holds 750 shares of a company quoted at Rs 1,120. The company announces a bonus issue in the ratio 3:5. What is her holding and the theoretical ex-bonus price?
A) 1,050 shares at Rs 800  B) 1,200 shares at Rs 700  C) 1,200 shares at Rs 1,120  D) 2,250 shares at Rs 373
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Q62.** Which statement about the Markowitz efficient frontier is correct?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Every portfolio on it is efficient, and which one an investor should hold depends on his risk tolerance**

The frontier is the **set** of portfolios offering the highest return for each level of risk. It is built from the assets' returns, variances and covariances alone, without knowing anything about the investor. The investor's own risk tolerance then selects the point, at the tangency with his highest attainable indifference curve.

- **A) It identifies a single optimal portfolio that every rational investor should hold** - wrong; the frontier is a menu, not a single dish. Two investors can rationally pick different points on the same frontier.
- **C) Portfolios lying below and to the right of it are unattainable** - wrong; those portfolios are attainable but **inefficient** (dominated). It is the region **above and to the left** that is unattainable.
- **D) The global minimum variance portfolio is the best choice for every investor** - wrong; the GMVP suits only an investor whose tolerance places him at the extreme left of the frontier.

- **C) Disclosure directly to SEBI within 7 days of the transaction** - wrong; the 7-day period relates to **initial** disclosure of holdings on appointment or on becoming a promoter, and the route runs through the company.

**Q79.** Money derived from a crime is moved through three shell companies in two jurisdictions, with securities repeatedly bought and sold along the way. Which stage of money laundering does this describe?
A) Placement  B) Layering  C) Integration  D) Structuring the beneficial ownership
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Layering**

**Layering** is the second stage, in which a **series of conversions and movements** is used to distance the funds from their criminal source and break the audit trail. Wiring money through shell entities and churning securities is the textbook illustration.

- **A) Placement** - wrong; placement is the **first** stage, where cash is physically introduced into the financial system. It is the stage the CTR and the ban on cash and third-party payments attack.
- **C) Integration** - wrong; integration is the **final** stage, where the funds re-enter the legitimate economy as apparently clean wealth, for example by buying property.

### Case 1: The Deshmukh household budget, Powai

**Q91.** What is the Deshmukhs' monthly savings ratio?

**Correct: A) 24.00%**

Gross monthly income = 2,10,000 + 1,15,000 = Rs 3,25,000. Savings (surplus) = 3,25,000 − 2,47,000 = Rs 78,000. Savings ratio = Savings / Gross income = 78,000 / 3,25,000 = **24.00%**. (Self-check: the expenses ratio is 2,47,000 / 3,25,000 = 76.00%, and 24 + 76 = 100.)

- **C) 37.14%** - wrong; this divides the household surplus by Kunal's income alone (78,000 / 2,10,000).

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 3.46 months**

- **C) 5.48 months** - wrong; this divides by expenses net of EMIs (8,55,000 / 1,56,000), but EMIs are exactly the outflow an emergency fund must keep paying.

Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities = 1,96,00,000 − 71,00,000 = Rs 1,25,00,000. Solvency ratio = Net worth / Total assets = 1,25,00,000 / 1,96,00,000 = 0.6378 = **0.64**. (Self-check: the leverage ratio is 71,00,000 / 1,96,00,000 = 0.36, and 0.64 + 0.36 = 1.)

### Case 4: Devansh Iyer, PMS and AIF

**Q107.** What performance fee does the portfolio manager earn in Year 1?

**Correct: B) Rs 2,64,000**

Gain in Year 1 = 1,44,00,000 − 1,20,00,000 = Rs 24,00,000, i.e. 20%. The hurdle absorbs the first 9% of 1,20,00,000 = Rs 10,80,000, and there is no catch-up, so the fee applies only to the excess of 24,00,000 − 10,80,000 = Rs 13,20,000. Performance fee = 20% × 13,20,000 = **Rs 2,64,000**.

- **C) Rs 4,80,000** - wrong; this is 20% of the whole Rs 24 lakh gain, ignoring the hurdle rate entirely.

**Q108.** What performance fee may the manager charge in Year 3, applying the high watermark?

**Correct: B) Rs 2,40,000**

- **A) Nil** - wrong; the portfolio has now exceeded its previous peak, so a fee is due on the excess.
- **C) Rs 4,80,000** - wrong; this charges 20% on the Rs 24 lakh rise from the Year 2 low of Rs 1.32 crore, making the client pay twice for ground merely recovered — precisely what the high watermark prevents.
- **D) Rs 6,00,000** - wrong; this is 20% of the Rs 30 lakh rise measured from the original Rs 1.20 crore.

**Q119.** How should the reduction of the equity target from 65% to 50% be classified?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Strategic asset allocation, because it is driven by a change in the clients' circumstances and risk capacity**

Strategic asset allocation is about the **client**; tactical asset allocation is about the **market**. Variable income and the loss of a second salary reduce the household's ability to bear risk, so the long-term policy mix itself is revised and the IPS is rewritten. Nothing here rests on a view about market prices.

- **A) Tactical asset allocation, because it is a temporary deviation to be reversed later** - wrong; TAA is a temporary, market-driven deviation that lives inside the existing bands and is reversed. This change is permanent and client-driven.

### Case 9: Nikhil Rao's bond and index positions

Nikhil Rao is reviewing a corporate bond and two index positions. The bond has a face value of Rs 1,000, pays an 8.4% annual coupon, has exactly 6 years to maturity, and the market's required yield on comparable paper is 9.6% a year. Its Macaulay duration is 4.85 years. Nikhil holds a portfolio of similar bonds with a total market value of Rs 40,00,000, and the market is pricing in a further 60 basis point rise in yields. Separately, the Nifty spot is 24,000, the three-month risk-free rate is 7% a year, the index dividend yield is 1.2% a year, and the three-month Nifty future is quoted at 24,395. Nikhil also buys **two lots** of a Nifty call option struck at 24,200 at a premium of Rs 285 per unit; the lot size is 25 and the index settles at 24,860 on expiry.

**Q131.** What is the fair price of the corporate bond?
A) Rs 921.97  B) Rs 947.12  C) Rs 1,000.00  D) Rs 1,054.81
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 947.12**

A bond is an annuity of coupons plus a lump sum at redemption.
Annuity factor = [1 − (1.096)^−6] / 0.096 = 4.406792.
PV of coupons = 84 × 4.406792 = Rs 370.17.
PV of redemption = 1,000 / (1.096)^6 = 1,000 / 1.729778 = Rs 576.95.
Price = 370.17 + 576.95 = **Rs 947.12**, a discount, as it must be when the 8.4% coupon is below the 9.6% required yield.

- **A) Rs 921.97** - wrong; that is the estimated price *after* the further 60 bp rise in yields, not the price today.
- **C) Rs 1,000.00** - wrong; the bond would trade at par only if the required yield equalled the 8.4% coupon.
- **D) Rs 1,054.81** - wrong; this swaps the coupon and the yield, discounting a 9.6% coupon at 8.4%.

</details>

**Q132.** What is the bond's modified duration, and what rupee loss does the 60 basis point rise imply on Nikhil's Rs 40,00,000 holding?
A) 4.43 and a loss of Rs 1,06,204  B) 4.43 and a loss of Rs 17,701  C) 4.85 and a loss of Rs 1,16,400  D) 5.32 and a loss of Rs 1,27,574
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 4.43 and a loss of Rs 1,06,204**

Modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + y) = 4.85 / 1.096 = **4.4252, i.e. about 4.43**.
Percentage price change ≈ −MD × Δy = −4.4252 × 0.0060 = −2.6551%.
Rupee loss = 2.6551% × 40,00,000 = **Rs 1,06,204**.

- **B) 4.43 and a loss of Rs 17,701** - wrong; the modified duration is right but the yield change has been taken as 10 basis points instead of 60.
- **C) 4.85 and a loss of Rs 1,16,400** - wrong; this uses the Macaulay duration directly without dividing by (1 + y), overstating the sensitivity.
- **D) 5.32 and a loss of Rs 1,27,574** - wrong; this multiplies the Macaulay duration by (1 + y) instead of dividing.

</details>

**Q133.** Using that duration estimate, what is the bond's approximate price after the 60 basis point rise?
A) Rs 905.21  B) Rs 921.97  C) Rs 941.44  D) Rs 972.27
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 921.97**

New price ≈ Current price × (1 + percentage change) = 947.12 × (1 − 0.026551) = 947.12 − 25.15 = **Rs 921.97**. Because the price–yield relationship is convex, the true price will be slightly above this straight-line estimate — duration alone always overstates the fall when yields rise.

- **A) Rs 905.21** - wrong; this applies the modified duration to a 100 basis point move rather than 60 basis points.
- **C) Rs 941.44** - wrong; this deducts a flat 0.60% from the price instead of multiplying the yield change by the modified duration.
- **D) Rs 972.27** - wrong; this adds 2.6551% to the price, reversing the sign — prices fall when yields rise.

</details>

**Q134.** What is the fair value of the three-month Nifty future, and how does the quoted price of 24,395 compare?
A) 24,348; overpriced by about 47 points  B) 24,341; overpriced by about 54 points  C) 24,409; underpriced by about 14 points  D) 25,392; underpriced by about 997 points
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 24,341; overpriced by about 54 points**

Fair value = S × (1 + r − q)^T = 24,000 × (1 + 0.07 − 0.012)^0.25 = 24,000 × (1.058)^0.25 = 24,000 × 1.014195 = **24,340.68**, i.e. about 24,341. The quoted future at 24,395 is therefore 24,395 − 24,340.68 = **about 54 points above fair value**, an arbitrage opportunity to sell the future and buy the basket, before costs.

- **A) 24,348; overpriced by about 47 points** - wrong; this prorates the net cost of carry simply (24,000 × [1 + 0.058 × 0.25]) instead of compounding it over the quarter.
- **C) 24,409; underpriced by about 14 points** - wrong; this ignores the 1.2% dividend yield, which reduces the cost of carry.
- **D) 25,392; underpriced by about 997 points** - wrong; this applies the full annual net carry of 5.8% to a three-month contract, forgetting the exponent T = 0.25.

</details>

**Q135.** What is Nikhil's net profit on the two lots of the Nifty call at expiry?

## Section A — Independent MCQs (90 questions × 1 mark = 90 marks)

**Q1.** Which sequence correctly describes the six steps of the financial planning process?
A) Data gathering → Relationship → Goals → Risk profiling → Construction → Review  B) Goals → Relationship → Data gathering → Risk profiling → Review → Construction  C) Relationship → Goals → Data gathering → Construction → Risk profiling → Review  D) Relationship → Data gathering → Goals → Risk profiling → Construction → Review
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Relationship → Data gathering → Goals → Risk profiling → Construction → Review**

The prescribed order is: (1) establish the client–planner relationship in writing, (2) gather data on the client's financial situation, (3) define needs and goals, (4) risk profiling and asset allocation, (5) portfolio construction, (6) reviewing and rebalancing. The engagement terms must be settled **before** any data is collected.

- **A) Data gathering → Relationship → …** - wrong; you cannot collect a client's confidential financial data before the scope, services and compensation have been agreed.
- **B) Goals → Relationship → …** - wrong on two counts: the relationship is step 1, and review comes last, after construction.
- **C) Relationship → Goals → Data gathering → …** - wrong; goals cannot be quantified until the client's income, assets and liabilities are known, and risk profiling must precede construction, not follow it.

</details>

**Q2.** Ridhima Kaushal is a 34-year-old freelance brand designer in Delhi with irregular project income, no dependants and a 26-year retirement horizon. Her risk-profiling questionnaire returns a "conservative" attitude score. How should her adviser proceed?
A) Plan to the lower of capacity and tolerance, and work on education over time  B) Use her high risk capacity, since capacity is objective and attitude is merely psychological  C) Average the capacity and tolerance scores to derive a balanced allocation  D) Ignore both and set the allocation purely from the return the goal requires
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Plan to the lower of capacity and tolerance, and work on education over time**

**Risk capacity** is the objective *ability* to bear risk (horizon, income stability, net worth, dependants); **risk tolerance** is the subjective *willingness*. Where they conflict, the rule is to plan to the **lower** of the two. A client with high capacity but low tolerance panic-sells at the bottom, converting a paper loss into a permanent one.

- **B) Use her high risk capacity** - wrong; ignoring tolerance is exactly what produces panic-selling. Her freelance income is also lumpy, which arguably reduces capacity too.
- **C) Average the two scores** - wrong; there is no averaging rule. The binding constraint is the lower of the two.
- **D) Set allocation purely from risk requirement** - wrong; the return the goal *needs* is a third input, but it can never override the client's ability and willingness to bear risk.

</details>

**Q3.** Ridhima's balance sheet shows: savings account Rs 3,15,000; liquid fund Rs 4,85,000; equity mutual funds Rs 12,40,000; PPF Rs 6,20,000; studio equipment Rs 3,40,000; car Rs 5,80,000. Her car loan outstanding is Rs 2,95,000 and her credit card outstanding is Rs 68,000. What is her net worth?
A) Rs 26,60,000  B) Rs 32,17,000  C) Rs 35,80,000  D) Rs 39,43,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 32,17,000**

Total assets = 3,15,000 + 4,85,000 + 12,40,000 + 6,20,000 + 3,40,000 + 5,80,000 = **Rs 35,80,000**.
Total liabilities = 2,95,000 + 68,000 = **Rs 3,63,000**.
Net worth = 35,80,000 − 3,63,000 = **Rs 32,17,000**.

- **A) Rs 26,60,000** - wrong; this is her *financial* assets only (3,15,000 + 4,85,000 + 12,40,000 + 6,20,000), excluding the studio equipment and car and ignoring liabilities altogether.
- **C) Rs 35,80,000** - wrong; this is total assets, the classic distractor. Net worth is assets **minus** liabilities.
- **D) Rs 39,43,000** - wrong; this adds the liabilities to the assets instead of subtracting them.

</details>

**Q4.** Ridhima's studio purchase, a planned six-month sabbatical and her parents' medical corpus all fall due in the same calendar year. Her adviser recommends shifting the sabbatical out by two years. Which goal-setting rule is being applied?
A) Prioritising goals  B) Quantifying goals in future rupees  C) Ensuring adequate focus on important goals  D) Staggering the timing of goals
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Staggering the timing of goals**

Staggering means deliberately moving a **discretionary** goal forward or back so that funding requirements do not collide in a single year. If three large goals land together, the plan breaks even when each is individually affordable.

- **A) Prioritising goals** - wrong; prioritising is about *ranking* which goals get funded first when resources are short (retirement over a car upgrade), not about spacing them in time.
- **B) Quantifying goals in future rupees** - wrong; that is the requirement to inflate a goal's cost to its target date, not a timing decision.
- **C) Ensuring adequate focus on important goals** - wrong; that rule addresses the tendency to neglect long-dated goals such as retirement because they feel distant.

</details>

**Q5.** Which statement best distinguishes a financial planning engagement from a typical distribution-led advisory service?
A) The planner always delivers higher product returns than a distributor  B) The planner works across the client's whole balance sheet and cash flow under a fiduciary duty  C) The planner is paid by the product manufacturer and owes only a suitability standard  D) The planner is barred from reviewing the plan once it has been implemented
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) The planner works across the client's whole balance sheet and cash flow under a fiduciary duty**

Financial planning is triggered by a **client goal needing funding**, covers cash flow, insurance, debt, investment, tax, retirement and estate in an integrated way, is typically paid for by a **fee from the client**, and carries a **fiduciary** obligation to place the client's interest ahead of the adviser's own.

- **A) Higher product returns** - wrong; a product can beat its benchmark and still fail the client if it is illiquid when the money is needed. Planning is measured by whether the **goal** was met.
- **C) Paid by the manufacturer, suitability only** - wrong; that describes the distribution model, which is the thing being contrasted.
- **D) Barred from reviewing** - wrong; reviewing and rebalancing is step 6 of the process and the source of much of the planner's long-term value.

</details>

**Q6.** Ridhima receives a Rs 6,50,000 lump-sum fee for a rebranding project and invests the whole amount for 7 years at 11.5% p.a. compounded annually. What will it grow to?
A) Rs 11,73,250  B) Rs 12,49,000  C) Rs 13,92,635  D) Rs 15,52,788
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 13,92,635**

FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ = 6,50,000 × (1.115)⁷.
(1.115)⁷ = **2.142516**, so FV = 6,50,000 × 2.142516 = **Rs 13,92,635**.

- **A) Rs 11,73,250** - wrong; this is **simple** interest: 6,50,000 × (1 + 0.115 × 7) = 6,50,000 × 1.805. It ignores interest earning interest.
- **B) Rs 12,49,000** - wrong; this compounds for only **6** years: 6,50,000 × (1.115)⁶ = 6,50,000 × 1.921539.
- **D) Rs 15,52,788** - wrong; this compounds for **8** years: 6,50,000 × (1.115)⁸ = 6,50,000 × 2.388905.

</details>

**Q7.** Ridhima's equity portfolio grew from Rs 8,40,000 to Rs 19,60,000 over exactly 9 years, with no fresh money added and nothing withdrawn. What was the CAGR?
A) 8.84%  B) 9.87%  C) 11.17%  D) 14.81%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 9.87%**

r = (FV ÷ PV)^(1/n) − 1 = (19,60,000 ÷ 8,40,000)^(1/9) − 1 = (2.33333)^0.11111 − 1 = 1.09872 − 1 = **9.87% p.a.**

- **A) 8.84%** - wrong; this uses **10** years instead of 9 in the exponent.
- **C) 11.17%** - wrong; this uses **8** years instead of 9.
- **D) 14.81%** - wrong; this is simple-interest thinking — total gain of 133.33% divided by 9 years. CAGR compounds, so it is always lower than this crude average.

</details>

**Q8.** A small finance bank quotes a nominal 9.6% p.a. on a term deposit, compounded **quarterly**. What is the effective annual rate?
A) 9.60%  B) 9.83%  C) 9.95%  D) 10.03%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 9.95%**

EAR = (1 + r/m)^m − 1 = (1 + 0.096/4)⁴ − 1 = (1.024)⁴ − 1 = 1.099512 − 1 = **9.95%**.

- **A) 9.60%** - wrong; that is the **nominal** (stated) rate. The nominal rate never changes with frequency — only the effective rate rises.
- **B) 9.83%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **half-yearly** compounding: (1.048)² − 1.
- **D) 10.03%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **monthly** compounding: (1 + 0.096/12)¹² − 1.

</details>

**Q9.** To smooth her irregular income, Ridhima commits to a Rs 18,000 monthly SIP for 12 years, expecting 12% p.a. compounded monthly, with each instalment paid at the **end** of the month. What corpus will she accumulate?
A) Rs 25,92,000  B) Rs 41,92,000  C) Rs 57,43,108  D) Rs 58,00,539
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 57,43,108**

r = 0.12 ÷ 12 = 0.01; n = 12 × 12 = 144; (1.01)¹⁴⁴ = **4.190616**.
FV = 18,000 × [(4.190616 − 1) ÷ 0.01] = 18,000 × 319.0616 = **Rs 57,43,108**.

- **A) Rs 25,92,000** - wrong; this is merely the total **contributed** (18,000 × 144), ignoring all returns.
- **B) Rs 41,92,000** - wrong; this is 18,000 × 100 × 2.329, a mis-application of the lump-sum growth factor to the contributions.
- **D) Rs 58,00,539** - wrong; this is the **annuity due** value (57,43,108 × 1.01). The question specifies payment at the end of the month, i.e. an ordinary annuity.

</details>

**Q10.** Because her fees arrive in lumps, Ridhima sets aside Rs 30,000 at the **beginning** of every quarter for 6 years into a fund returning 10% p.a. compounded quarterly. What is the value at the end of 6 years?
A) Rs 7,20,000  B) Rs 9,25,873  C) Rs 9,70,471  D) Rs 9,94,733
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 9,94,733**

r = 0.10 ÷ 4 = 0.025; n = 6 × 4 = 24; (1.025)²⁴ = **1.808726**.
Ordinary annuity FV = 30,000 × [(1.808726 − 1) ÷ 0.025] = 30,000 × 32.34904 = Rs 9,70,471.
Payment at the **beginning** makes it an annuity **due**: 9,70,471 × 1.025 = **Rs 9,94,733**.

- **A) Rs 7,20,000** - wrong; this is only the total contributed (30,000 × 24), ignoring returns.
- **B) Rs 9,25,873** - wrong; this treats it as Rs 1,20,000 invested **annually** at 10% for 6 years, losing the benefit of quarterly compounding.
- **C) Rs 9,70,471** - wrong; this is the **ordinary** annuity value. Each payment here sits invested one extra quarter, so the answer must be multiplied by (1 + r).

</details>

**Q11.** Ridhima's short-duration debt fund is expected to deliver 8.4% a year while inflation runs at 5.6%. What is her expected **real** return?
A) 1.50%  B) 2.65%  C) 2.80%  D) 14.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 2.65%**

Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1 = (1.084 ÷ 1.056) − 1 = 1.026515 − 1 = **2.65%**.

- **A) 1.50%** - wrong; this comes from dividing the two rates themselves (8.4 ÷ 5.6 = 1.5) and reading the result as a percentage.
- **C) 2.80%** - wrong; this is the crude subtraction 8.4 − 5.6. It always **overstates** the real return, and the exam offers it deliberately.
- **D) 14.00%** - wrong; this adds the two rates instead of deflating.

</details>

**Q12.** A licensing arrangement will pay Ridhima Rs 96,000 at the end of next year, and the payment is expected to grow at 4.5% a year **in perpetuity**. At a 9% discount rate, what is the present value of the stream?
A) Rs 2,13,333  B) Rs 7,11,111  C) Rs 10,66,667  D) Rs 21,33,333
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 21,33,333**

Growing perpetuity: PV = PMT₁ ÷ (r − g) = 96,000 ÷ (0.09 − 0.045) = 96,000 ÷ 0.045 = **Rs 21,33,333**. The condition g < r is satisfied.

- **A) Rs 2,13,333** - wrong; a decimal slip — dividing by 0.45 rather than 0.045.
- **B) Rs 7,11,111** - wrong; this **adds** the growth rate to the discount rate (96,000 ÷ 0.135) instead of subtracting it.
- **C) Rs 10,66,667** - wrong; this is a level perpetuity, 96,000 ÷ 0.09, ignoring growth entirely.

</details>

**Q13.** Over the last 12 months Ridhima's professional fees averaged Rs 1,45,000 a month and her total outflows averaged Rs 1,04,400 a month. What is her savings ratio?
A) 28.00%  B) 38.89%  C) 40.60%  D) 72.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 28.00%**

Savings = 1,45,000 − 1,04,400 = Rs 40,600.
Savings ratio = Savings ÷ Gross income = 40,600 ÷ 1,45,000 = **28.00%** — comfortably above the 20% healthy threshold.

- **B) 38.89%** - wrong; this divides savings by **expenses** (40,600 ÷ 1,04,400) instead of by income.
- **C) 40.60%** - wrong; this is the savings *amount* in thousands read off as a percentage.
- **D) 72.00%** - wrong; this is the **expenses** ratio (1,04,400 ÷ 1,45,000). Savings ratio + expenses ratio = 100%, a useful self-check.

</details>

**Q14.** Ridhima holds a savings balance of Rs 2,10,000, a liquid fund of Rs 3,45,000, equity mutual funds of Rs 9,00,000 and PPF of Rs 4,00,000. Her monthly expenses are Rs 1,20,000. What is her liquidity ratio?
A) 2.88 months  B) 4.63 months  C) 7.96 months  D) 15.46 months
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 4.63 months**

Liquid assets = savings balance + liquid fund = 2,10,000 + 3,45,000 = Rs 5,55,000. **Equity and PPF are not liquid assets** — one is volatile, the other locked in.
Liquidity ratio = 5,55,000 ÷ 1,20,000 = **4.63 months**, inside the 3–6 band but thin for someone with irregular freelance income.

- **A) 2.88 months** - wrong; this counts only the liquid fund and omits the savings balance.
- **C) 7.96 months** - wrong; this wrongly includes PPF (5,55,000 + 4,00,000) in liquid assets. PPF is locked in for 15 years.
- **D) 15.46 months** - wrong; this includes every financial asset, equity mutual funds included. Equity must never back an emergency fund.

</details>

**Q15.** Ridhima's total assets are Rs 48,60,000 and her total liabilities Rs 17,01,000. What is her solvency ratio?
A) 0.35  B) 0.54  C) 0.65  D) 2.86
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 0.65**

Net worth = 48,60,000 − 17,01,000 = Rs 31,59,000.
Solvency ratio = Net worth ÷ Total assets = 31,59,000 ÷ 48,60,000 = **0.65** — healthy, since above 0.5.

- **A) 0.35** - wrong; this is the **leverage** ratio (liabilities ÷ assets). Solvency + leverage = 1, so 0.35 is its complement.
- **B) 0.54** - wrong; this divides liabilities by net worth (17,01,000 ÷ 31,59,000), which is neither ratio.
- **D) 2.86** - wrong; this inverts the leverage ratio (assets ÷ liabilities).

</details>

**Q16.** Given Ridhima's irregular freelance income, which statement about her contingency fund is correct?
A) It should equal 3–6 months of gross income and sit in equity funds for growth  B) It is unnecessary once she holds adequate health and life insurance  C) It should equal one year of EMIs and be held in her PPF account  D) It should be sized against monthly expenses, held above the usual 3–6 months, and parked in savings, sweep-in FDs or liquid funds
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) It should be sized against monthly expenses, held above the usual 3–6 months, and parked in savings, sweep-in FDs or liquid funds**

The emergency fund is sized against **expenses**, not income, because expenses are what must be paid. Irregular income, single-earner status and many dependants all argue for going above the standard 3–6 months. The requirement is **immediate availability plus certainty of value** — return is secondary.

- **A) 3–6 months of gross income in equity** - wrong twice: sized against income rather than expenses, and equity is exactly the wrong home. The moment the fund is needed (recession, project drought) is when equity is most likely to be down.
- **B) Unnecessary given insurance** - wrong; insurance is *part* of contingency planning, not a substitute. It does not pay the rent during a six-week gap between projects.
- **C) One year of EMIs in PPF** - wrong; the fund covers all living expenses, not just debt servicing, and PPF is locked in.

</details>

**Q17.** Ridhima's lumpy annual outflows are: term and health insurance premiums Rs 42,000, professional association fee Rs 18,000, festival and gifting spend Rs 36,000 and car servicing Rs 14,400. How much should she set aside each month so these never break her budget?
A) Rs 3,500  B) Rs 5,500  C) Rs 9,200  D) Rs 1,10,400
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 9,200**

Total periodic outflows = 42,000 + 18,000 + 36,000 + 14,400 = Rs 1,10,400 a year.
Monthly set-aside = 1,10,400 ÷ 12 = **Rs 9,200**. Ignoring **periodic** outflows is the commonest budgeting failure: a budget that balances every month collapses in the month the annual premium falls due.

- **A) Rs 3,500** - wrong; this provides only for the insurance premiums (42,000 ÷ 12) and ignores the other three items.
- **B) Rs 5,500** - wrong; this covers only the association fee and car servicing (32,400 ÷ 12 = 2,700) plus a partial provision — it under-provides.
- **D) Rs 1,10,400** - wrong; this is the **annual** total, not the monthly set-aside.

</details>

**Q18.** Ridhima borrows Rs 12,00,000 to re-equip her studio, at 13% p.a. for 5 years. What is her EMI?
A) Rs 13,000  B) Rs 20,000  C) Rs 27,304  D) Rs 33,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 27,304**

r = 0.13 ÷ 12 = 0.0108333; n = 60; (1.0108333)⁶⁰ = **1.908857**.
EMI = 12,00,000 × (0.0108333 × 1.908857) ÷ (1.908857 − 1) = 12,00,000 × 0.0206792 ÷ 0.908857 = **Rs 27,304**.

- **A) Rs 13,000** - wrong; this is the **first month's interest** (12,00,000 × 0.0108333), a standard distractor.
- **B) Rs 20,000** - wrong; this is principal ÷ n (12,00,000 ÷ 60) with no interest at all.
- **D) Rs 33,000** - wrong; this is a flat/simple-interest calculation: (12,00,000 + 12,00,000 × 0.13 × 5) ÷ 60.

</details>

**Q19.** On the same Rs 12,00,000 studio loan (13% p.a., 5 years, EMI Rs 27,304), what is the total interest Ridhima will pay over the full tenure?

**Correct: B) Rs 4,38,240**

Total interest = (EMI × n) − P = (27,304 × 60) − 12,00,000 = 16,38,240 − 12,00,000 = **Rs 4,38,240**.

- **C) Rs 7,80,000** - wrong; this is **simple** interest (12,00,000 × 0.13 × 5). It ignores the fact that the outstanding principal falls every month.
- **D) Rs 16,38,240** - wrong; this is the **total repaid** (EMI × 60), which includes the principal. The question asks for interest only.

</details>

**Q20.** Ridhima's partner takes a Rs 55,00,000 home loan at 8.7% p.a. for 22 years; the EMI works out to Rs 46,830. How much of the **first** EMI actually reduces the principal?
A) Rs 6,955  B) Rs 20,833  C) Rs 39,875  D) Rs 46,830
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Rs 6,955**

Monthly rate = 0.087 ÷ 12 = 0.00725.
Interest in month 1 = 55,00,000 × 0.00725 = Rs 39,875.
Principal in month 1 = 46,830 − 39,875 = **Rs 6,955** — barely 15% of the EMI. This is why early prepayment saves so much interest.

- **B) Rs 20,833** - wrong; this is principal ÷ n (55,00,000 ÷ 264), which would only be the split if the loan carried no interest.
- **C) Rs 39,875** - wrong; this is the **interest** component, not the principal component.
- **D) Rs 46,830** - wrong; this is the whole EMI. Only the part left after interest reduces the debt.

</details>

**Q21.** Ridhima pledges gold for a gold loan, hypothecates her new car for a car loan, and mortgages a shop for a loan against property. Which statement is correct?
A) A pledge passes possession of movable property to the lender, hypothecation leaves possession with the borrower, and a mortgage is over immovable property  B) A mortgage is created over movable property and a pledge over immovable property  C) In a pledge the borrower retains possession; in hypothecation the lender takes possession  D) All three require the lender to take physical possession of the asset
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) A pledge passes possession of movable property to the lender, hypothecation leaves possession with the borrower, and a mortgage is over immovable property**

The three security forms: **mortgage = immovable property**; **pledge = movable, lender holds it** (gold loan); **hypothecation = movable, borrower keeps it** (car loan).

- **B) Mortgage over movable, pledge over immovable** - wrong; exactly the wrong way round.

</details>

**Q22.** Ridhima owes Rs 62,000 on a credit card at 42% p.a., Rs 2,40,000 on a personal loan at 16% p.a. and Rs 4,80,000 on a car loan at 9.5% p.a. She wants the mathematically cheapest route out of debt. Which method should she use and where should she start?
A) Snowball, starting with the credit card  B) Avalanche, starting with the credit card  C) Snowball, starting with the car loan  D) Avalanche, starting with the car loan
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Avalanche, starting with the credit card**

The **avalanche** method attacks the **highest interest rate** first while paying minimums on the rest — it is mathematically optimal and minimises total interest. Here the highest rate is the 42% credit card, which happens also to be the smallest balance.

- **A) Snowball, credit card** - wrong method label. Snowball targets the **smallest balance** regardless of rate; it happens to point at the same loan here, but it is not the mathematically cheapest rule and the question asked for that rule.
- **C) Snowball, car loan** - wrong; the car loan is the **largest** balance, so snowball would never start there.
- **D) Avalanche, car loan** - wrong; the car loan carries the **lowest** rate (9.5%), so avalanche attacks it last.

</details>

**Q23.** Which regulator supervises the money market, credit information companies and NBFC-Account Aggregators?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) RBI**

- **A) SEBI** - wrong; SEBI's remit is the **securities market** — exchanges, brokers, mutual funds, portfolio managers, investment advisers, AIFs, credit **rating** agencies and depositories. Note that credit *rating* agencies (SEBI) differ from credit *information* companies (RBI).

**Q24.** Ridhima's buy order for a listed share is matched on the exchange. Which entity then acts as the central counterparty and guarantees that the trade settles?
A) The clearing corporation  B) The depository  C) The depository participant  D) SEBI
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) The clearing corporation**

The **exchange matches** trades; the **clearing corporation clears and settles** them, standing between buyer and seller as the **central counterparty (CCP)**. It manages margins and the settlement guarantee fund, so neither side faces counterparty default.

- **B) The depository** - wrong; NSDL and CDSL hold securities in electronic (demat) form. They do not guarantee settlement.
- **C) The depository participant** - wrong; a DP is the **agent of the depository** and the investor's access point for a demat account.
- **D) SEBI** - wrong; SEBI is the regulator, not a settlement counterparty.

</details>

**Q25.** Which statement about payment banks in India is correct?
A) They may accept deposits up to Rs 5 lakh per customer and issue credit cards  B) They may lend to priority sectors but cannot accept demand deposits  C) They may accept deposits up to Rs 2 lakh per customer but cannot lend  D) They are regulated by SEBI as market infrastructure institutions
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) They may accept deposits up to Rs 2 lakh per customer but cannot lend**

Payment banks accept deposits capped at **Rs 2 lakh per customer** and provide payments and remittance services, but they **cannot lend and cannot issue credit cards**.

- **B) Lend to priority sectors, no demand deposits** - wrong; that describes a **small finance bank**, which does full banking with a priority-sector focus.
- **D) SEBI-regulated MII** - wrong; payment banks are RBI-regulated. The three MIIs are exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories.

</details>

**Q26.** Ridhima uses an Account Aggregator to share her bank and mutual fund data with a prospective lender. Which statement is correct?
A) The AA is a SEBI-registered intermediary that stores and analyses the shared data  B) The AA may share the data without consent provided the recipient is RBI-regulated  C) The AA may lend on its own book once it holds the customer's data  D) The AA is an RBI-regulated NBFC-AA that moves data only with explicit consent and is "data blind"
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Q80.** Section 15EB of the SEBI Act provides a penalty of not less than Rs 1 lakh, extending to Rs 1 lakh for each day a failure continues, subject to a maximum of Rs 1 crore. After how many days of continuing default is the statutory ceiling reached?

**Q81.** Over one year an adviser turns over Ritwik's Rs 25,00,000 portfolio eight times, moving between funds with almost identical mandates. The stated strategy never changes, but the adviser earns fresh commission on each switch. What is this called?

**Correct: B) Churning**

**Churning is excessive trading, switching or turnover in a client's portfolio to generate brokerage or fresh commission.** The give-away is exactly what is described: **a lot of activity with no change in the underlying strategy**. ### Case 8: Ranking three managers for the Ambedkar Family Trust

The Ambedkar Family Trust is choosing between three equity managers for the coming year. Over the review period the risk-free rate was 6.5% and the benchmark index returned 13.0%. **Q126.** Which manager delivered the highest return per unit of total risk, and what was that ratio?

Bhaskar: (13.8 − 6.5) ÷ 9.5 = 7.3 ÷ 9.5 = **0.77**.
Chinar: (18.9 − 6.5) ÷ 19.5 = 12.4 ÷ 19.5 = 0.64.

- **A) Arcline, 0.71** - wrong; Arcline's Sharpe ratio is correctly computed but it is not the highest.

A) 0.64  B) 0.72  C) 0.82  D) 1.42
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 0.82**

First the return: 0.65 × 16.4 + 0.35 × 13.8 = 10.66 + 4.83 = 15.49%.
Then the ratio: (15.49 − 6.5) ÷ 10.92 = 8.99 ÷ 10.92 = **0.82**.
The blend beats every individual manager's Sharpe ratio (0.71, 0.77, 0.64) — return is a weighted average but risk is not, so combining imperfectly correlated managers improves the trade-off.

- **A) 0.64** - wrong; this divides the blend's excess return by Arcline's standard deviation of 14% instead of the portfolio's 10.92%.
- **B) 0.72** - wrong; this uses the weighted-average standard deviation of 12.43%, throwing away the diversification benefit.
- **D) 1.42** - wrong; this omits the risk-free rate, dividing the raw 15.49% return by 10.92%.

### Case 9: Vasudha Iyer's bond and index book

Vasudha Iyer holds Bond X: face value Rs 1,000, an annual coupon of 8.4%, exactly 5 years to maturity, and a required yield in the market of 9.6%. Her analyst has computed the bond's Macaulay duration at that yield as 4.29 years. She expects the required yield to rise by 75 basis points over the next quarter. On the equity side, the Nifty spot index stands at 24,800, the one-month risk-free rate is 7% a year, and the index dividend yield is 1.2% a year. Expecting a moderate rally, Vasudha also builds a bull call spread on the index: she buys one lot of the 24,800-strike call at a premium of Rs 182 and sells one lot of the 25,200-strike call at a premium of Rs 74. The index lot size is 75. At expiry the Nifty settles at 25,400.

**Q131.** What is the fair price of Bond X today?
A) Rs 321.71  B) Rs 632.34  C) Rs 954.04  D) Rs 1,047.41
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 954.04**

A bond is an annuity of coupons plus a lump sum at redemption.
(1.096)^5 = 1.581440, so the discount factor is 1 ÷ 1.581440 = 0.632335.
Annuity factor = (1 − 0.632335) ÷ 0.096 = 3.829843.
PV of coupons = 84 × 3.829843 = Rs 321.71.
PV of redemption = 1,000 × 0.632335 = Rs 632.34.
Price = 321.71 + 632.34 = **Rs 954.04** — a discount, as it must be when the 8.4% coupon is below the 9.6% required yield.

- **A) Rs 321.71** - wrong; that is only the present value of the coupon stream, omitting the redemption amount.
- **B) Rs 632.34** - wrong; that is only the present value of the Rs 1,000 redemption, omitting the coupons.
- **D) Rs 1,047.41** - wrong; this swaps the coupon and the yield, pricing a 9.6% coupon at an 8.4% yield, which produces a premium bond instead.

</details>

**Q132.** By approximately what percentage will Bond X's price fall if the required yield rises by 75 basis points?
A) −2.94%  B) −3.22%  C) −4.29%  D) −29.36%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) −2.94%**

First convert Macaulay duration to modified duration: MD = 4.29 ÷ (1 + 0.096) = 4.29 ÷ 1.096 = 3.9142.
Then apply the price-change rule: %ΔP ≈ −MD × Δy = −3.9142 × 0.0075 = −0.02936 = **−2.94%**.

- **B) −3.22%** - wrong; this uses the Macaulay duration of 4.29 directly (−4.29 × 0.0075) without dividing by (1 + y).
- **C) −4.29%** - wrong; that is the Macaulay duration read as if it were a percentage, ignoring the size of the yield move.
- **D) −29.36%** - wrong; a decimal slip that treats 75 basis points as 7.5%.

</details>

**Q133.** What is Bond X's approximate price after the 75 basis point rise in yields?
A) Rs 923.35  B) Rs 926.03  C) Rs 954.04  D) Rs 982.05
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 926.03**

The price falls by 2.94% of Rs 954.04, i.e. 954.04 × 0.029357 = Rs 28.01. New price ≈ 954.04 − 28.01 = **Rs 926.03**. (Duration is a first-order approximation; convexity means the true price will be a little higher, which works in the bondholder's favour.)

- **A) Rs 923.35** - wrong; this applies the Macaulay duration of 4.29 instead of the modified duration of 3.91.
- **C) Rs 954.04** - wrong; that is the price before the yield change.
- **D) Rs 982.05** - wrong; this adds the price change instead of subtracting it. Price and yield move in opposite directions.

</details>

**Q134.** Using the cost-of-carry model, by roughly how many index points should the one-month Nifty futures trade above the spot index?
A) 25 points  B) 117 points  C) 145 points  D) 1,438 points
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 117 points**

F = S × (1 + r − q)^T with r = 7%, q = 1.2% and T = 1/12.
(1 + 0.07 − 0.012)^(1/12) = (1.058)^(0.083333) = 1.004709.
F = 24,800 × 1.004709 = 24,916.8.
Premium over spot = 24,916.8 − 24,800 = **about 117 points**. The futures trade above spot — contango — because the financing cost exceeds the dividend yield.

- **A) 25 points** - wrong; this carries only the dividend yield (24,800 × 1.2% ÷ 12), leaving out the financing cost.
- **C) 145 points** - wrong; this carries only the 7% interest cost and ignores the dividend yield, which is a credit to the carry.
- **D) 1,438 points** - wrong; this applies the full annual net carry of 5.8% instead of one month of it.

</details>

**Q135.** What is Vasudha's net profit on the bull call spread at expiry?
A) Rs 8,100  B) Rs 21,900  C) Rs 30,000  D) Rs 31,350
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 21,900**

Net premium paid = (182 − 74) × 75 = 108 × 75 = Rs 8,100.
At a settlement of 25,400 the long 24,800 call is worth 25,400 − 24,800 = 600 points, and the short 25,200 call owes 25,400 − 25,200 = 200 points. Net payoff = 400 × 75 = Rs 30,000.
Net profit = 30,000 − 8,100 = **Rs 21,900**. This is also the strategy's maximum profit, (400 − 108) × 75, since the index closed above the higher strike.

- **A) Rs 8,100** - wrong; that is the net premium outlay, which is also the maximum *loss* on the strategy.
- **C) Rs 30,000** - wrong; that is the gross payoff at expiry before deducting the net premium paid.
- **D) Rs 31,350** - wrong; this counts only the long call, (600 − 182) × 75, ignoring the short 25,200 call that caps the gain.

</details>

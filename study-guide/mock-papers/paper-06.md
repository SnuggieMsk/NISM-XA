**Q1.** Faizal Rahman has returned to Kochi after twelve years in Dubai. His statement of affairs shows NRE fixed deposits Rs 34,00,000, equity mutual funds Rs 27,50,000, an apartment in Kochi worth Rs 96,00,000, an imported car worth Rs 11,00,000 and gold worth Rs 8,50,000. He owes an outstanding home loan principal of Rs 41,00,000 and a credit card balance of Rs 1,50,000. What is his net worth?
A) Rs 1,23,50,000  B) Rs 1,34,50,000  C) Rs 1,36,00,000  D) Rs 1,77,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 1,34,50,000**

Total assets = 34,00,000 + 27,50,000 + 96,00,000 + 11,00,000 + 8,50,000 = **Rs 1,77,00,000**.
Total liabilities = 41,00,000 + 1,50,000 = **Rs 42,50,000**.
Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities = 1,77,00,000 − 42,50,000 = **Rs 1,34,50,000**.

- **A) Rs 1,23,50,000** - wrong; this drops the car from the asset side. Personal-use assets are still assets on the personal balance sheet, even though they depreciate.
- **C) Rs 1,36,00,000** - wrong; this ignores the credit card outstanding. Short-term liabilities must be included.
- **D) Rs 1,77,00,000** - wrong; this is total assets, not net worth.

</details>

**Q2.** Faizal is 44, has a stable consultancy income, no dependants and a sixteen-year horizon, so the questionnaire scores his risk capacity as high. In discussion, however, he says a 12% fall in a single month would make him liquidate everything. How should the adviser set the asset allocation?
A) To the higher of the two, because capacity is objective and measurable  B) To the lower of the two, because willingness will govern actual behaviour  C) To capacity alone, since tolerance is merely psychological and can be coached away  D) To the arithmetic mid-point of the two scores
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) To the lower of the two, because willingness will govern actual behaviour**

Risk **capacity** is the objective *ability* to bear risk (horizon, income stability, net worth, dependants). Risk **tolerance** is the subjective *willingness* to bear it. Where the two conflict, the plan must be built to the **lower** of the two — otherwise the client panic-sells at the bottom and converts a temporary paper loss into a permanent one.

- **A) To the higher of the two** - wrong; this is exactly the mistake that produces panic selling.
- **C) To capacity alone** - wrong; tolerance is a genuine planning constraint, not noise to be overridden.
- **D) The arithmetic mid-point** - wrong; there is no such averaging rule, and it still leaves the client above his behavioural limit.

</details>

**Q3.** In the six-step financial planning process, which step immediately follows "defining client needs and financial goals"?
A) Establishing the client–planner relationship  B) Gathering data on the client's financial situation  C) Risk profiling and asset allocation  D) Reviewing and rebalancing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Risk profiling and asset allocation**

The sequence is: **(1)** establish the client–planner relationship → **(2)** gather data on the client's financial situation → **(3)** define client needs and financial goals → **(4) risk profiling and asset allocation** → **(5)** portfolio construction → **(6)** reviewing and rebalancing.

- **A) Establishing the relationship** - wrong; that is step 1, well before goals are defined.
- **B) Gathering data** - wrong; that is step 2, immediately *before* goal definition.
- **D) Reviewing and rebalancing** - wrong; that is the final step, step 6.

</details>

**Q4.** Faizal's daughter's college fees of about Rs 38 lakh and his planned Rs 30 lakh renovation of the Kochi flat both fall due in 2031. His adviser recommends deferring the renovation to 2034 so the funding requirement smooths out. Which goal-setting principle is being applied?
A) Prioritising goals  B) Staggering the timing of goals  C) Ensuring adequate focus on important goals  D) Quantifying goals in future rupees
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Staggering the timing of goals**

**Staggering** means shifting a discretionary goal forward or back so that several large funding requirements do not collide in the same year and break the plan. The education goal is non-negotiable in its date; the renovation is discretionary, so it moves.

- **A) Prioritising goals** - wrong; prioritising is about deciding which goals get funded first when resources are short, not about moving dates.
- **C) Ensuring adequate focus on important goals** - wrong; that principle guards long-dated goals such as retirement from being neglected.
- **D) Quantifying in future rupees** - wrong; that is about inflating the cost, which has already been done here.

</details>

**Q5.** Which statement best captures the defining obligation of a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser as compared with a product distributor?
A) The adviser must place the client's interest ahead of their own and keep advice segregated from distribution  B) The adviser must guarantee that recommended products beat their benchmark  C) The adviser must recommend only products manufactured by SEBI-registered entities  D) The adviser must earn commission from the product manufacturer rather than a fee from the client
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) The adviser must place the client's interest ahead of their own and keep advice segregated from distribution**

An Investment Adviser owes a **fiduciary** duty — the client's interest comes first. SEBI reinforces this by requiring the **segregation of advice and distribution**, so the recommendation is not shaped by what pays the adviser best. A distributor may be held only to a suitability standard.

- **B) Guarantee benchmark-beating returns** - wrong; no adviser may guarantee returns, and performance is not the fiduciary test.
- **C) Only SEBI-registered manufacturers** - wrong; advice may span bank deposits, small savings and insurance too.
- **D) Commission from the manufacturer** - wrong; that describes the distribution model the fiduciary standard is designed to keep separate.

</details>

**Q6.** Faizal's end-of-service gratuity from his Dubai employer is Rs 18,50,000. He invests the whole amount in India at 9.5% p.a. compounded annually for 7 years. What is the maturity value?
A) Rs 16,41,970  B) Rs 30,80,250  C) Rs 34,91,970  D) Rs 35,69,450
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 34,91,970**

FV = PV × (1 + r)^n = 18,50,000 × (1.095)^7.
(1.095)^7 = **1.88755**.
FV = 18,50,000 × 1.88755 = **Rs 34,91,970**.

- **A) Rs 16,41,970** - wrong; this is the *interest earned* (34,91,970 − 18,50,000), not the maturity value.
- **B) Rs 30,80,250** - wrong; this applies simple interest, 18,50,000 × (1 + 0.095 × 7).
- **D) Rs 35,69,450** - wrong; this compounds quarterly, which the question does not state.

</details>

**Q7.** The Kochi apartment Faizal bought twelve years ago for Rs 62,00,000 is now valued at Rs 1,48,00,000. What compound annual growth rate does this represent?
A) 6.00%  B) 7.52%  C) 11.56%  D) 138.71%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 7.52%**

CAGR = (FV ÷ PV)^(1/n) − 1 = (1,48,00,000 ÷ 62,00,000)^(1/12) − 1 = (2.3871)^(0.08333) − 1 = 1.0752 − 1 = **7.52% p.a.**

- **A) 6.00%** - wrong; this is 72 ÷ 12 from a careless Rule of 72 shortcut, which only applies when money has exactly *doubled*.
- **C) 11.56%** - wrong; this is the total gain divided by 12 years — simple-interest thinking that ignores compounding.
- **D) 138.71%** - wrong; this is the *total* percentage gain over the whole twelve years, not an annual rate.

</details>

**Q8.** A bank offers Faizal a rupee term deposit at a nominal 8.4% p.a. compounded quarterly. What is the effective annual rate?
A) 8.40%  B) 8.58%  C) 8.67%  D) 8.73%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 8.67%**

EAR = (1 + r/m)^m − 1 with r = 0.084 and m = 4.
= (1 + 0.021)^4 − 1 = 1.08668 − 1 = **8.67%**.

- **A) 8.40%** - wrong; that is the *nominal* (stated) rate, which never changes with compounding frequency.
- **B) 8.58%** - wrong; that is the EAR for **half-yearly** compounding.

**Q9.** Preetha Ravindran invests Rs 45,000 at the end of every month for 11 years in a fund expected to return 10.5% p.a. compounded monthly. What corpus does she accumulate?
A) Rs 51,58,891  B) Rs 59,40,000  C) Rs 1,10,98,891  D) Rs 1,11,96,006
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 1,10,98,891**

r = 0.105 ÷ 12 = **0.00875**; n = 11 × 12 = **132**; (1.00875)^132 = **3.15812**.
FV = PMT × [((1+r)^n − 1) ÷ r] = 45,000 × [(3.15812 − 1) ÷ 0.00875] = 45,000 × 246.6421 = **Rs 1,10,98,891**.

- **A) Rs 51,58,891** - wrong; this is only the *return* component (corpus minus contributions).
- **B) Rs 59,40,000** - wrong; this is the total *contributed* (45,000 × 132), ignoring all growth.
- **D) Rs 1,11,96,006** - wrong; this treats the SIP as an annuity **due** (start-of-month), but the question states end of month.

</details>

**Q10.** Zubin Wadia holds a fixed deposit yielding 8.4% p.a. He is in the 30% tax bracket and inflation is running at 6.5%. What is his post-tax real return?
A) −0.58%  B) 1.78%  C) 1.90%  D) 5.88%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) −0.58%**

Tax first, then deflate.
Post-tax nominal = 8.4% × (1 − 0.30) = **5.88%**.
Real return = [(1 + 0.0588) ÷ (1 + 0.065)] − 1 = (1.0588 ÷ 1.065) − 1 = **−0.58%**.
The "safe" deposit is quietly losing purchasing power.

- **B) 1.78%** - wrong; this is the *pre-tax* real return, (1.084 ÷ 1.065) − 1.
- **C) 1.90%** - wrong; this is the crude pre-tax subtraction, 8.4 − 6.5.
- **D) 5.88%** - wrong; this is the post-tax *nominal* return, before deflating for inflation.

</details>

**Q11.** Aruna Chidambaram invests Rs 30,000 at the **beginning** of every month for 8 years, earning 9% p.a. compounded monthly. What is the accumulated value?
A) Rs 28,80,000  B) Rs 39,70,251  C) Rs 41,95,685  D) Rs 42,27,153
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 42,27,153**

r = 0.09 ÷ 12 = **0.0075**; n = 8 × 12 = **96**; (1.0075)^96 = **2.04892**.
Ordinary annuity FV = 30,000 × [(2.04892 − 1) ÷ 0.0075] = **Rs 41,95,685**.
Annuity **due** = ordinary × (1 + r) = 41,95,685 × 1.0075 = **Rs 42,27,153**.

- **A) Rs 28,80,000** - wrong; this is the total contributed (30,000 × 96) with no growth.
- **B) Rs 39,70,251** - wrong; this lumps the year's investment into a single Rs 3,60,000 annual payment at 9%, losing the monthly compounding.
- **C) Rs 41,95,685** - wrong; this is the **ordinary** annuity value. Beginning-of-period payments each earn one extra month, so multiply by (1 + r).

</details>

**Q12.** A trust deed requires a perpetual annual grant of Rs 9,00,000, the first payment falling one year from now and each subsequent payment rising 4% a year. At a discount rate of 9%, what corpus is required today?
A) Rs 69,23,077  B) Rs 1,00,00,000  C) Rs 1,80,00,000  D) Rs 2,25,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 1,80,00,000**

Growing perpetuity: PV = PMT₁ ÷ (r − g) = 9,00,000 ÷ (0.09 − 0.04) = 9,00,000 ÷ 0.05 = **Rs 1,80,00,000**.

- **A) Rs 69,23,077** - wrong; this divides by (r + g) = 0.13 instead of (r − g).
- **B) Rs 1,00,00,000** - wrong; this is a *level* perpetuity, 9,00,000 ÷ 0.09, ignoring the 4% growth.
- **D) Rs 2,25,00,000** - wrong; this divides by g alone (0.04) instead of by (r − g).

</details>

**Q13.** Yusuf Merchant's monthly household income is Rs 2,15,000 and his monthly expenses are Rs 1,54,800. What is his savings ratio?
A) 20.00%  B) 28.00%  C) 38.89%  D) 72.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 28.00%**

Savings = 2,15,000 − 1,54,800 = **Rs 60,200**.
Savings ratio = Savings ÷ Gross income = 60,200 ÷ 2,15,000 = **28.00%**.
Cross-check: expenses ratio = 1,54,800 ÷ 2,15,000 = 72%, and 28 + 72 = 100 ✓.

- **A) 20.00%** - wrong; that is the *healthy benchmark* for a savings ratio, not Yusuf's actual figure.
- **C) 38.89%** - wrong; this divides savings by expenses instead of by gross income.
- **D) 72.00%** - wrong; that is the expenses ratio, the complement of the answer.

</details>

**Q14.** Faizal's assets include a savings account balance of Rs 3,80,000, a liquid fund holding of Rs 5,20,000, equity mutual funds of Rs 22,00,000 and a PPF balance of Rs 12,00,000. His monthly household expenses are Rs 1,50,000. What is his liquidity ratio?
A) 0.50 months  B) 6.00 months  C) 14.00 months  D) 28.67 months
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 6.00 months**

Liquid assets = savings balance + liquid fund = 3,80,000 + 5,20,000 = **Rs 9,00,000**. Equity mutual funds (volatile) and PPF (locked in) do **not** qualify as liquid assets.
Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets ÷ **Monthly** expenses = 9,00,000 ÷ 1,50,000 = **6.00 months**, at the top of the healthy 3–6 month band.

- **A) 0.50 months** - wrong; this divides by *annual* expenses (1,50,000 × 12).
- **C) 14.00 months** - wrong; this wrongly counts the PPF balance as a liquid asset.
- **D) 28.67 months** - wrong; this counts both the equity mutual funds and the PPF as liquid.

**Q17.** Nandita's monthly budget balances almost exactly every month, yet twice a year she has to fall back on her credit card. Those are the months in which her annual motor insurance premium, the school admission fee and Onam expenses fall due. What is the most likely defect in her budget?
A) Her savings ratio has fallen below the 20% benchmark  B) She has not provided for periodic, lumpy outflows by setting aside one-twelfth of each such item every month  C) Her liquidity ratio exceeds six months, so too much is held in cash  D) Her fixed outflows exceed her variable outflows
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) She has not provided for periodic, lumpy outflows by setting aside one-twelfth of each such item every month**

**Periodic (lumpy) outflows** — annual premiums, festivals, school fees, vacations, car servicing — are the most commonly forgotten category. A budget that balances every ordinary month collapses in the month they land. The fix is to divide each annual item by 12 and set that amount aside monthly.

- **A) Savings ratio below 20%** - wrong; the symptom described is a *timing* mismatch, not a chronic shortage of surplus.
- **C) Liquidity ratio above six months** - wrong; excess liquidity would *prevent* this problem, not cause it.
- **D) Fixed exceeding variable outflows** - wrong; the relative size of the two categories does not by itself create a mid-year cash crunch.

</details>

**Q18.** Faizal takes a home loan of Rs 62,00,000 at 8.65% p.a. for 18 years. What is his EMI?
A) Rs 28,704  B) Rs 44,692  C) Rs 54,395  D) Rs 56,712
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 56,712**

r = 0.0865 ÷ 12 = **0.00720833**; n = 18 × 12 = **216**; (1.00720833)^216 = **4.71814**.
EMI = P × r(1+r)^n ÷ [(1+r)^n − 1] = 62,00,000 × (0.00720833 × 4.71814) ÷ 3.71814 = **Rs 56,712**.

- **A) Rs 28,704** - wrong; this is simply principal ÷ number of months, with no interest at all.
- **B) Rs 44,692** - wrong; this is the *first month's interest* (62,00,000 × 0.00720833), a standard distractor.
- **C) Rs 54,395** - wrong; this is the EMI over a **20**-year tenure, not 18.

</details>

**Q21.** Faizal has three secured borrowings: a home loan against his Kochi flat, a gold loan where the ornaments are deposited with the lender, and a car loan where the car stays with him. In that order, the securities created are:
A) Mortgage, pledge, hypothecation  B) Hypothecation, mortgage, pledge  C) Pledge, hypothecation, mortgage  D) Mortgage, hypothecation, pledge
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Mortgage, pledge, hypothecation**

- **Mortgage** — security over **immovable** property (the flat).
- **Pledge** — security over **movable** property where **possession passes to the lender** (the gold).
- **Hypothecation** — security over **movable** property where **possession stays with the borrower** (the car).

- **B) Hypothecation, mortgage, pledge** - wrong; a flat is immovable, so it can only be mortgaged.
- **C) Pledge, hypothecation, mortgage** - wrong; the order is entirely reversed.
- **D) Mortgage, hypothecation, pledge** - wrong; the gold and the car are swapped. Possession of the gold passes to the lender, which makes it a pledge.

</details>

**Q22.** Kabir Sheth owes Rs 2,10,000 on a credit card at 42% p.a., Rs 6,00,000 on a personal loan at 15% p.a. and Rs 3,20,000 on a car loan at 10% p.a. He wants to minimise the total interest he pays. Which approach should the adviser recommend?
A) Snowball — clear the car loan first because it is the smallest  B) Avalanche — clear the credit card first because it carries the highest rate  C) Snowball — clear the credit card first because it is the smallest  D) Blizzard — clear the personal loan first because it is the largest
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Avalanche — clear the credit card first because it carries the highest rate**

The **avalanche** method attacks the **highest interest rate** first while paying minimums on the rest. It is **mathematically optimal**, minimising total interest — exactly the stated objective. Here the credit card at 42% is both the costliest and, happily, the smallest.

- **A) Snowball, car loan first** - wrong; the car loan at Rs 3,20,000 is not the smallest balance, and snowball does not minimise interest.
- **C) Snowball, credit card first** - wrong outcome label; the credit card *is* the smallest here, but the reason given for a total-interest objective must be the rate, which makes this the avalanche.
- **D) Blizzard, personal loan first** - wrong; blizzard clears one or two *small* balances for momentum before switching to the highest rate, and it does not target the largest loan.

</details>

**Correct: A) 1.76 pp**

- **C) 4.10 pp** - wrong; this overstates the benefit and corresponds to a strongly negative correlation, which was not given.

**Correct: C) Expected return is unchanged; standard deviation falls**

Portfolio return is a **pure weighted average** of the component returns — correlation does not appear in the formula at all. Correlation appears only in the **third (interaction) term** of the risk formula, so as it falls, portfolio variance falls and the standard deviation drops below the weighted average of the individual standard deviations.

- **A) Both fall** - wrong; adjusting the expected return for correlation is the classic error. Return stays at the weighted average whatever the correlation.
- **B) Expected return falls; standard deviation is unchanged** - wrong on both limbs; it reverses the actual relationship.

A **CTR** is triggered by all cash transactions of **more than Rs 10 lakh**, and by a **series of integrally connected cash transactions** within a month aggregating to more than that. It must reach the Director, FIU-IND by the **15th day of the succeeding month**.

- **A) A Suspicious Transaction Report, within 7 working days** - wrong; an STR is triggered by **suspicion, with no monetary threshold**, and the 7-working-day clock runs from the conclusion that the transaction is suspicious. Crossing Rs 10 lakh in cash is not by itself suspicion.
- **C) A Counterfeit Currency Report, within 7 working days** - wrong; a CCR arises only where **forged or counterfeit currency** was used, and it too is due by the 15th of the succeeding month.
- **D) A Cash Transaction Report, within 3 working days** - wrong report deadline; the CTR timeline is the 15th of the following month.

**Correct: B) USD 2,50,000**

Under FEMA's **Liberalised Remittance Scheme**, a **resident individual** may remit up to **USD 2,50,000 per financial year** for permissible current and capital account transactions, including overseas securities, property and education. Remittances above a prescribed annual threshold attract TCS, at concessional rates for education and medical purposes.

- **C) USD 10,00,000** - wrong; **USD 1 million per financial year** is the ceiling on repatriation of **NRO balances** by a non-resident — a different rule for a different person.

**Q81.** An adviser sells a 63-year-old client, who retires in two years and needs income, a single-premium product with a 15-year lock-in that pays the adviser a large commission. The client's risk profile on file is conservative. This is best described as:
A) Churning  B) Mis-selling  C) Front-running  D) Insider trading
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Mis-selling**

**Mis-selling** is recommending a product that does **not suit** the client's needs, horizon or risk profile, typically because it pays the adviser more. The give-away is that the product is right for the **seller**, not the buyer. A single transaction can be mis-selling — no pattern is needed.

- **A) Churning** - wrong; churning is **excessive trading or switching** to generate repeated commission. It is about frequency, and there is only one transaction here.
- **C) Front-running** - wrong; front-running means dealing **ahead of a client's known impending order** to profit from the price move it will cause.
- **D) Insider trading** - wrong; insider trading means dealing on **unpublished price-sensitive information about a company**, which does not arise here.

**Q82.** An adviser identifies a conflict of interest that could be removed altogether by declining a particular arrangement. What does the ethical hierarchy require?
A) Disclose it in writing and continue, since disclosure cures every conflict  B) Avoid it first; manage it if it cannot be avoided; disclose only what remains  C) Report it to SEBI and await instructions before acting  D) Ignore it if no rule expressly prohibits the arrangement
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Avoid it first; manage it if it cannot be avoided; disclose only what remains**

The Code of Conduct requires an adviser to **avoid** conflicts as far as possible, and where they are unavoidable, to **manage** them and **disclose** them so that clients are fairly treated. The order matters: **avoid, manage, disclose**. Disclosure is the fallback for what cannot be eliminated, not a licence to keep an avoidable conflict.

- **A) Disclose it in writing and continue, since disclosure cures every conflict** - wrong; disclosure is the last resort. A conflict that can simply be removed should be removed.
- **C) Report it to SEBI and await instructions before acting** - wrong; SEBI does not pre-clear individual commercial arrangements. The duty is the adviser's own.
- **D) Ignore it if no rule expressly prohibits the arrangement** - wrong; this is the "is it permitted?" mindset. Ethics asks "is it right?", and Regulation 15(1) requires disclosure of **all** conflicts as and when they arise.

**Q83.** What is the essential difference between the **fiduciary standard** applying to an investment adviser and the **suitability standard** applying to a distributor?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) The fiduciary asks whether the product is the best available for the client; the suitability standard asks whether it is not unsuitable**

A fiduciary must place the client's interest **above his own** and recommend the **best** option, monitor it over time, and take his fee from the client. A distributor operating on a suitability standard need only ensure the product is **not unsuitable**, may legitimately earn a commission from the manufacturer, and owes a largely point-of-sale duty.

- **B) The fiduciary standard applies only to non-individual advisers** - wrong; Regulation 15(1) applies to **every** registered investment adviser, individual or not.
- **C) The suitability standard is the higher of the two, because it requires risk profiling** - wrong; the fiduciary standard is the higher one, and it includes profiling and suitability obligations of its own under Regulations 16 and 17.
- **D) There is no practical difference; both require the client's interest to rank first** - wrong; only the fiduciary standard subordinates the adviser's interest to the client's.

</details>

Borealis = (13.1 − 6.4) ÷ 9.6 = 6.7 ÷ 9.6 = **0.70**
Cygnus = (17.4 − 6.4) ÷ 19.8 = 11.0 ÷ 19.8 = **0.56**
Borealis wins on total risk despite the lowest raw return.

- **A) Arcturus, 0.65** - wrong; the value is right for Arcturus but it ranks second, not first.
- **C) Cygnus, 0.56** - wrong; the value is right for Cygnus but it ranks last — the highest return here buys the worst return per unit of total risk.
- **D) Cygnus, 0.88** - wrong; 17.4 ÷ 19.8 forgets to deduct the risk-free rate from the return.

</details>

**Q127.** Ranked by the Treynor ratio, which manager comes **second**, and what is that manager's Treynor ratio?

- **A) Arcturus, 7.80%** - wrong; the value is right but Arcturus ranks third on Treynor.
- **B) Borealis, 8.17%** - wrong; Borealis ranks first, not second.

**Q128.** Which manager generated the highest Jensen's alpha, and how large was it?
A) Arcturus, 1.65%  B) Borealis, 1.45%  C) Cygnus, 2.36%  D) Cygnus, 4.60%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Cygnus, 2.36%**

Market risk premium = 12.8 − 6.4 = **6.4%**. Alpha = Rp − [Rf + β(Rm − Rf)]:
Arcturus: required = 6.4 + 1.18 × 6.4 = 13.95% → alpha = 15.6 − 13.95 = **1.65%**
Borealis: required = 6.4 + 0.82 × 6.4 = 11.65% → alpha = 13.1 − 11.65 = **1.45%**
Cygnus: required = 6.4 + 1.35 × 6.4 = 15.04% → alpha = 17.4 − 15.04 = **2.36%**

- **A) Arcturus, 1.65%** - wrong; correct value, second place.
- **B) Borealis, 1.45%** - wrong; correct value, third place — Borealis wins on Sharpe and Treynor but not on alpha.
- **D) Cygnus, 4.60%** - wrong; 17.4 − 12.8 is simple outperformance of the market, with no adjustment for Cygnus's beta of 1.35.

</details>

**Q129.** What is the standard deviation of the 65:35 Borealis–Cygnus blend, and how much risk does diversification remove relative to the weighted average of the two standard deviations?
A) 11.10%, removing 2.07 percentage points  B) 13.17%, removing nothing  C) 11.10%, removing 8.70 percentage points  D) 14.70%, removing 1.20 percentage points
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 11.10%, removing 2.07 percentage points**

σp = √(w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂σ₁σ₂ρ)
= √(0.65² × 9.6² + 0.35² × 19.8² + 2 × 0.65 × 0.35 × 9.6 × 19.8 × 0.42)
= √(38.94 + 48.02 + 36.32) = √123.29 = **11.10%**.
Weighted average σ = 0.65 × 9.6 + 0.35 × 19.8 = 6.24 + 6.93 = **13.17%**.
Diversification benefit = 13.17 − 11.10 = **2.07 percentage points**, obtained purely because ρ = 0.42 < 1.

- **B) 13.17%, removing nothing** - wrong; that is the weighted average, which would be the answer only if ρ were exactly +1.
- **C) 11.10%, removing 8.70 percentage points** - wrong; the σp is right, but 8.70 measures the fall from Cygnus's own 19.8% standard deviation, not from the 13.17% weighted average of the two.
- **D) 14.70%, removing 1.20 percentage points** - wrong; portfolio risk can never exceed the weighted average when ρ < 1.

</details>

**Q130.** Does the 65:35 blend beat the best single manager on a Sharpe basis?
A) Yes; the blend's Sharpe is 0.74 against Borealis's 0.70  B) No; the blend's Sharpe is 0.66 against Borealis's 0.70  C) Yes; the blend's Sharpe is 1.32 against Borealis's 0.70  D) No; a blend can never have a higher Sharpe ratio than its best component

**Correct: A) Yes; the blend's Sharpe is 0.74 against Borealis's 0.70**

Blend return = 0.65 × 13.1 + 0.35 × 17.4 = 8.52 + 6.09 = **14.61%** (returns combine as a simple weighted average). Blend σ from Q129 = **11.10%**.
Sharpe = (14.61 − 6.4) ÷ 11.10 = 8.21 ÷ 11.10 = **0.74**, against Borealis's 0.70. The blend picks up Cygnus's higher return while the imperfect correlation stops risk rising proportionately.

- **B)** - wrong; 0.66 arises from using the weighted-average σ of 13.17% rather than the true portfolio σ of 11.10% — precisely the mistake that hides the diversification benefit.
- **C)** - wrong; 1.32 omits the risk-free rate from the numerator (14.61 ÷ 11.10).

### Case 9: Rajiv Bhandari's debt and derivatives sleeve

Rajiv Bhandari holds two bonds and runs a small derivatives book on the Nifty 50. **Bond A** has a face value of **Rs 1,000**, pays a **9% annual coupon**, has **6 years to maturity**, and the market's required yield on it is **10.5%**. **Bond B** is a **zero-coupon bond of Rs 1,00,000 face value maturing in exactly 7 years**, priced to yield **7.8% a year**. On the derivatives side Rajiv has **bought 2 lots of the Nifty 26,500 call at a premium of Rs 168** and has **shorted 3 lots of Nifty futures at 26,720**. The **Nifty lot size is 25**. At expiry the Nifty settles at **26,940**.

**Q131.** What is the fair price of Bond A, and does it trade at a premium or a discount?
A) Rs 935.62, a discount  B) Rs 935.62, a premium  C) Rs 1,000.00, at par  D) Rs 1,064.42, a premium
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Rs 935.62, a discount**

A bond is an annuity of coupons plus a lump sum at redemption.
Annuity factor = [1 − (1.105)^−6] ÷ 0.105 = **4.29218**; PV of coupons = 90 × 4.29218 = **Rs 386.30**.
(1.105)^6 = 1.82042, so PV of redemption = 1,000 ÷ 1.82042 = **Rs 549.32**.
Price = 386.30 + 549.32 = **Rs 935.62**. The coupon of 9% is below the required yield of 10.5%, so the bond must trade at a **discount** to par.

- **B) Rs 935.62, a premium** - wrong; the price is right but a price below Rs 1,000 is by definition a discount.
- **C) Rs 1,000.00, at par** - wrong; par pricing happens only when coupon equals required yield.
- **D) Rs 1,064.42, a premium** - wrong; this is what the bond would be worth if the required yield were below the 9% coupon, i.e. the direction reversed.

</details>

**Q132.** What is Bond A's current yield, and how does the ordering of coupon, current yield and YTM run for this bond?
A) 9.62%; coupon < current yield < YTM  B) 9.62%; coupon > current yield > YTM  C) 9.00%; all three are equal  D) 10.50%; coupon < YTM < current yield
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 9.62%; coupon < current yield < YTM**

Current yield = Annual coupon ÷ Market price = 90 ÷ 935.62 = **9.62%**. For a discount bond the required ordering is **coupon (9.00%) < current yield (9.62%) < YTM (10.50%)**, because the current yield captures only the coupon income while the YTM also captures the Rs 64.38 pull to par over six years.

- **B)** - wrong; the value is right but the ordering is the *premium* bond's ordering, which applies when the coupon exceeds the yield.
- **C)** - wrong; 9.00% is the coupon rate on face value; the three measures coincide only at par.
- **D)** - wrong; 10.50% is the required yield (YTM), and current yield can never exceed YTM on a discount bond.

</details>

**Q133.** Bond B's yield rises by 50 basis points immediately after purchase. Using modified duration, what is its approximate new price?
A) Rs 55,273  B) Rs 57,192  C) Rs 59,111  D) Rs 61,030
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 57,192**

**Step 1 — price.** A zero-coupon bond is priced as P = F ÷ (1+y)ⁿ = 1,00,000 ÷ (1.078)^7 = 1,00,000 ÷ 1.69173 = **Rs 59,111**.
**Step 2 — duration.** A zero's Macaulay duration equals its maturity, so 7 years; modified duration = 7 ÷ 1.078 = **6.49**.
**Step 3 — the price move.** %ΔP ≈ −6.49 × 0.0050 = **−3.25%**, i.e. a fall of 59,111 × 3.25% = **Rs 1,919**. New price ≈ 59,111 − 1,919 = **Rs 57,192**.

- **A) Rs 55,273** - wrong; applies a 100 bp shift instead of the stated 50 bp.
- **C) Rs 59,111** - wrong; that is the price before the yield move.
- **D) Rs 61,030** - wrong; adds the Rs 1,919 instead of subtracting it. Prices fall when yields rise.

</details>

**Q134.** What is Rajiv's net profit or loss on the long call position at expiry, and where was its break-even?
A) Profit Rs 13,600; break-even 26,668  B) Profit Rs 22,000; break-even 26,500  C) Loss Rs 8,400; break-even 26,332  D) Profit Rs 6,800; break-even 26,668
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Profit Rs 13,600; break-even 26,668**

Intrinsic value at expiry = max(Spot − Strike, 0) = max(26,940 − 26,500, 0) = **440 points**. Net gain per unit = 440 − 168 premium = **272 points**. Quantity = 2 lots × 25 = **50 units**. Profit = 272 × 50 = **Rs 13,600**. Break-even on a long call = Strike + Premium = 26,500 + 168 = **26,668**.

- **B)** - wrong; Rs 22,000 counts the 440 points of intrinsic value on 50 units without deducting the premium paid, and the strike is not the break-even.
- **C)** - wrong; the option finished 440 points in the money, well beyond the 168-point premium, so it is profitable. 26,332 is the *put* break-even formula (Strike − Premium).
- **D)** - wrong; Rs 6,800 applies the 272-point gain to one lot of 25 rather than two lots of 25.

</details>

**Q135.** Taking the futures and the option together, what is Rajiv's overall result on the derivatives book at expiry?
A) A net loss of Rs 2,900  B) A net profit of Rs 2,900  C) A net loss of Rs 16,500  D) A net profit of Rs 30,100

**Futures leg.** He is **short** 3 lots at 26,720 and the index settled at 26,940, so the market moved against him by 220 points. Loss = 220 × 25 × 3 = **Rs 16,500**.
**Option leg.** From Q134, a gain of **Rs 13,600**.

- **B) A net profit of Rs 2,900** - wrong; reverses the sign. A short futures position loses when the index rises.
- **C) A net loss of Rs 16,500** - wrong; that is the futures loss alone, ignoring the offsetting option gain.
- **D) A net profit of Rs 30,100** - wrong; adds the two legs instead of netting them, treating the short futures loss as a gain.

</details>

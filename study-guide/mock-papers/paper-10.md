**Q1.** Which sequence correctly states the first four steps of the financial planning process?
A) Data gathering → Establishing the relationship → Defining goals → Risk profiling  B) Establishing the relationship → Data gathering → Defining goals → Risk profiling  C) Defining goals → Establishing the relationship → Data gathering → Risk profiling  D) Establishing the relationship → Defining goals → Data gathering → Risk profiling
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Establishing the relationship → Data gathering → Defining goals → Risk profiling**

The six steps run: (1) establish the client–planner relationship, (2) gather data on the client's financial situation, (3) define needs and financial goals, (4) risk profiling and asset allocation, (5) portfolio construction, (6) review and rebalancing. The engagement terms must be settled **in writing before** any data is collected.

- **A) Data gathering → Establishing the relationship → Defining goals → Risk profiling** - wrong; you cannot collect a client's confidential financial data before the scope, fees and responsibilities are agreed.
- **C) Defining goals → Establishing the relationship → Data gathering → Risk profiling** - wrong; goals are quantified only after the current position is known.
- **D) Establishing the relationship → Defining goals → Data gathering → Risk profiling** - wrong; this inverts steps 2 and 3, so goals would be set with no knowledge of income, assets or liabilities.

</details>

**Q2.** Sohom is 26, works in a stable IT role in Salt Lake, Kolkata, has no dependants and is investing for a goal 24 years away — but he redeemed his entire equity fund after an 8% market fall last quarter. How should his adviser characterise him?
A) High risk capacity and high risk tolerance  B) Low risk capacity and low risk tolerance  C) High risk capacity but low risk tolerance  D) Low risk capacity but high risk tolerance
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) High risk capacity but low risk tolerance**

**Risk capacity (ability)** is objective — a long 24-year horizon, stable income and no dependants all point to a **high** capacity. **Risk tolerance (willingness)** is psychological, and panic-selling on an 8% fall reveals a **low** tolerance. Where the two conflict, the plan is built to the **lower** of the two, because a client who exits at the bottom converts a paper loss into a permanent one.

- **A) High risk capacity and high risk tolerance** - wrong; his behaviour on an 8% drawdown contradicts high tolerance.
- **B) Low risk capacity and low risk tolerance** - wrong; a 24-year horizon with stable income and no dependants is textbook high capacity.
- **D) Low risk capacity but high risk tolerance** - wrong; this reverses both assessments.

</details>

**Q3.** Ishani, a 26-year-old first-time investor in Kolkata, holds: savings balance Rs 64,000, liquid fund Rs 1,36,000, equity mutual funds Rs 4,12,000, EPF Rs 3,18,000, gold Rs 2,70,000 and a two-wheeler worth Rs 84,000. Her education loan outstanding is Rs 3,90,000 and her credit card outstanding is Rs 47,000. What is her net worth?

Net worth = 12,84,000 − 4,37,000 = **Rs 8,47,000**. All assets, including personal-use assets such as the two-wheeler, are listed at current market value; liabilities are taken at outstanding principal.

- **A) Rs 7,63,000** - wrong; this drops the two-wheeler from the asset side, but personal-use assets still belong on the balance sheet.
- **C) Rs 8,94,000** - wrong; this ignores the Rs 47,000 credit card outstanding.
- **D) Rs 12,84,000** - wrong; this is total assets, not net worth.

**Q5.** Which of the following is framed as a properly specified financial goal?
A) Build a large enough retirement corpus  B) Beat the Nifty 50 over the long term  C) Accumulate Rs 34 lakh by April 2039 for a postgraduate degree  D) Save aggressively so that money is never a worry
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Accumulate Rs 34 lakh by April 2039 for a postgraduate degree**

A good goal is **specific, quantified in future (inflation-adjusted) rupees, and dated**, so that a required corpus and a monthly contribution can actually be computed. Option C supplies purpose, amount and date.

- **A) Build a large enough retirement corpus** - wrong; "large enough" is neither quantified nor dated, so no SIP can be derived from it.
- **B) Beat the Nifty 50 over the long term** - wrong; this is a product/benchmark objective, not a life goal. A portfolio can beat its benchmark and still fail to fund a goal.
- **D) Save aggressively so that money is never a worry** - wrong; an aspiration with no amount, no date and no cost attached.

**Q6.** Piyali invests a lump sum of Rs 2,75,000 today in an instrument expected to compound at 11.5% p.a. What is its value after 9 years?
A) Rs 5,59,625  B) Rs 6,56,949  C) Rs 7,32,498  D) Rs 8,16,735
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 7,32,498**

FV = PV × (1 + r)^n = 2,75,000 × (1.115)^9. (1.115)^9 = 2.66363, so FV = 2,75,000 × 2.66363 = **Rs 7,32,498**.

- **A) Rs 5,59,625** - wrong; this applies simple interest, 2,75,000 × (1 + 0.115 × 9), ignoring compounding entirely.
- **B) Rs 6,56,949** - wrong; this compounds for only 8 years.
- **D) Rs 8,16,735** - wrong; this compounds for 10 years.

</details>

**Q7.** An investment of Rs 3,20,000 made by Kaustav grew to Rs 8,60,000 over exactly 7 years. What is the CAGR?
A) 12.32%  B) 15.17%  C) 18.40%  D) 24.11%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 15.17%**

CAGR = (FV ÷ PV)^(1/n) − 1 = (8,60,000 ÷ 3,20,000)^(1/7) − 1 = (2.6875)^0.142857 − 1 = 1.15169 − 1 = **15.17% p.a.**

- **A) 12.32%** - wrong; this understates the rate and does not satisfy 3,20,000 × (1+r)^7 = 8,60,000.
- **C) 18.40%** - wrong; overstates the rate; 3,20,000 compounded at 18.40% for 7 years exceeds Rs 10.5 lakh.
- **D) 24.11%** - wrong; this is simple-interest thinking — total gain of 168.75% divided by 7 years — which ignores compounding.

</details>

**Q8.** Debolina starts a SIP of Rs 6,500 a month for 12 years, with returns expected at 13% p.a. compounded monthly. What corpus does she accumulate?
A) Rs 9,36,000  B) Rs 20,00,714  C) Rs 22,31,439  D) Rs 22,55,612
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 22,31,439**

r = 0.13 ÷ 12 = 0.0108333; n = 12 × 12 = 144; (1.0108333)^144 = 4.71906.
FV = 6,500 × [(4.71906 − 1) ÷ 0.0108333] = 6,500 × 343.298 = **Rs 22,31,439**.

- **A) Rs 9,36,000** - wrong; this is merely the total contributed (6,500 × 144), with no return at all.
- **B) Rs 20,00,714** - wrong; this treats the SIP as one annual contribution of Rs 78,000 compounded annually, losing the benefit of monthly compounding.
- **D) Rs 22,55,612** - wrong; this is the annuity-**due** value (ordinary × 1.0108333), which applies only if instalments are paid at the beginning of each month.

</details>

**Q9.** A deposit offers Rana a nominal return of 10.4% p.a. while inflation runs at 5.8%. What is the real return?
A) 4.35%  B) 4.60%  C) 4.85%  D) 5.02%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 4.35%**

Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1 = (1.104 ÷ 1.058) − 1 = 1.04348 − 1 = **4.35%**.

- **B) 4.60%** - wrong; this is the crude subtraction 10.4 − 5.8, which always **overstates** the real return.
- **C) 4.85%** - wrong; no valid combination of the two rates produces this.
- **D) 5.02%** - wrong; this multiplies rather than divides through by inflation.

**Q10.** A bank quotes a nominal rate of 9.6% p.a. compounded quarterly. What is the effective annual rate?
A) 9.60%  B) 9.83%  C) 9.95%  D) 10.03%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 9.95%**

EAR = (1 + r/m)^m − 1 = (1 + 0.096/4)^4 − 1 = (1.024)^4 − 1 = 1.09951 − 1 = **9.95%**.

- **A) 9.60%** - wrong; that is the nominal (stated) rate, which never changes with frequency.
- **B) 9.83%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **half-yearly** compounding.
- **D) 10.03%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **monthly** compounding, which is more frequent than quarterly.

**Q11.** A family trust will pay Rimjhim Rs 84,000 at the end of the coming year, and that payment will grow at 5% a year forever. At a discount rate of 9%, what is the present value of the stream?
A) Rs 6,00,000  B) Rs 9,33,333  C) Rs 16,80,000  D) Rs 21,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 21,00,000**

For a growing perpetuity, PV = PMT₁ ÷ (r − g) = 84,000 ÷ (0.09 − 0.05) = 84,000 ÷ 0.04 = **Rs 21,00,000**. The formula requires g < r, which holds here.

- **A) Rs 6,00,000** - wrong; this **adds** the growth rate to the discount rate instead of subtracting it.
- **B) Rs 9,33,333** - wrong; this is a level perpetuity, 84,000 ÷ 0.09, ignoring growth altogether.
- **C) Rs 16,80,000** - wrong; this divides by the growth rate alone, 84,000 ÷ 0.05.

</details>

Because payments are made at the beginning of each month, this is an **annuity due**: 3,26,679 × 1.01 = **Rs 3,29,945**.

- **B) Rs 3,26,679** - wrong; this is the ordinary-annuity value, which assumes end-of-month payments.
- **D) Rs 3,66,120** - wrong; this over-adjusts, effectively multiplying by more than (1 + r).

**Q15.** Anirban's total assets are Rs 23,40,000 and his total liabilities are Rs 8,89,200. What is his solvency ratio?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 0.62**

Net worth = 23,40,000 − 8,89,200 = Rs 14,50,800. Solvency ratio = Net worth ÷ Total assets = 14,50,800 ÷ 23,40,000 = **0.62**. Cross-check: leverage ratio = 8,89,200 ÷ 23,40,000 = 0.38, and 0.62 + 0.38 = 1. ✓

- **D) 1.62** - wrong; this divides total assets by net worth, inverting the ratio.

</details>

**Q16.** Which statement about a contingency (emergency) fund is correct?
A) It should be sized at 3–6 months of gross income  B) It should be sized at 3–6 months of expenses and held in liquid instruments  C) It is best held in a mid-cap equity fund to beat inflation  D) It is unnecessary once the client holds adequate health insurance
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) It should be sized at 3–6 months of expenses and held in liquid instruments**

The fund exists to meet obligations during job loss, illness or an unplanned expense, so it is sized against **expenses** — what must actually be paid — and parked where **immediate availability and certainty of value** are assured: savings account, sweep-in FD or liquid/overnight funds. Return is secondary.

- **A) It should be sized at 3–6 months of gross income** - wrong; the benchmark is expenses, not income; income overstates the requirement.
- **C) It is best held in a mid-cap equity fund to beat inflation** - wrong; the moment the fund is needed (a recession or job loss) is exactly when equity is likely to be down.
- **D) It is unnecessary once the client holds adequate health insurance** - wrong; insurance covers specified perils only, and does not fund a job loss, a deductible or a claim still under process.

</details>

**Q17.** A Kolkata household's monthly budget balances every month yet collapses each March. The single most likely cause is:
A) The savings ratio is above 20%  B) Fixed outflows exceed variable outflows  C) Periodic outflows such as annual insurance premiums were never provisioned monthly  D) The emergency fund is held in a sweep-in fixed deposit
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Periodic outflows such as annual insurance premiums were never provisioned monthly**

**Periodic (lumpy) outflows** — annual premiums, school admission fees, festival spending, vehicle servicing — are the most commonly forgotten category. The fix is to divide each annual amount by 12 and set that sum aside every month, so the lumpy month does not break the budget.

- **A) The savings ratio is above 20%** - wrong; a healthy savings ratio is a strength, not the cause of a March crunch.
- **B) Fixed outflows exceed variable outflows** - wrong; this describes the composition of spending, not a timing mismatch.
- **D) The emergency fund is held in a sweep-in fixed deposit** - wrong; a sweep-in FD is an appropriate, fully liquid home for the contingency fund.

</details>

**Q22.** Tanushree has three loans: Rs 42,000 at 38% (credit card), Rs 1,90,000 at 13% (personal loan) and Rs 6,20,000 at 9% (education loan). She clears the credit card first, then the personal loan, then the education loan. Which strategy is she following?
A) Snowball  B) Avalanche  C) Blizzard  D) Refinancing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Avalanche**

The **avalanche** method targets the **highest interest rate first** while paying minimums on the rest. Here the order 38% → 13% → 9% is strictly by descending rate, and it happens also to be ascending balance. Avalanche is **mathematically optimal** — it minimises total interest paid.

- **A) Snowball** - wrong; snowball targets the **smallest balance** first regardless of rate. Since the order here follows the rates exactly, avalanche is the strategy being described.
- **C) Blizzard** - wrong; blizzard is a hybrid — clear one or two small balances for momentum, then switch to attacking the highest rate.
- **D) Refinancing** - wrong; refinancing replaces an existing loan with a cheaper new one; no new loan is being taken here.

</details>

**Q28.** Which of the following correctly contrasts a rights issue with a bonus issue?
A) Both raise fresh capital for the company  B) A rights issue raises capital; a bonus issue does not  C) A bonus issue raises capital; a rights issue does not  D) Neither raises capital; both merely capitalise reserves
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) A rights issue raises capital; a bonus issue does not**

A **rights issue** offers existing shareholders the chance to **buy** more shares pro rata at a discount, so money flows into the company. A **bonus issue** gives free shares out of the **capitalisation of reserves** — no money is raised and the shareholder's total value is unchanged.

- **A) Both raise fresh capital for the company** - wrong; a bonus issue is a pure book entry converting reserves into share capital.
- **C) A bonus issue raises capital; a rights issue does not** - wrong; this reverses the two.
- **D) Neither raises capital; both merely capitalise reserves** - wrong; only the bonus issue capitalises reserves; a rights issue brings in cash.

</details>

**Q29.** Bidisha holds 400 shares of a company trading at Rs 950. The company announces a 3:5 bonus issue. What are her holding and the theoretical ex-bonus price?

**Q115.** How should the trail commission earned by Meghna's spouse from three of her advisory clients be treated?

- **C) Fund R, 0.71** - wrong; correctly computed, but Fund R ranks second.

**Q128.** Comparing Funds P and Q on Jensen's alpha, which is higher and by how much?

**Q130.** Fund P ranks last of the three on Sharpe but second on Treynor. What does that pattern indicate?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

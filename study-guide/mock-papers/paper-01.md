**Q1.** Keerthi Nagaraj, a 27-year-old software engineer in Bengaluru, lists the following on her personal balance sheet: savings account Rs 3,10,000; liquid fund Rs 1,40,000; equity mutual funds Rs 6,80,000; EPF balance Rs 4,20,000; car Rs 2,50,000. Her liabilities are a car loan outstanding of Rs 5,60,000 and a credit card outstanding of Rs 1,15,000. What is her net worth?
A) Rs 11,25,000  B) Rs 6,75,000  C) Rs 15,50,000  D) Rs 18,00,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Rs 11,25,000**

Total assets = 3,10,000 + 1,40,000 + 6,80,000 + 4,20,000 + 2,50,000 = **Rs 18,00,000**.
Total liabilities = 5,60,000 + 1,15,000 = **Rs 6,75,000**.
Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities = 18,00,000 − 6,75,000 = **Rs 11,25,000**.

- **B) Rs 6,75,000** - wrong; this is total liabilities, not net worth.
- **C) Rs 15,50,000** - wrong; this drops the car from assets but forgets to deduct liabilities altogether.
- **D) Rs 18,00,000** - wrong; this is total assets. Assets are not net worth — the liabilities must still be subtracted.

</details>

**Q2.** In the six-step financial planning process, which step immediately follows "defining client needs and financial goals"?
A) Portfolio construction  B) Risk profiling and asset allocation  C) Gathering data on the client's financial situation  D) Reviewing and rebalancing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Risk profiling and asset allocation**

The sequence is: (1) establish the client–planner relationship, (2) gather data on the client's financial situation, (3) define client needs and financial goals, (4) **risk profiling and asset allocation**, (5) portfolio construction, (6) reviewing and rebalancing. Goals must be defined before risk can be profiled, because the return the goals require is itself an input to the risk discussion.

- **A) Portfolio construction** - wrong; this is step 5. Products can only be chosen after the asset allocation is fixed.
- **C) Gathering data on the client's financial situation** - wrong; this is step 2, which comes *before* goals are defined.
- **D) Reviewing and rebalancing** - wrong; this is the final step, step 6.

</details>

**Q3.** Sandeep, aged 26, works in a stable product-engineering role in Bengaluru, has no dependants and a 30-year horizon for his retirement goal — but he tells his adviser that a 10% fall in his portfolio would keep him awake at night. How should the adviser set the asset allocation?
A) On risk capacity alone, since capacity is objective and measurable  B) On the simple average of capacity and tolerance  C) On the higher of risk capacity and risk tolerance  D) On the lower of risk capacity and risk tolerance
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) On the lower of risk capacity and risk tolerance**

Sandeep has **high risk capacity** (long horizon, stable income, no dependants — an objective assessment) but **low risk tolerance** (his subjective willingness to bear volatility). Where the two conflict, the plan is built to the **lower** of the two. An allocation his psychology cannot sustain will be abandoned in the first drawdown, converting a paper loss into a permanent one.

- **A) On risk capacity alone** - wrong; ignoring tolerance is exactly what produces panic-selling at the bottom.
- **B) On the simple average** - wrong; there is no averaging rule. The binding constraint is the lower of the two.
- **C) On the higher of risk capacity and risk tolerance** - wrong; a high return requirement does not override the client's ability to stay invested through a fall.

</details>

**Q4.** How does SEBI address the conflict of interest that arises when the person advising a client also earns commission on executing the recommendation?
A) By requiring advice and distribution to be segregated, so that an individual investment adviser cannot do both  B) By requiring only that the commission be disclosed to the client in writing  C) By permitting the adviser to retain the commission if it is lower than the advisory fee charged  D) By delegating supervision of the conflict to the stock exchange as first-level regulator
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) By requiring advice and distribution to be segregated, so that an individual investment adviser cannot do both**

The structural answer SEBI adopts is **segregation of advice and distribution**. A registered Investment Adviser must keep advisory activity separate from any distribution activity, and an **individual IA cannot carry on both**. The adviser owes a **fiduciary** duty — the client's interest ahead of the adviser's own — which mere disclosure cannot satisfy.

- **B) Disclosure alone** - wrong; disclosure is necessary but SEBI goes further and requires segregation.
- **C) Retaining commission if lower than the fee** - wrong; there is no such proportionality test in the regulations.
- **D) Exchange supervision** - wrong; exchanges act as first-level regulators of their *brokers*, not of investment advisers' compensation model.

</details>

**Q5.** An adviser notices that a client's home down-payment, a sibling's wedding contribution and a planned car purchase all fall in the same calendar year, and recommends deferring the car purchase by two years. Which goal-setting principle is being applied?
A) Staggering the timing of goals  B) Prioritising goals  C) Ensuring adequate focus on important goals  D) Quantifying goals in future rupees
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Staggering the timing of goals**

**Staggering** means moving discretionary goals forward or back so that several large funding requirements do not collide in a single year, which would break the plan's cash flow. That is precisely what deferring the car purchase achieves.

- **B) Prioritising goals** - wrong; prioritisation ranks goals by importance (retirement over a car upgrade). Here the car is not being dropped, only re-dated.
- **C) Ensuring adequate focus on important goals** - wrong; this principle addresses long-dated goals such as retirement being neglected because they feel far away.
- **D) Quantifying goals in future rupees** - wrong; that is about inflating today's cost to the goal date, not about timing.

</details>

**Q6.** Tejaswini receives Rs 3,40,000 from an ESOP exercise and invests the whole amount for 12 years in an instrument earning 11% p.a. compounded annually. What will it be worth at the end of 12 years?
A) Rs 7,88,800  B) Rs 11,89,473  C) Rs 10,71,597  D) Rs 13,20,315
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 11,89,473**

FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ = 3,40,000 × (1.11)¹² .
(1.11)¹² = 3.498451.
FV = 3,40,000 × 3.498451 = **Rs 11,89,473**.

- **A) Rs 7,88,800** - wrong; this is simple interest, 3,40,000 × (1 + 0.11 × 12). It ignores compounding entirely.
- **C) Rs 10,71,597** - wrong; this compounds for only 11 years.
- **D) Rs 13,20,315** - wrong; this compounds for 13 years.

</details>

**Q7.** An investment of Rs 2,80,000 made by Vikram nine years ago is worth Rs 7,70,000 today. What is the compound annual growth rate?
A) 10.64%  B) 13.48%  C) 19.44%  D) 11.90%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) 11.90%**

CAGR = (FV ÷ PV)^(1/n) − 1 = (7,70,000 ÷ 2,80,000)^(1/9) − 1 = (2.75)^0.1111 − 1 = 1.1190 − 1 = **11.90% p.a.**

- **A) 10.64%** - wrong; this uses n = 10 years instead of 9.
- **B) 13.48%** - wrong; this uses n = 8 years instead of 9.
- **C) 19.44%** - wrong; this is simple-interest thinking — total gain of 175% divided by 9 years. It ignores compounding.

</details>

**Q8.** Anusha starts a SIP of Rs 18,000 a month at the end of every month for 12 years in a fund expected to return 13% p.a. compounded monthly. What corpus does she accumulate?
A) Rs 25,92,000  B) Rs 55,40,438  C) Rs 62,46,312  D) Rs 61,79,368
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) Rs 61,79,368**

r = 0.13 ÷ 12 = 0.0108333; n = 12 × 12 = 144.
(1.0108333)¹⁴⁴ = 4.719064.
FV = 18,000 × [(4.719064 − 1) ÷ 0.0108333] = 18,000 × 343.298 = **Rs 61,79,368**.

- **A) Rs 25,92,000** - wrong; this is only the total contributed (18,000 × 144). It ignores all returns.
- **B) Rs 55,40,438** - wrong; this treats the SIP as an annual investment of Rs 2,16,000 compounded annually at 13%, losing the benefit of monthly compounding.
- **C) Rs 62,46,312** - wrong; this is the annuity *due* value (× 1.0108333), but the question specifies payment at the **end** of each month.

</details>

**Q9.** Rohit invests Rs 25,000 at the **beginning** of every month for 8 years in a scheme returning 10.5% p.a. compounded monthly. What is the maturity value?
A) Rs 24,00,000  B) Rs 37,69,610  C) Rs 37,36,912  D) Rs 41,28,288
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 37,69,610**

r = 0.105 ÷ 12 = 0.00875; n = 96.
(1.00875)⁹⁶ = 2.308119.
Ordinary annuity FV = 25,000 × [(2.308119 − 1) ÷ 0.00875] = 25,000 × 149.4765 = Rs 37,36,912.
Payments are at the **beginning** of each month, so this is an annuity **due**: 37,36,912 × 1.00875 = **Rs 37,69,610**.

- **A) Rs 24,00,000** - wrong; this is only the sum contributed (25,000 × 96).
- **C) Rs 37,36,912** - wrong; this is the ordinary (end-of-month) annuity value, missing the (1 + r) adjustment.
- **D) Rs 41,28,288** - wrong; this multiplies the ordinary annuity by (1 + annual rate) instead of (1 + monthly rate).

</details>

**Q10.** A debt fund is expected to return 10.4% p.a. while inflation runs at 5.8% p.a. What is the real rate of return?
A) 4.17%  B) 4.60%  C) 16.80%  D) 4.35%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) 4.35%**

Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1 = (1.104 ÷ 1.058) − 1 = 1.043478 − 1 = **4.35%**.

- **A) 4.17%** - wrong; this divides the difference by (1 + nominal) instead of (1 + inflation).
- **B) 4.60%** - wrong; this is the crude subtraction 10.4 − 5.8, which always overstates the real return.
- **C) 16.80%** - wrong; this multiplies (1.104 × 1.058) instead of dividing — it compounds inflation onto the return rather than removing it.

</details>

**Q11.** A bank quotes a nominal rate of 9.6% p.a. on a deposit, compounded quarterly. What is the effective annual rate?
A) 9.60%  B) 9.83%  C) 9.95%  D) 10.03%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 9.95%**

EAR = (1 + r/m)^m − 1 with r = 0.096 and m = 4.
= (1 + 0.024)⁴ − 1 = (1.024)⁴ − 1 = 1.099512 − 1 = **9.95%**.

- **A) 9.60%** - wrong; this is the nominal (stated) rate. The nominal rate never changes with frequency — only the effective rate does.
- **B) 9.83%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **half-yearly** compounding, (1.048)² − 1.
- **D) 10.03%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **monthly** compounding, (1.008)¹² − 1.

</details>

**Q12.** Deepika wants to accumulate Rs 60,00,000 in 14 years. Her portfolio is expected to earn 12% p.a. compounded monthly. What monthly investment, made at the end of each month, is required?
A) Rs 13,886  B) Rs 13,748  C) Rs 15,436  D) Rs 35,714
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Rs 13,886**

PMT = FV × r ÷ [(1 + r)ⁿ − 1], with r = 0.12 ÷ 12 = 0.01 and n = 14 × 12 = 168.
(1.01)¹⁶⁸ = 5.320970.
PMT = (60,00,000 × 0.01) ÷ (5.320970 − 1) = 60,000 ÷ 4.320970 = **Rs 13,886 a month**.

- **B) Rs 13,748** - wrong; this is the annuity **due** payment (divided by 1.01), but the question specifies end-of-month contributions.
- **C) Rs 15,436** - wrong; this solves for an annual contribution at 12% annual compounding and then divides by 12.
- **D) Rs 35,714** - wrong; this is simply 60,00,000 ÷ 168, assuming zero return on the contributions.

</details>

**Q13.** Nikhil's gross monthly income is Rs 1,45,000 and his total monthly expenses are Rs 98,600. What is his savings ratio?
A) 20.00%  B) 47.06%  C) 32.00%  D) 68.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 32.00%**

Savings (surplus) = 1,45,000 − 98,600 = Rs 46,400.
Savings ratio = Savings ÷ Gross income = 46,400 ÷ 1,45,000 = **32.00%**.
Self-check: savings ratio + expenses ratio = 32% + 68% = 100%. ✓

- **A) 20.00%** - wrong; 20% is the *healthy benchmark* for a savings ratio, not Nikhil's actual figure.
- **B) 47.06%** - wrong; this divides savings by expenses (46,400 ÷ 98,600) instead of by gross income.
- **D) 68.00%** - wrong; this is the expenses ratio, the complement of the savings ratio.

</details>

**Q14.** Shruti holds a savings account balance of Rs 1,35,000, a liquid fund of Rs 2,25,000, equity mutual funds of Rs 4,00,000 and a PPF balance of Rs 3,60,000. Her monthly household expenses are Rs 84,000. What is her liquidity ratio?
A) 0.36 months  B) 9.05 months  C) 13.33 months  D) 4.29 months
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) 4.29 months**

Liquid assets = savings balance + liquid fund = 1,35,000 + 2,25,000 = **Rs 3,60,000**. Equity mutual funds (volatile) and PPF (locked in) do **not** qualify as liquid assets.
Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets ÷ **Monthly** expenses = 3,60,000 ÷ 84,000 = **4.29 months**, which sits inside the healthy 3–6 month band.

- **A) 0.36 months** - wrong; this divides by *annual* expenses (84,000 × 12). The denominator must be monthly expenses.
- **B) 9.05 months** - wrong; this wrongly counts the equity mutual funds as liquid.
- **C) 13.33 months** - wrong; this counts both the equity funds and the PPF as liquid.

**Q15.** A client's personal balance sheet shows total assets of Rs 62,00,000 and total liabilities of Rs 23,25,000. What is the solvency ratio?
A) 0.375  B) 0.625  C) 0.600  D) 1.600

**Correct: B) 0.625**

Net worth = 62,00,000 − 23,25,000 = Rs 38,75,000.

**Q16.** A 26-year-old Bengaluru IT professional asks how to build a contingency fund. Which recommendation is correct?
A) 3–6 months of gross income, held in an ELSS fund  B) 12 months of expenses, held in PPF  C) 3–6 months of expenses, held in a mid-cap equity fund  D) 3–6 months of expenses, held in a savings account, sweep-in FD or liquid fund
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D) 3–6 months of expenses, held in a savings account, sweep-in FD or liquid fund**

The contingency fund is sized against **expenses** (what must actually be paid) at **3–6 months**, and must satisfy two requirements: **immediate availability** and **certainty of value**. Return is secondary.

- **A) Income-based, in an ELSS fund** - wrong on both counts: the fund is sized against expenses, and ELSS carries a three-year lock-in plus equity risk.
- **B) 12 months in PPF** - wrong; PPF is locked in for 15 years and cannot serve an emergency.
- **C) Mid-cap equity fund** - wrong; the moment the fund is needed (job loss, downturn) is exactly when equity is likely to be down.

</details>

**Q17.** A household's monthly budget balances comfortably every month, yet it collapses each March when the annual motor insurance and health insurance premiums fall due. Which category of outflow was mishandled?
A) Fixed/committed outflows  B) Periodic/lumpy outflows  C) Variable/discretionary outflows  D) Savings outflows
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Periodic/lumpy outflows**

**Periodic outflows** — annual premiums, festival spending, school admission fees, car servicing, vacations — are predictable but infrequent, and are the most commonly forgotten item in a household budget. The fix is to divide each annual amount by 12 and set that sum aside every month.

- **A) Fixed/committed outflows** - wrong; these are the *monthly* contractual items such as EMIs, rent and school fees, which the budget already handles.
- **C) Variable/discretionary outflows** - wrong; groceries, fuel and dining recur monthly and are controllable, not lumpy.
- **D) Savings outflows** - wrong; SIPs and recurring deposits are treated as committed monthly outflows, not annual surprises.

</details>

**Q18.** Karthik takes a home loan of Rs 32,00,000 at 8.65% p.a. for 18 years. What is his EMI?
A) Rs 14,815  B) Rs 29,270  C) Rs 23,067  D) Rs 31,794
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 29,270**

r = 0.0865 ÷ 12 = 0.00720833; n = 18 × 12 = 216.
(1.00720833)²¹⁶ = 4.718142.
EMI = 32,00,000 × [0.00720833 × 4.718142] ÷ (4.718142 − 1) = 32,00,000 × 0.0340135 ÷ 3.718142 = **Rs 29,270**.

- **A) Rs 14,815** - wrong; this is simply principal ÷ number of months (32,00,000 ÷ 216), ignoring interest.
- **C) Rs 23,067** - wrong; this is the **first month's interest** (32,00,000 × 0.00720833), a classic near-miss distractor.
- **D) Rs 31,794** - wrong; this is the EMI for a 15-year tenure, not 18 years.

</details>

**Q20.** Arjun takes a gold loan and, in the same month, a car loan. Which pair correctly describes the security created?
A) Gold loan — pledge; car loan — hypothecation  B) Gold loan — hypothecation; car loan — pledge  C) Gold loan — mortgage; car loan — pledge  D) Both are hypothecations
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Gold loan — pledge; car loan — hypothecation**

A **pledge** is security over **movable** property where **possession passes to the lender** — the lender physically holds the gold. A **hypothecation** is security over **movable** property where **possession stays with the borrower** — Arjun keeps driving the car. A **mortgage** is security over **immovable** property, such as a home loan.

- **B)** - wrong; the two are reversed. The lender does not keep the car, and it does hold the gold.
- **C)** - wrong; a mortgage requires immovable property, and gold is movable.
- **D)** - wrong; gold physically moves to the lender's custody, which makes it a pledge, not a hypothecation.

</details>

**Q21.** Which debt-repayment strategy clears the **smallest outstanding balance first**, regardless of the interest rate on each loan?
A) Avalanche  B) Snowball  C) Blizzard  D) Refinancing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Snowball**

The **snowball** method targets the **smallest balance** first while paying minimums on the rest. It is psychologically powerful because it produces quick wins, but it costs more in total interest than the mathematically optimal alternative.

- **A) Avalanche** - wrong; the avalanche attacks the **highest interest rate** first and minimises total interest paid.
- **C) Blizzard** - wrong; the blizzard is the hybrid — clear one or two small balances for momentum, then switch to attacking the highest rate.
- **D) Refinancing** - wrong; refinancing replaces an existing loan with a cheaper one; it is not an ordering strategy across multiple loans.

</details>

**Q25.** Which market infrastructure institution acts as the **central counterparty** and guarantees that a trade will settle even if a member defaults?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) The clearing corporation**

The **clearing corporation** clears and settles trades and interposes itself as the **central counterparty (CCP)** to every trade, so neither buyer nor seller faces the other's default. It manages margins and operates the settlement guarantee fund.

**Q28.** An investor holds 500 shares of a company quoted at Rs 920 each. The company announces a bonus issue in the ratio 3:5. What is the theoretical price per share immediately after the bonus?

Theoretical price = 4,60,000 ÷ 800 = **Rs 575**.

- **A) Rs 368** - wrong; this applies the ratio the wrong way round (920 × 2/5).
- **B) Rs 460** - wrong; this assumes a 1:1 bonus, halving the price.
- **C) Rs 552** - wrong; this reduces the price by 40% rather than dividing the unchanged value by the new share count.

**Q115.** Is her husband permitted to earn trail commission on the schemes Ananya recommends to her clients?
A) Yes, provided the commission is disclosed to the client in writing  B) Yes, provided Ananya charges the client under the AUA mode  C) No — the family of an individual investment adviser may not provide distribution services to the adviser's clients  D) Yes, provided the distribution business is run through a separately identifiable department or division
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) No — the family of an individual investment adviser may not provide distribution services to the adviser's clients**

Regulation 22 states that an individual IA shall not provide distribution services at all, and that the **family of the individual IA shall not provide distribution services to the IA's clients**. "Family of an individual IA" covers the adviser, spouse, children and parents. The arrangement is prohibited outright, not merely disclosable.

- **A)** - wrong; disclosure does not cure a prohibited arrangement.
- **B)** - wrong; the fee mode is irrelevant to the segregation requirement.

### Case 6: Shruti Balan's portfolio after a career change

Shruti Balan (34) is a UX lead in Bengaluru. Her Investment Policy Statement sets a strategic asset allocation of 70% equity, 25% debt and 5% gold, with a tolerance band of plus or minus 5 percentage points on equity. At the start of the year her corpus stood at Rs 40,00,000, invested Rs 28,00,000 in equity, Rs 10,00,000 in debt and Rs 2,00,000 in gold. **Q116.** What is the equity weight in Shruti's portfolio at the end of the year?

Gold = 2,00,000 × 1.18 = Rs 2,36,000
Total = Rs 48,34,000

- **C) 76.20%** - wrong; this omits gold from the denominator (35,28,000 ÷ 46,30,000).

**Q117.** Under a pure threshold (tolerance band) rule, what action is required at year end?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) No rebalancing is required**

Drift = 72.98% − 70.00% = 2.98 percentage points, which is **inside** the plus or minus 5 percentage point band. A threshold rule acts only when the band is breached, so nothing is done.

- **B)** - wrong; Rs 1,44,200 is the trade a *calendar* rule would require. A threshold rule asks a different question.
- **C)** - wrong; Rs 8,69,300 is the trade needed to move to the proposed new 55% target, which is a change of strategy, not a rebalancing trigger.
- **D)** - wrong; equity is overweight, not underweight, so buying more would worsen the drift.

**Correct: B) Rs 1,44,200**

Target equity = 70% × 48,34,000 = Rs 33,83,800.
Equity to sell = 35,28,000 − 33,83,800 = **Rs 1,44,200**.

- **A) Nil** - wrong; that is the threshold-rule answer. A calendar rule acts on the review date regardless of how small the drift is.
- **C) Rs 2,87,000** - wrong; this computes the target as 70% of equity plus debt only (70% × 46,30,000), leaving gold out of the base.
- **D) Rs 7,28,000** - wrong; this applies the 70% target to the *opening* corpus of Rs 40,00,000 rather than to the year-end value.

**Q119.** How should the proposed permanent cut in the equity target from 70% to 55% be classified?

**Correct: B) A change in the strategic asset allocation, requiring the IPS to be revised**

The trigger is a change in the **client's** circumstances — a move to variable start-up income and a dependant on the way — which reduces both her risk capacity and her liquidity headroom. - **A)** - wrong; a move to 55% is outside the 65–75% band the IPS permits, and tactical asset allocation is temporary and market-driven by definition.
- **C)** - wrong; rebalancing restores the *existing* targets; it never changes them.

### Case 7: Harish Bhat's goal plan and home purchase

Harish Bhat (30) is a DevOps engineer in Bengaluru earning Rs 1,55,000 a month gross, and already pays a car loan EMI of Rs 12,400. He wants to fund his daughter's undergraduate education, which costs Rs 22,00,000 at today's prices and is 15 years away; education costs are assumed to inflate at 8% a year. He has Rs 6,00,000 already earmarked for this goal, expected to grow at 11% a year. Any monthly SIP he starts will also be assumed to earn 11% a year compounded monthly. Separately, he wants to buy a flat costing Rs 95,00,000 with a 20% down payment, borrowing the balance at 8.75% a year for 20 years. His bank will sanction a loan only if his total EMIs stay within 45% of gross monthly income.

**Q121.** What is the shortfall Harish must fund through fresh savings for the education goal?
A) Rs 16,00,000  B) Rs 41,08,018  C) Rs 63,78,772  D) Rs 69,78,772
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 41,08,018**

Step 1 — inflate the goal: 22,00,000 × (1.08)^15 = 22,00,000 × 3.172169 = Rs 69,78,772.
Step 2 — grow what is already earmarked: 6,00,000 × (1.11)^15 = 6,00,000 × 4.784589 = Rs 28,70,754.
Step 3 — the gap: 69,78,772 − 28,70,754 = **Rs 41,08,018**.

- **A) Rs 16,00,000** - wrong; this is today's cost less today's earmarked corpus, ignoring both inflation and growth.
- **C) Rs 63,78,772** - wrong; this deducts the earmarked Rs 6,00,000 at its present value instead of letting it compound for 15 years.
- **D) Rs 69,78,772** - wrong; this is the inflated cost of the goal, before crediting the existing corpus.

</details>

**Q122.** What level monthly SIP, starting now, will close that shortfall in 15 years at 11% a year compounded monthly?
A) Rs 3,519  B) Rs 9,035  C) Rs 15,350  D) Rs 22,822
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 9,035**

Monthly rate r = 11% ÷ 12 = 0.0091667; n = 15 × 12 = 180.
(1.0091667)^180 = 5.167988, so the future-value annuity factor = (5.167988 − 1) ÷ 0.0091667 = 454.690.
PMT = 41,08,018 ÷ 454.690 = **Rs 9,035 a month**. In spreadsheet terms, `=ABS(PMT(11%/12, 180, 0, 4108018))`.

- **A) Rs 3,519** - wrong; this annuitises the un-inflated shortfall of Rs 16,00,000, so the goal would be badly underfunded.
- **C) Rs 15,350** - wrong; this funds the entire Rs 69,78,772 and gives no credit for the Rs 6,00,000 already earmarked.
- **D) Rs 22,822** - wrong; this is the shortfall divided by 180 months, ignoring investment returns altogether.

</details>

**Q123.** What EMI will the home loan carry?
A) Rs 31,667  B) Rs 55,417  C) Rs 67,162  D) Rs 83,953
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 67,162**

Down payment = 20% × 95,00,000 = Rs 19,00,000, so the loan is 95,00,000 − 19,00,000 = Rs 76,00,000.
r = 8.75% ÷ 12 = 0.0072917; n = 240; (1.0072917)^240 = 5.71815.
EMI = 76,00,000 × (0.0072917 × 5.71815) ÷ (5.71815 − 1) = 76,00,000 × 0.0088371 = **Rs 67,162**.

- **A) Rs 31,667** - wrong; this is the principal spread over 240 months with no interest at all (76,00,000 ÷ 240).
- **B) Rs 55,417** - wrong; this is the *first month's interest* (76,00,000 × 0.0072917), a classic near-miss distractor.
- **D) Rs 83,953** - wrong; this is the EMI on the full flat cost of Rs 95,00,000, ignoring the down payment.

</details>

**Q124.** How much interest will Harish pay over the full 20-year tenure of the home loan?

### Case 8: Ranking three fund managers

Aditya's adviser compares three equity funds over the same five-year period. Meridian Bluechip returned 15.2% a year with a standard deviation of 18.0% and a beta of 1.10. Kaveri Focused Equity returned 13.4% with a standard deviation of 12.5% and a beta of 0.85. Tungabhadra Multicap returned 16.8% with a standard deviation of 24.0% and a beta of 1.35. Over the same period the risk-free rate averaged 6.5% and the market returned 12.5% with a standard deviation of 15.0%. The adviser also models a two-fund blend of 65% Meridian Bluechip and 35% Kaveri Focused Equity; the correlation between the two funds' returns is 0.62.

**Q126.** Which fund ranks highest on the Sharpe ratio, and what is that ratio?
A) Meridian Bluechip, 0.48  B) Kaveri Focused Equity, 0.55  C) Tungabhadra Multicap, 0.43  D) Tungabhadra Multicap, 0.70
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Kaveri Focused Equity, 0.55**

Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) ÷ σp.
Meridian: (15.2 − 6.5) ÷ 18.0 = 8.7 ÷ 18.0 = 0.48
Kaveri: (13.4 − 6.5) ÷ 12.5 = 6.9 ÷ 12.5 = **0.55**
Tungabhadra: (16.8 − 6.5) ÷ 24.0 = 10.3 ÷ 24.0 = 0.43
Kaveri wins despite the lowest raw return, because it delivered the most excess return per unit of **total** risk.

- **A) Meridian Bluechip, 0.48** - wrong; the ratio is right for Meridian but it is not the highest.
- **C) Tungabhadra Multicap, 0.43** - wrong; the ratio is right for Tungabhadra but it is the *lowest* of the three. Highest return does not mean best risk-adjusted return.
- **D) Tungabhadra Multicap, 0.70** - wrong; 16.8 ÷ 24.0 omits the risk-free rate from the numerator.

</details>

**Q127.** Which fund ranks highest on the Treynor ratio, and what is that ratio?
A) Kaveri Focused Equity, 8.12  B) Meridian Bluechip, 7.91  C) Tungabhadra Multicap, 7.63  D) Tungabhadra Multicap, 12.44
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Kaveri Focused Equity, 8.12**

Treynor = (Rp − Rf) ÷ βp.
Meridian: 8.7 ÷ 1.10 = 7.91
Kaveri: 6.9 ÷ 0.85 = **8.12**
Tungabhadra: 10.3 ÷ 1.35 = 7.63
Kaveri ranks first on Treynor as well as on Sharpe. When the two measures agree, the funds are similarly diversified; the exam only makes them disagree when it wants you to spot a poorly diversified portfolio.

- **B) Meridian Bluechip, 7.91** - wrong; correct arithmetic for Meridian, but it ranks second.
- **C) Tungabhadra Multicap, 7.63** - wrong; correct arithmetic for Tungabhadra, but it ranks last.
- **D) Tungabhadra Multicap, 12.44** - wrong; 16.8 ÷ 1.35 omits the risk-free rate.

</details>

**Q128.** Which fund produced the highest Jensen's alpha, and what is it?
A) Meridian Bluechip, +2.10%  B) Kaveri Focused Equity, +1.80%  C) Tungabhadra Multicap, +2.20%  D) Tungabhadra Multicap, +4.30%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Tungabhadra Multicap, +2.20%**

Market risk premium = 12.5 − 6.5 = 6.0%. Alpha = Rp − [Rf + β(Rm − Rf)].
Meridian: 15.2 − [6.5 + 1.10 × 6.0] = 15.2 − 13.1 = +2.10%
Kaveri: 13.4 − [6.5 + 0.85 × 6.0] = 13.4 − 11.60 = +1.80%
Tungabhadra: 16.8 − [6.5 + 1.35 × 6.0] = 16.8 − 14.60 = **+2.20%**
Note the tension: Tungabhadra beat CAPM by the most, yet ranked last on Sharpe. Alpha rewards beating the beta-implied return; Sharpe penalises the total risk carried to do it.

- **A) Meridian Bluechip, +2.10%** - wrong; correct alpha for Meridian, but it is not the highest.
- **B) Kaveri Focused Equity, +1.80%** - wrong; correct alpha for Kaveri, and the lowest of the three.
- **D) Tungabhadra Multicap, +4.30%** - wrong; 16.8 − 12.5 is the raw excess over the market, which ignores the 1.35 beta the fund carried.

</details>

**Q129.** What is the standard deviation of the 65% Meridian / 35% Kaveri blend?
A) 12.49%  B) 14.82%  C) 15.25%  D) 16.08%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 14.82%**

σp = √(w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2·w1·w2·σ1·σ2·ρ)
= √(0.65² × 18² + 0.35² × 12.5² + 2 × 0.65 × 0.35 × 18 × 12.5 × 0.62)
= √(136.890 + 19.141 + 63.473) = √219.503 = **14.82%**.

- **A) 12.49%** - wrong; this is the result if the correlation were zero. The stated correlation is 0.62.
- **C) 15.25%** - wrong; this is the *unweighted* average of the two standard deviations.
- **D) 16.08%** - wrong; this is the weighted average of the two standard deviations. Portfolio risk equals the weighted average only when the correlation is exactly +1.

</details>

**Q130.** How much risk does diversification remove in this blend, measured against the weighted average of the two funds' standard deviations?

Actual portfolio σ = 14.82%.

### Case 9: Rohan Prabhu's bond and derivative positions

Rohan Prabhu (33) is an engineering manager in Bengaluru. He holds a Karnataka Infra Ltd bond: face value Rs 1,000, 9% annual coupon, exactly 5 years to maturity, with a required yield of 11%. His entire fixed income book, including this bond, has a market value of Rs 18,50,000 and a Macaulay duration of 4.21 years at the current 11% yield. On the derivatives side, the Nifty 50 spot is 24,600, the risk-free rate is 7% a year, the index dividend yield is 1.4% a year, and the three-month futures trade at their cost-of-carry fair value. **Q131.** What is the fair price of the Karnataka Infra bond?
A) Rs 818.18  B) Rs 900.00  C) Rs 926.08  D) Rs 1,000.00
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 926.08**

Coupon = 9% × 1,000 = Rs 90 a year; y = 11%; n = 5.
(1.11)^5 = 1.685058, so the discount factor is 1 ÷ 1.685058 = 0.593452.
Annuity factor = (1 − 0.593452) ÷ 0.11 = 3.69589.
PV of coupons = 90 × 3.69589 = Rs 332.63
PV of redemption = 1,000 × 0.593452 = Rs 593.45
Price = 332.63 + 593.45 = **Rs 926.08** — a discount, as it must be when the 9% coupon is below the 11% required yield.

- **A) Rs 818.18** - wrong; this is C ÷ y (90 ÷ 0.11), the price of a *perpetual* bond. This one redeems in 5 years.
- **B) Rs 900.00** - wrong; a crude straight-line adjustment of 2% × 5 years off par, with no discounting.
- **D) Rs 1,000.00** - wrong; par would apply only if the required yield equalled the 9% coupon rate.

**Q132.** If the required yield falls to 9.5%, what capital gain does Rohan make on one bond?

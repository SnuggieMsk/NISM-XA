**Q1.** Meenakshi Deshpande, a 45-year-old schoolteacher in Pune who was recently widowed, lists her position as: savings account Rs 3,20,000; liquid fund Rs 2,10,000; mutual funds Rs 18,50,000; EPF and gratuity received Rs 9,60,000; her flat in Kothrud Rs 62,00,000; gold jewellery Rs 6,40,000. Her liabilities are a home loan outstanding of Rs 11,40,000 and a car loan outstanding of Rs 2,60,000. What is her net worth?
A) Rs 19,40,000  B) Rs 33,40,000  C) Rs 87,80,000  D) Rs 1,01,80,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 87,80,000**

Total assets = 3,20,000 + 2,10,000 + 18,50,000 + 9,60,000 + 62,00,000 + 6,40,000 = **Rs 1,01,80,000**. Total liabilities = 11,40,000 + 2,60,000 = **Rs 14,00,000**. Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities = 1,01,80,000 − 14,00,000 = **Rs 87,80,000**.

- **A) Rs 19,40,000** - wrong; this deducts the liabilities from only the financial assets (Rs 33,40,000), ignoring the flat and the gold.
- **B) Rs 33,40,000** - wrong; this is the total of her financial assets alone, with no liabilities deducted.
- **D) Rs 1,01,80,000** - wrong; this is total assets, not net worth. Net worth requires the liabilities to be subtracted.

</details>

**Q2.** Meenakshi has a 20-year horizon, no dependants other than a self-supporting son, and a stable salary — giving her a high risk capacity. However, she becomes acutely anxious when her portfolio value falls and says she could not tolerate a 20% drawdown. How should her adviser set the risk level of the plan?
A) Plan to her risk capacity, since it is objective and measurable  B) Plan to the lower of capacity and tolerance  C) Plan to the average of capacity and tolerance  D) Plan to her risk requirement alone, since the goal return is what matters
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Plan to the lower of capacity and tolerance**

Risk **capacity** is the objective *ability* to bear risk (horizon, income stability, net worth, dependants); risk **tolerance** is the subjective *willingness*. Where the two conflict, the plan must be built to the **lower** of the two. A client with high capacity but low tolerance will panic-sell in a drawdown, converting a temporary paper loss into a permanent one.

- **A) Plan to her risk capacity** - wrong; being objectively able to bear a loss does not mean the client will hold through it. Ignoring tolerance invites panic-selling.
- **C) Plan to the average** - wrong; averaging is not the prescribed rule and still leaves the portfolio above the client's psychological limit.
- **D) Plan to her risk requirement alone** - wrong; the return the goal needs is a third input, but it can never override the constraint set by capacity and tolerance.

</details>

**Q3.** Which of the following is the correct sequence of the six steps of the financial planning process?
A) Data gathering → Relationship → Goals → Risk profiling → Review → Portfolio construction  B) Relationship → Data gathering → Goals → Risk profiling → Portfolio construction → Review  C) Goals → Relationship → Risk profiling → Data gathering → Portfolio construction → Review  D) Relationship → Goals → Data gathering → Portfolio construction → Risk profiling → Review
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Relationship → Data gathering → Goals → Risk profiling → Portfolio construction → Review**

The prescribed order is: (1) establish the client–planner relationship, (2) gather data on the client's financial situation, (3) define needs and financial goals, (4) risk profiling and asset allocation, (5) portfolio construction, (6) reviewing and rebalancing.

- **A) Data gathering first** - wrong; data cannot be gathered before the engagement, scope and compensation are agreed in writing, and review always comes last.
- **C) Goals first** - wrong; goals cannot be defined before the relationship is established or before data is collected.
- **D) Goals before data gathering** - wrong; goals are defined *after* data gathering, and risk profiling must precede portfolio construction, not follow it.

</details>

**Q4.** A SEBI-registered Investment Adviser is described as owing a *fiduciary* duty to clients. What does this mean in practice?
A) The adviser must guarantee that recommended products will not lose money  B) The adviser must place the client's interest ahead of the adviser's own  C) The adviser must only recommend products approved by the manufacturer  D) The adviser must ensure recommendations are merely suitable, without regard to conflicts
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) The adviser must place the client's interest ahead of the adviser's own**

A fiduciary standard requires the adviser to act in the client's best interest, disclose conflicts, and put the client first. It is the defining obligation of a registered IA and underpins SEBI's requirement that advice be segregated from distribution.

- **A) Guarantee against loss** - wrong; no adviser can guarantee investment outcomes, and promising assured returns is itself prohibited.
- **C) Only manufacturer-approved products** - wrong; that describes a distribution arrangement, which is the very conflict the fiduciary standard is designed to neutralise.
- **D) Merely suitable** - wrong; suitability is the *lower* standard applied to some distribution models. Fiduciary duty is stricter than suitability.

</details>

**Q5.** Which of the following statements best describes a well-framed financial goal?
A) "Build a large corpus for my son's post-graduate education"  B) "Invest Rs 25,000 a month in an equity fund with a good track record"  C) "Accumulate Rs 46 lakh by June 2034 for a two-year post-graduate course"  D) "Earn at least 14% a year on my investments for the next ten years"
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) "Accumulate Rs 46 lakh by June 2034 for a two-year post-graduate course"**

A good goal is **specific, quantified in future (inflation-adjusted) rupees, and dated**, so it can be prioritised and funded. Option C states the purpose, the future cost and the date.

- **A) "A large corpus"** - wrong; it names a purpose but has no amount and no date, so nothing can be computed from it.
- **B) "Invest Rs 25,000 a month..."** - wrong; this is a *means* (a product decision), not a goal. It does not say what is being funded or when.
- **D) "Earn at least 14% a year"** - wrong; a return target is not a life goal, and it is not within the investor's control.

</details>

**Q6.** Meenakshi invests a lump sum of Rs 18,00,000 from her late husband's provident fund settlement in an instrument compounding at 9.5% per annum. What will it be worth after 7 years?
A) Rs 29,97,000  B) Rs 31,02,825  C) Rs 33,97,593  D) Rs 37,20,364
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 33,97,593**

FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ = 18,00,000 × (1.095)⁷. (1.095)⁷ = 1.887552, so FV = 18,00,000 × 1.887552 = **Rs 33,97,593**.

- **A) Rs 29,97,000** - wrong; this is simple interest, 18,00,000 × (1 + 0.095 × 7). It ignores compounding.
- **B) Rs 31,02,825** - wrong; this compounds for only 6 years, one period short.
- **D) Rs 37,20,364** - wrong; this compounds for 8 years, one period too many.

</details>

- **B) Rs 13,04,436** - wrong; this discounts for only 11 years.
- **C) Rs 15,84,158** - wrong; this uses simple interest in the denominator, 32,00,000 ÷ (1 + 0.085 × 12).

</details>

**Q8.** A holding of Meenakshi's grew from Rs 6,50,000 to Rs 15,60,000 over exactly 9 years. What is the compound annual growth rate?
A) 10.22%  B) 12.45%  C) 15.56%  D) 24.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 10.22%**

CAGR = (FV ÷ PV)^(1/n) − 1 = (15,60,000 ÷ 6,50,000)^(1/9) − 1 = (2.4)^0.1111 − 1 = 1.10216 − 1 = **10.22% p.a.**

- **B) 12.45%** - wrong; it does not follow from any correct rearrangement of FV = PV(1+r)ⁿ at these inputs.
- **C) 15.56%** - wrong; this is simple-interest thinking — the total gain of 140% divided by 9 years.
- **D) 24.00%** - wrong; this grossly overstates the compounded rate and ignores that returns compound on a rising base.

</details>

FV = 14,500 × [(3.892543 − 1) ÷ 0.00875] = 14,500 × 330.5763 = **Rs 47,93,357**.

**Q10.** A bank quotes a nominal rate of 8.4% per annum on a deposit, compounded quarterly. What is the effective annual rate?
A) 8.40%  B) 8.58%  C) 8.67%  D) 8.73%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 8.67%**

EAR = (1 + r/m)^m − 1 = (1 + 0.084/4)⁴ − 1 = (1.021)⁴ − 1 = 1.086683 − 1 = **8.67%**.

- **A) 8.40%** - wrong; this is the nominal (quoted) rate. The nominal rate never changes with frequency; only the effective rate does.
- **B) 8.58%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **half-yearly** compounding, (1.042)² − 1.
- **D) 8.73%** - wrong; this is the EAR for **monthly** compounding, (1.007)¹² − 1.

**Q11.** Meenakshi's debt portfolio is expected to return 11.25% per annum while inflation runs at 5.75%. What is her expected real return?
A) 5.00%  B) 5.20%  C) 5.50%  D) 6.05%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 5.20%**

Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1 = (1.1125 ÷ 1.0575) − 1 = 1.052009 − 1 = **5.20%**.

- **A) 5.00%** - wrong; a rounded guess that does not follow from the formula.
- **C) 5.50%** - wrong; this is the crude subtraction 11.25 − 5.75, which always **overstates** the real return.
- **D) 6.05%** - wrong; this multiplies rather than divides, which is the route from real to nominal, not the reverse.

</details>

**Q16.** Meenakshi's adviser recommends she build a contingency fund. Which statement about the contingency fund is correct?
A) It should be sized at 3–6 months of gross income  B) It should be sized at 3–6 months of expenses and held in liquid, capital-stable instruments  C) It should be invested in equity mutual funds so that it grows faster than inflation  D) It is unnecessary once adequate health and life insurance are in place
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) It should be sized at 3–6 months of expenses and held in liquid, capital-stable instruments**

The fund is sized against **expenses** — what must actually be paid — and demands **immediate availability plus certainty of value**: savings balance, sweep-in FD or liquid/overnight funds. Return is secondary.

- **A) 3–6 months of gross income** - wrong; income is not the benchmark. A high earner with modest expenses would end up holding far more idle cash than needed.
- **C) Invested in equity** - wrong; equity is exactly the wrong home. Job loss and recessions tend to coincide with market falls, so the money would be needed precisely when it is down.
- **D) Unnecessary with insurance** - wrong; insurance pays only for specified insured events and only after a claim process. The fund covers income gaps and unplanned costs immediately.

</details>

**Q17.** Meenakshi's monthly budget balances neatly in most months, yet she runs a deficit every March and every October. The most likely cause is that her budget has failed to provide for:
A) Fixed outflows such as EMIs and rent  B) Variable outflows such as groceries and fuel  C) Periodic outflows such as annual insurance premiums and festival spending  D) Savings outflows such as SIPs and recurring deposits
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Periodic outflows such as annual insurance premiums and festival spending**

**Periodic (lumpy) outflows** are the most commonly forgotten category — predictable but infrequent, so they never appear in a typical month and then break the budget when they fall due. The fix is to divide each annual item by 12 and set that amount aside monthly.

- **A) Fixed outflows** - wrong; EMIs and rent recur every month and are the easiest items to capture in a monthly budget.
- **B) Variable outflows** - wrong; these recur monthly too. They can cause general overspending but not a deficit confined to two specific months.
- **D) Savings outflows** - wrong; omitting SIPs from the budget would show up as an apparent *surplus*, not a recurring deficit.

</details>

**Q18.** Meenakshi refinances the outstanding home loan on her flat — Rs 22,00,000 at 8.75% per annum for 12 years. What is the EMI?
A) Rs 15,278  B) Rs 16,042  C) Rs 24,728  D) Rs 27,315
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 24,728**

r = 0.0875 ÷ 12 = 0.00729167; n = 12 × 12 = 144; (1.00729167)¹⁴⁴ = 2.846785.
EMI = P × r(1+r)ⁿ ÷ [(1+r)ⁿ − 1] = 22,00,000 × (0.00729167 × 2.846785) ÷ 1.846785 = **Rs 24,728**.

- **A) Rs 15,278** - wrong; this is simply principal ÷ number of months (22,00,000 ÷ 144), which ignores interest entirely.
- **B) Rs 16,042** - wrong; this is the **first month's interest** (22,00,000 × 0.00729167), a standard distractor.
- **D) Rs 27,315** - wrong; it overstates the EMI and does not follow from the formula at these inputs.

</details>

**Q19.** For the loan in the previous question — Rs 22,00,000 at 8.75% for 12 years, EMI Rs 24,728 — how much of the **first** EMI goes towards repaying principal?

- **B) Rs 15,278** - wrong; this is principal ÷ 144, a straight-line split that an amortising loan never follows.
- **C) Rs 16,042** - wrong; this is the **interest** component of the first EMI, not the principal component.
- **D) Rs 24,728** - wrong; this is the whole EMI, which still contains the interest portion.

**Q21.** Meenakshi has three loans: a home loan on her Kothrud flat, a car loan, and a gold loan taken against her jewellery. Which describes the security arrangements correctly?
A) Home loan — pledge; car loan — mortgage; gold loan — hypothecation  B) Home loan — mortgage; car loan — hypothecation; gold loan — pledge  C) Home loan — hypothecation; car loan — pledge; gold loan — mortgage  D) Home loan — mortgage; car loan — pledge; gold loan — hypothecation
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Home loan — mortgage; car loan — hypothecation; gold loan — pledge**

**Mortgage** = security over **immovable** property (the flat). **Hypothecation** = security over **movable** property where **possession stays with the borrower** (she drives the car). **Pledge** = security over **movable** property where **possession passes to the lender** (the bank holds the gold).

- **A) Pledge / mortgage / hypothecation** - wrong; it reverses all three. Immovable property can never be pledged or hypothecated.
- **C) Hypothecation / pledge / mortgage** - wrong; a flat cannot be hypothecated, and gold held by the lender is a pledge, not a mortgage.
- **D) Mortgage / pledge / hypothecation** - wrong; the home loan is right but the car and the gold are swapped. Possession is the deciding test.

</details>

**Q22.** Meenakshi owes Rs 1,80,000 on a credit card at 42% annualised, Rs 4,50,000 on a personal loan at 15%, and Rs 9,00,000 on a car loan at 10%. Her adviser recommends the **avalanche** method. Which debt does she attack first with her surplus?
A) The car loan, because it is the largest balance  B) The credit card, because it carries the highest interest rate  C) The credit card, because it has the smallest balance  D) The personal loan, because it sits between the other two on both rate and size
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) The credit card, because it carries the highest interest rate**

The **avalanche** method targets the **highest interest rate** first while paying minimums on the rest. It is mathematically optimal — it minimises total interest paid. Memory hook: **Ava**lanche → **A** for **A**PR.

- **A) The car loan, largest balance** - wrong; no named strategy selects on the largest balance, and doing so leaves the costliest debt compounding at 42%.
- **C) The credit card, smallest balance** - wrong; the card happens to be the right target, but the **reason** stated describes the **snowball** method, which selects on balance size, not rate.
- **D) The personal loan** - wrong; at 15% it is not the costliest debt, so avalanche would not select it first.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) The clearing corporation**

**Q80.** Which of the following is **NOT** a "security" as defined in Section 2(h) of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956?
A) A Government security  B) A unit of a mutual fund scheme  C) A unit-linked insurance policy  D) A debenture of an incorporated company
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) A unit-linked insurance policy**

Section 2(h) expressly **excludes a unit-linked insurance policy (ULIP)** or any similar instrument that combines life risk cover with investment and is issued by an insurer - the exclusion inserted after the 2010 SEBI-IRDA dispute over ULIPs. Fixed deposits, bank accounts, gold, real estate and insurance policies are likewise not securities.

- **A) A Government security** - wrong choice; G-Secs are expressly included in the definition.
- **B) A unit of a mutual fund scheme** - wrong choice; units issued to investors under a mutual fund scheme are expressly included.
- **D) A debenture of an incorporated company** - wrong choice; shares, scrips, stocks, bonds and debentures are the core of the definition.

**Q81.** In the eleven months before her husband's death, Sunita's previous adviser switched her equity holdings between schemes eleven times. Her stated investment strategy and risk profile did not change once during the period, and each switch generated fresh commission. This conduct is best labelled:
A) Mis-selling  B) Churning  C) Front-running  D) Rebalancing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Churning**

**Churning is excessive trading, switching or turnover in a client's portfolio to generate brokerage or fresh commission.** The give-away sign is exactly what is described: **a lot of activity with no change in the underlying strategy**.

- **A) Mis-selling** - wrong; mis-selling is about the **wrong product** for the client's needs, and a single transaction can be mis-selling. Here the issue is the **frequency**, not necessarily the schemes.
- **C) Front-running** - wrong; front-running means dealing **ahead of a client's known impending order** to profit from the price move it will cause.
- **D) Rebalancing** - wrong; rebalancing restores agreed target weights on a documented calendar or threshold rule; it is neither frequent nor commission-driven.

**Q82.** An adviser learns that a large client is about to place a substantial buy order in a mid-cap stock, and buys the stock for his own account fifteen minutes earlier. This is:
A) Insider trading, dealt with under the PIT Regulations, 2015  B) Front-running, dealt with under the PFUTP Regulations, 2003  C) Churning, dealt with under the IA Code of Conduct  D) Mis-selling, dealt with under Regulation 17
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Front-running, dealt with under the PFUTP Regulations, 2003**

**Front-running** is dealing in a security **ahead of a substantial impending client order** of which one has unpublished knowledge. It is a deemed manipulative and unfair practice under **Regulation 4(2) of the SEBI (Prohibition of Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices) Regulations, 2003**. Memory hook: **front-run an ORDER, insider-trade on NEWS.**

- **A) Insider trading, dealt with under the PIT Regulations, 2015** - wrong; insider trading uses **unpublished price-sensitive information about the company itself**, not knowledge of an order book.
- **C) Churning, dealt with under the IA Code of Conduct** - wrong; churning is excessive turnover in the **client's** portfolio, not a trade in the adviser's own account.
- **D) Mis-selling, dealt with under Regulation 17** - wrong; nothing unsuitable has been recommended to the client here.

</details>

**Q83.** An adviser recommends a complex market-linked debenture to a client without having researched the product or considered available alternatives. Which limb of his fiduciary duty under Regulation 15(1) has he primarily breached?
A) The duty of loyalty  B) The duty of care  C) The duty of confidentiality  D) The duty of disclosure
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) The duty of care**

The **duty of care** requires **competent, diligent and suitable** advice - know the client, research the product, consider alternatives, recommend what fits, and monitor it afterwards. Recommending something he has not researched is a failure of care. (Loyalty answers "whose side are you on?"; care answers "did you do the work?")

- **A) The duty of loyalty** - wrong; loyalty concerns putting the client first and not profiting at his expense - undisclosed commission, front-running, misuse of client information. No such self-interest is alleged here.
- **C) The duty of confidentiality** - wrong; no client information has been revealed or misused.
- **D) The duty of disclosure** - wrong; disclosure is a component of loyalty and the Code of Conduct, but the defect described is the **absence of analysis**, not the absence of a disclosure.

</details>

**Q84.** A distributor's brochure claims "225% return!" for a scheme whose NAV rose from Rs 12 to Rs 39 over 8 years. What is the honest annualised (CAGR) figure?
A) 15.87%  B) 12.50%  C) 28.13%  D) 225.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 15.87%**

CAGR = (39 / 12) raised to the power (1/8), minus 1 = (3.25)^0.125 - 1 = 1.1587 - 1 = **15.87% per annum**. The "225%" headline is the arithmetically true **absolute** return over the whole 8 years ((39 - 12)/12), quoted without its period - the commonest form of return misrepresentation in India.

- **B) 12.50%** - wrong; this is 100 divided by 8, an arbitrary figure with no basis in the data.
- **C) 28.13%** - wrong; this simply divides the 225% absolute return by 8 years, which ignores compounding altogether.
- **D) 225.00%** - wrong; this is the absolute return over 8 years, not an annual rate.

**Q85.** The annual compliance audit of a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser throws up adverse observations. Within what time must those observations, together with the action taken on them, be reported?
A) Within 7 days of the audit report  B) Within one month of the date of the audit report  C) Within six months of the end of the financial year  D) Within one year of the audit report
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Within one month of the date of the audit report**

The audit must be conducted by a member of **ICAI or ICSI**. **Adverse findings, together with the action taken on them and duly approved by the management/board/proprietor, must be reported within one month of the date of the audit report.** Note that the reportable item is the finding **plus** the action taken - reporting the observation alone is incomplete.

- **A) Within 7 days of the audit report** - wrong; no seven-day rule applies to this reporting.
- **C) Within six months of the end of the financial year** - wrong; six months (i.e. by 30 September) is the deadline for **completing the audit**, not for reporting the action taken on adverse findings.
- **D) Within one year of the audit report** - wrong; this is far outside the prescribed window and would defeat the purpose of contemporaneous remediation.

**Q86.** Under SCORES 2.0, within how many days must the entity complained against file its Action Taken Report?
A) 7 calendar days  B) 15 calendar days  C) 21 calendar days  D) 30 calendar days
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 21 calendar days**

The SCORES 2.0 rhythm is **21 - 15 - 21 - 15 - 21**: the **entity has 21 calendar days** to file its Action Taken Report; the investor may seek a first review within 15 days; the **Designated Body** has 21 days; a second review may be sought within 15 days; and **SEBI** has 21 days. If the entity misses its 21 days, the complaint **auto-escalates** without the investor doing anything.

- **A) 7 calendar days** - wrong; seven **working** days is the STR deadline under PMLA, not the SCORES clock.
- **B) 15 calendar days** - wrong; 15 days is the window in which the **investor** may seek a review, not the entity's response time.
- **D) 30 calendar days** - wrong; 30 days is the RBI Ombudsman escalation trigger and the IA's own grievance-redressal condition of registration, not the SCORES ATR deadline.

**Q87.** Dissatisfied with her adviser's fee billing, Sunita lodges a complaint on SCORES without ever having raised the matter with the adviser. What is the likely outcome?
A) SEBI will investigate the billing and order compensation  B) The complaint is not maintainable until she has first taken it up with the adviser  C) SEBI will forward it directly to the Securities Appellate Tribunal  D) SEBI will refer it to the consumer commission on her behalf
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) The complaint is not maintainable until she has first taken it up with the adviser**

Every Indian redress system follows the same ladder: **intermediary first, regulator next, tribunal or adjudicator last.** SCORES expressly requires that the investor has **first approached the entity** and received no reply, an unsatisfactory reply, or no action. A complaint that skips the firm is bounced back.

- **A) SEBI will investigate the billing and order compensation** - wrong; SEBI **routes, tracks and escalates** complaints and enforces timelines. It does **not adjudicate individual money claims** - that is for an ombudsman, an ODR arbitrator, a consumer commission or a civil court.
- **C) SEBI will forward it directly to the Securities Appellate Tribunal** - wrong; SAT is an **appellate** body against regulators' orders, not a complaint counter.
- **D) SEBI will refer it to the consumer commission on her behalf** - wrong; SCORES does not transfer complaints to consumer fora; the investor chooses that route herself.

**Q88.** Sunita has a disputed money claim of Rs 3,20,000 against a stock broker. She has already complained to the broker and escalated through SCORES without a satisfactory outcome. What is the appropriate next step?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Initiate proceedings on the SMART ODR portal**

**SCORES chases; ODR decides.** Having exhausted the market participant and SCORES, a **disputed money claim** goes to the **SMART ODR portal** (smartodr.in), introduced by SEBI's circular of 31 July 2023 - **online conciliation** first (non-binding, target about 21 days) and then **online arbitration**, producing a binding award enforceable under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996.

- **A) File an appeal before the Securities Appellate Tribunal within 45 days** - wrong; SAT hears appeals by a person **aggrieved by an ORDER of a regulator**. It is not a first-instance forum for an investor's claim against a broker.
- **C) File directly before the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission** - wrong; even if the consumer route were used, a Rs 3.20 lakh claim would go to the **District Commission** (up to Rs 50 lakh under the 2021 Rules), not the National Commission.
- **D) Approach the RBI Integrated Ombudsman** - wrong; RB-IOS 2021 covers banks, NBFCs, payment system participants and credit information companies, not stock brokers.

</details>

**Q89.** Under the RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021, what is the maximum total compensation the Ombudsman may award?
A) Rs 1 lakh  B) Rs 20 lakh  C) Rs 21 lakh  D) Rs 30 lakh

**Correct: C) Rs 21 lakh**

The Ombudsman may award up to **Rs 20 lakh for the actual loss suffered** **plus** a separate amount of up to **Rs 1 lakh** for loss of time, expenses, harassment and mental anguish. The two caps are **separate and additive**, giving a theoretical maximum of **Rs 21 lakh**.

- **A) Rs 1 lakh** - wrong; that is only the mental-agony and time component.
- **B) Rs 20 lakh** - wrong; that is only the actual-loss component and ignores the additional Rs 1 lakh head.
- **D) Rs 30 lakh** - wrong; Rs 30 lakh is the value limit of the **Insurance Ombudsman**, a different scheme.

</details>

**Q90.** Sunita's late husband's life insurer has partly repudiated a claim of Rs 26 lakh. She made a written representation to the insurer and received a reply she finds unsatisfactory. Which forum should she approach next?

### Case 1: Kavita Deshpande's household after a bereavement

Kavita Deshpande is 45 and teaches physics at a school in Kothrud, Pune. Her husband Anirudh died eight months ago. She now draws a salary of Rs 78,000 a month and a family pension of Rs 22,000 a month. Household expenses for her and her 13-year-old daughter Ira come to Rs 64,000 a month, of which Rs 23,680 is the EMI on the home loan on their flat. She holds Rs 1,45,000 in a savings account and Rs 2,75,000 in a liquid fund. Her total assets — the flat, the insurance and settlement proceeds she received, her gold and her financial investments — come to Rs 1,32,00,000, and the outstanding home loan is Rs 21,12,000. Because the household now runs on a single earner with a dependent child, her adviser wants the contingency fund built up to nine months of expenses.

**Q91.** What is Kavita's monthly savings ratio?
A) 36.00%  B) 46.15%  C) 56.25%  D) 64.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 36.00%**

Gross monthly income = 78,000 + 22,000 = Rs 1,00,000. Savings = 1,00,000 − 64,000 = Rs 36,000.
Savings ratio = Savings ÷ Gross income = 36,000 ÷ 1,00,000 = **36.00%**.
Check: expenses ratio = 64,000 ÷ 1,00,000 = 64.00%, and 36.00 + 64.00 = 100 ✓

- **B) 46.15%** - wrong; this uses only her salary (36,000 ÷ 78,000) and ignores the family pension, which is part of gross income.
- **C) 56.25%** - wrong; this divides savings by expenses (36,000 ÷ 64,000) instead of by income.
- **D) 64.00%** - wrong; this is the expenses ratio, the complement of the savings ratio.

</details>

**Q92.** What is her liquidity ratio?
A) 2.27 months  B) 4.20 months  C) 6.56 months  D) 10.42 months
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 6.56 months**

Liquid assets = 1,45,000 (savings account) + 2,75,000 (liquid fund) = Rs 4,20,000.
Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets ÷ **Monthly expenses** = 4,20,000 ÷ 64,000 = **6.5625 = 6.56 months** — comfortably inside the healthy 3–6 month band, and just above it.

- **A) 2.27 months** - wrong; this counts only the savings account (1,45,000 ÷ 64,000). A liquid fund redeems in one business day and is a liquid asset.
- **B) 4.20 months** - wrong; this divides by gross income (4,20,000 ÷ 1,00,000). The liquidity ratio is measured against expenses, because expenses are what must be paid.
- **D) 10.42 months** - wrong; this strips the EMI out of expenses (4,20,000 ÷ 40,320). The EMI still has to be paid during an emergency, so it stays in.

</details>

**Q93.** What is her solvency ratio?
A) 0.16  B) 0.19  C) 0.84  D) 1.19
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 0.84**

Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities = 1,32,00,000 − 21,12,000 = Rs 1,10,88,000.
Solvency ratio = Net worth ÷ Total assets = 1,10,88,000 ÷ 1,32,00,000 = **0.84**.
Check: leverage ratio = 21,12,000 ÷ 1,32,00,000 = 0.16, and solvency + leverage = 1 ✓

- **A) 0.16** - wrong; this is the leverage (debt-to-assets) ratio, not solvency.
- **B) 0.19** - wrong; this divides liabilities by net worth (21,12,000 ÷ 1,10,88,000), which is a debt-to-equity figure.
- **D) 1.19** - wrong; this inverts the ratio (assets ÷ net worth). Solvency can never exceed 1.

</details>

**Q94.** What is her debt-to-income ratio?
A) 23.68%  B) 30.36%  C) 37.00%  D) 40.32%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 23.68%**

Debt-to-income ratio = Total EMIs ÷ Gross income = 23,680 ÷ 1,00,000 = **23.68%**, well inside the 35–40% comfort ceiling.

- **B) 30.36%** - wrong; this uses only her salary as the denominator (23,680 ÷ 78,000).
- **C) 37.00%** - wrong; this divides the EMI by expenses (23,680 ÷ 64,000) rather than by income.
- **D) 40.32%** - wrong; this is the non-EMI expense burden (40,320 ÷ 1,00,000), not the debt burden.

</details>

**Q95.** How much more must Kavita set aside to reach the contingency fund her adviser recommends?
A) Rs 1,56,000  B) Rs 3,84,000  C) Rs 4,80,000  D) Rs 5,76,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Rs 1,56,000**

Target = 9 months × monthly **expenses** = 9 × 64,000 = Rs 5,76,000.
Existing liquid assets = 1,45,000 + 2,75,000 = Rs 4,20,000.
Shortfall = 5,76,000 − 4,20,000 = **Rs 1,56,000**.

- **B) Rs 3,84,000** - wrong; this is a six-month target (6 × 64,000), not the nine months the adviser recommends, and it ignores what she already holds.
- **C) Rs 4,80,000** - wrong; this sizes the fund against income (9 × 1,00,000 = 9,00,000, less 4,20,000). The contingency fund is sized against expenses.
- **D) Rs 5,76,000** - wrong; this is the full nine-month target and ignores the Rs 4,20,000 already in place.

**Q98.** Using the approximate formula, what is the yield to maturity on the Godavari Power bonds?
A) 8.00%  B) 8.51%  C) 9.79%  D) 10.11%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

- **C) Rs 50.63** - wrong; this is the simple arithmetic average of the four NAVs. A fixed rupee SIP buys more units at low NAVs, so the average **cost** is always below the average **NAV**.
- **D) Rs 51.25** - wrong; this weights the NAVs by their own level rather than by units bought, which reverses the averaging effect.

### Case 4: Kavita considers a PMS and an AIF

After keeping her contingency fund and her goal money aside, Kavita has an investible surplus of Rs 1,15,00,000. Her adviser puts three managed options in front of her. **Q106.** What are the minimum investment amounts SEBI prescribes for a PMS and for an AIF respectively?

**Correct: B) Rs 50 lakh and Rs 1 crore**

Under the SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, 2020 the minimum client investment in a PMS is **Rs 50 lakh**. Under the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012 the minimum investment per investor in an AIF is **Rs 1 crore**. The ladder to remember is: mutual fund Rs 100–5,000 → PMS Rs 50 lakh → AIF Rs 1 crore.

- **A) Rs 25 lakh and Rs 50 lakh** - wrong; Rs 25 lakh is the reduced AIF minimum for employees and directors of the AIF or its manager, and for angel investors.

**Q107.** What performance fee may the portfolio manager charge for year 1?
A) Rs 1,08,000  B) Rs 1,20,000  C) Rs 2,16,000  D) Rs 14,16,000

**Correct: A) Rs 1,08,000**

Gross gain = 94,40,000 − 80,00,000 = Rs 14,40,000, i.e. an 18% return.

Return above the hurdle = 14,40,000 − 7,20,000 = Rs 7,20,000.

- **C) Rs 2,16,000** - wrong; this charges 15% on the entire gain of Rs 14,40,000 and ignores the hurdle.

**Q108.** Ignoring the hurdle rate, on what amount of gain may a performance fee be charged in year 3 under the high watermark principle?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Rs 2,60,000**

The high watermark is the highest value the portfolio has previously reached — **Rs 94,40,000** at the end of year 1. Year 2's fall to Rs 88,00,000 attracts no performance fee. In year 3 the fee applies only to the gain **above the watermark**: 97,00,000 − 94,40,000 = **Rs 2,60,000**.

- **B) Rs 9,00,000** - wrong; this is the year-3 rise from Rs 88,00,000. Charging on it would make the client pay a second time for recovering ground already lost — exactly what the high watermark prevents.
- **C) Rs 17,00,000** - wrong; this is the gain over the original Rs 80,00,000 investment, on part of which a fee has already been charged in year 1.

**Q110.** Which statement about the taxation of the two AIFs is correct?

### Case 8: Choosing a manager for the equity sleeve

**Correct: C) Godavari Balanced, 0.66**

**Q127.** Which fund has the highest Treynor ratio, and what is it?
A) Warkari Bluechip, 9.37%  B) Deccan Opportunities, 7.64%  C) Godavari Balanced, 8.55%  D) Warkari Bluechip, 16.21%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Warkari Bluechip, 9.37%**

Warkari: 8.9 ÷ 0.95 = **9.37%** — the highest

Godavari: 5.3 ÷ 0.62 = **8.55%**

- **B) Deccan Opportunities, 7.64%** - wrong; the ratio is correct but it is the lowest of the three.
- **C) Godavari Balanced, 8.55%** - wrong; the ratio is correct but Godavari wins on Sharpe, not on Treynor.

### Case 9: The debt sleeve and a derivative overlay

Kavita's adviser is working through the fixed income and derivative parts of her portfolio. Bond X has a face value of Rs 1,000, pays a 9% annual coupon, has exactly 5 years to maturity and carries a required yield of 11%. The adviser also models what happens a year from now, when 4 years remain and the required yield has fallen to 9.5%. Bond Y is a separate holding worth Rs 24,00,000 at present with a modified duration of 6.8; the adviser stresses it for a 75 basis point rise in yields. On the derivatives side the Nifty spot is 24,800 and the lot size is 25. Kavita has bought one Nifty 24,900 call at a premium of Rs 185. She also holds an index-tracking equity portfolio worth Rs 24,60,000 with a beta of 1.0, protected by four Nifty 24,600 put contracts bought at a premium of Rs 142 each. Two expiry scenarios are examined: **Scenario 1**, the Nifty at 25,340, and **Scenario 2**, the Nifty at 23,700.

**Q131.** What is the fair price of Bond X today?
A) Rs 926.08  B) Rs 962.09  C) Rs 1,000.00  D) Rs 1,077.79
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Rs 926.08**

A bond is an annuity of coupons plus a lump sum at redemption.
(1.11)^5 = 1.6850582, so the discount factor is 1 ÷ 1.6850582 = 0.5934513.
Annuity factor = (1 − 0.5934513) ÷ 0.11 = 3.6959.
PV of coupons = 90 × 3.6959 = Rs 332.63
PV of redemption = 1,000 × 0.5934513 = Rs 593.45
Price = **Rs 926.08**. Coupon (9%) is below the required yield (11%), so the bond must trade at a **discount** ✓

- **B) Rs 962.09** - wrong; this discounts at 10%, the midpoint of the coupon and the yield, rather than at the required yield of 11%.
- **C) Rs 1,000.00** - wrong; a bond prices at par only when the coupon equals the required yield.
- **D) Rs 1,077.79** - wrong; this swaps the coupon and the yield, pricing an 11% coupon at a 9% yield, and so produces a premium instead of a discount.

</details>

**Q132.** A year from now, with 4 years to maturity and the required yield down to 9.5%, what will Bond X be worth and what capital gain will that represent per bond?
A) Rs 983.98, a gain of Rs 57.90  B) Rs 983.98, a loss of Rs 57.90  C) Rs 962.09, a gain of Rs 36.01  D) Rs 1,000.00, a gain of Rs 73.92

Annuity factor = (1 − 0.6955730) ÷ 0.095 = 3.2044947.
PV of coupons = 90 × 3.2044947 = Rs 288.404; PV of redemption = 1,000 × 0.6955730 = Rs 695.573.
New price = **Rs 983.98**.
Step 2 — the gain: 983.98 − 926.08 = **Rs 57.90 a bond**. Falling yields lift bond prices, and the bond is still at a small discount because 9% coupon < 9.5% yield ✓

- **B) Rs 983.98, a loss of Rs 57.90** - wrong; the price is right but the direction is reversed. A fall in yields produces a capital **gain**.
- **C) Rs 962.09, a gain of Rs 36.01** - wrong; Rs 962.09 comes from discounting five years of cash flows at 10%, not four years at 9.5%.

**Q134.** Under Scenario 1, what is Kavita's net profit on the 24,900 call, and where was its break-even?
A) Rs 6,375, break-even 25,085  B) Rs 11,000, break-even 24,900  C) Rs 6,375, break-even 24,715  D) Rs 13,500, break-even 25,085

- **D) Rs 13,500, break-even 25,085** - wrong; Rs 13,500 measures the move from the spot of 24,800 rather than from the 24,900 strike, and ignores the premium.

**Q135.** Under Scenario 2, what is the net outcome on Kavita's Rs 24,60,000 index-tracking portfolio together with its four protective puts?

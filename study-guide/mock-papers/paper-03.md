**Q1.** Jignesh Bhatt, a 52-year-old textile-machinery business owner in Ahmedabad, has engaged an Investment Adviser. Which sequence correctly describes the six-step financial planning process?
A) Data gathering → Client-planner relationship → Goals → Risk profiling → Portfolio construction → Review  B) Client-planner relationship → Data gathering → Goals → Risk profiling → Portfolio construction → Review  C) Client-planner relationship → Goals → Data gathering → Portfolio construction → Risk profiling → Review  D) Goals → Client-planner relationship → Risk profiling → Data gathering → Review → Portfolio construction
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Client-planner relationship → Data gathering → Goals → Risk profiling → Portfolio construction → Review**

The six steps in order are: (1) establish the **client–planner relationship** (scope, services, compensation, in writing), (2) **gather data** on the client's financial situation, (3) define **needs and financial goals**, (4) **risk profiling** and asset allocation, (5) **portfolio construction**, (6) **reviewing and rebalancing**. The engagement terms must be settled before any data is collected.

- **A) Data gathering → Client-planner relationship → ...** - wrong; data cannot be collected before the engagement and its scope are agreed.
- **C) Client-planner relationship → Goals → Data gathering → ...** - wrong; goals are defined after the data is in hand, and risk profiling precedes construction rather than following it.
- **D) Goals → Client-planner relationship → ...** - wrong on both the starting point and the placement of review before construction.

</details>

**Q2.** Jignesh has a net worth of Rs 10.8 crore, stable business income and a 13-year horizon to his target retirement date, but he tells his adviser that a 15% fall in his portfolio would make him "sell everything the same week". How should the adviser set the allocation?
A) To his high risk capacity, since capacity is objective and measurable  B) To his low risk tolerance, since the plan must be built to the lower of capacity and tolerance  C) To the simple average of capacity and tolerance  D) To the risk requirement of the goal, ignoring both capacity and tolerance
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) To his low risk tolerance, since the plan must be built to the lower of capacity and tolerance**

**Risk capacity** is the objective *ability* to bear risk (horizon, income stability, net worth, dependants). **Risk tolerance** is the subjective *willingness*. Where they conflict, the plan is built to the **lower** of the two — a client with high capacity but low tolerance will panic-sell at the bottom, converting a temporary paper loss into a permanent one.

- **A) To his high risk capacity** - wrong; objectivity does not override the client's demonstrated behaviour under stress.
- **C) To the simple average** - wrong; there is no averaging rule, and the average still exceeds what he can psychologically hold.
- **D) To the risk requirement of the goal** - wrong; the risk requirement tells you what return the goal *needs*, but it can never override the binding constraint.

</details>

**Q5.** Which statement best describes the defining obligation of a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser compared with a product distributor?
A) The adviser must guarantee that recommended products beat their benchmark  B) The adviser is a fiduciary and must place the client's interest ahead of their own  C) The adviser may recommend only products on which no commission is payable to anyone in the market  D) The adviser must earn income exclusively from the execution of transactions
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) The adviser is a fiduciary and must place the client's interest ahead of their own**

A **fiduciary** duty means the client's interest comes *ahead of* the adviser's own. A distributor may operate under a lower suitability standard and is typically paid by the manufacturer. SEBI reinforces the distinction through the **segregation of advice and distribution** — an individual Investment Adviser cannot do both.

- **A) Must guarantee outperformance** - wrong; no adviser can guarantee returns, and planning success is measured by whether the goal was met, not by beating a benchmark.
- **C) Only products paying no commission to anyone** - wrong; the requirement is segregation and disclosure of conflicts, not the removal of commission-bearing products from the market.
- **D) Income exclusively from execution** - wrong; that describes distribution, the very activity that must be kept separate from advice.

</details>

**Q6.** Jignesh invests a business bonus of Rs 18,50,000 in a scheme expected to compound at 9.5% per annum. What will it grow to in 8 years?
A) Rs 32,56,000  B) Rs 34,91,970  C) Rs 38,23,708  D) Rs 41,86,960
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Rs 38,23,708**

FV = PV × (1+r)^n = 18,50,000 × (1.095)^8. (1.095)^8 = 2.066869. FV = 18,50,000 × 2.066869 = **Rs 38,23,708**.

- **A) Rs 32,56,000** - wrong; this uses simple interest, 18,50,000 × (1 + 0.095 × 8) = 18,50,000 × 1.76.
- **B) Rs 34,91,970** - wrong; this compounds for only 7 years.
- **D) Rs 41,86,960** - wrong; this compounds for 9 years.

</details>

**Q7.** An investment made by Jignesh's wife Rupal grew from Rs 6,40,000 to Rs 21,60,000 over 11 years. What is the CAGR?
A) 9.85%  B) 11.69%  C) 13.42%  D) 21.59%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 11.69%**

CAGR = (FV ÷ PV)^(1/n) − 1 = (21,60,000 ÷ 6,40,000)^(1/11) − 1 = (3.375)^0.090909 − 1 = 1.11693 − 1 = **11.69% p.a.**

- **A) 9.85%** - wrong; this understates the rate, as would result from spreading the same growth over 13 years.
- **C) 13.42%** - wrong; this overstates the rate, as would result from using 9 years.
- **D) 21.59%** - wrong; this is simple-interest thinking — a total gain of 237.5% divided by 11 years, which ignores compounding entirely.

</details>

**Correct: C) Rs 78,68,824**

r = 0.115 ÷ 12 = 0.00958333; n = 13 × 12 = 156. (1.00958333)^156 = 4.42771. FV = 22,000 × [(4.42771 − 1) ÷ 0.00958333] = 22,000 × 357.674 = **Rs 78,68,824**.

- **B) Rs 44,36,824** - wrong; this is the return component alone, without adding back the contributions.

**Correct: A) Rs 1,15,513**

- **B) Rs 1,82,292** - wrong; this is simply 1,75,00,000 ÷ 96, assuming the contributions earn nothing.
- **C) Rs 2,73,013** - wrong; this solves from the PV side (the EMI formula), treating the corpus as a loan to be amortised.
- **D) Rs 96,340** - wrong; this understates the payment, as would follow from an incorrect rate conversion.

**Q13.** Kaushik Shah has a savings account balance of Rs 2,10,000, a liquid fund of Rs 3,90,000 and equity mutual funds of Rs 4,00,000. His monthly household expenses are Rs 1,45,000. What is his liquidity ratio?

Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets ÷ **Monthly** expenses. Equity mutual funds are **not** liquid assets, because their value is volatile. Liquid assets = 2,10,000 + 3,90,000 = Rs 6,00,000. Ratio = 6,00,000 ÷ 1,45,000 = **4.14 months** — inside the healthy 3–6 month band, though not generous.

- **A) 0.34 months** - wrong; this divides by *annual* expenses (1,45,000 × 12) instead of monthly expenses.
- **C) 6.90 months** - wrong; this wrongly counts the Rs 4,00,000 of equity funds as liquid.

**Q14.** Nilima Desai has total assets of Rs 1,86,00,000 and total liabilities of Rs 62,00,000. What are her solvency ratio and leverage ratio respectively?
A) 0.33 and 0.67  B) 0.67 and 0.33  C) 0.50 and 0.50  D) 1.50 and 0.33
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 0.67 and 0.33**

Net worth = 1,86,00,000 − 62,00,000 = Rs 1,24,00,000. Solvency ratio = Net worth ÷ Total assets = 1,24,00,000 ÷ 1,86,00,000 = **0.67**. Leverage ratio = Total liabilities ÷ Total assets = 62,00,000 ÷ 1,86,00,000 = **0.33**. Check: 0.67 + 0.33 = 1.

- **A) 0.33 and 0.67** - wrong; the two ratios are transposed.
- **C) 0.50 and 0.50** - wrong; that would require liabilities to equal exactly half of total assets.
- **D) 1.50 and 0.33** - wrong; 1.50 is net worth divided by liabilities, and no solvency ratio can exceed 1.

</details>

**Q16.** Which statement about a contingency (emergency) fund is correct?
A) It should be sized at 3-6 months of gross income and held largely in equity funds  B) It should be sized at 3-6 months of expenses and held in savings, sweep-in FDs or liquid funds  C) It should be sized at 12 months of income and held in PPF for the tax benefit  D) It becomes unnecessary once the client holds adequate health insurance
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) It should be sized at 3-6 months of expenses and held in savings, sweep-in FDs or liquid funds**

The fund is sized against **expenses**, because expenses are what must continue to be paid. It requires **immediate availability plus certainty of value**; return is secondary.

- **A) 3-6 months of gross income, held in equity** - wrong on both counts: expenses not income, and equity is likely to be down exactly when the fund is needed.
- **C) 12 months of income in PPF** - wrong; PPF is locked in and is not a liquid asset, so it cannot serve as an emergency reserve.
- **D) Unnecessary with health insurance** - wrong; insurance handles medical costs but not job loss, a business downturn or urgent repairs.

</details>

**Q17.** Jignesh's household budget balances comfortably in most months but collapses every March, when the annual motor and health insurance premiums and a wedding-season outflow all fall due. What is the correct budgeting remedy?
A) Increase the credit card limit so the March shortfall can be revolved  B) Reclassify the March items as discretionary and postpone them  C) Divide each such annual expense by 12 and set that amount aside every month  D) Suspend all savings from the budget until the March obligations are cleared
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Divide each such annual expense by 12 and set that amount aside every month**

These are **periodic (lumpy) outflows** — predictable but infrequent, and the most commonly ignored budget category. A budget that balances monthly but ignores them will always break in the month they fall due. The fix is to provide for them monthly.

- **A) Increase the credit card limit** - wrong; revolving a card balance is the most expensive borrowing available (36–48% annualised) and does not fix the structural gap.
- **B) Reclassify as discretionary and postpone** - wrong; insurance premiums are contractual fixed outflows and letting cover lapse destroys the protection layer of the plan.
- **D) Suspend all savings** - wrong; this inverts "pay yourself first" and makes savings the residual, which is the classic failure mode.

</details>

**Q21.** Jignesh has three borrowings: a gold loan, a car loan and a home loan. Which description of the security created is correct?
A) Gold loan is a hypothecation, car loan is a pledge, home loan is a mortgage  B) Gold loan is a pledge, car loan is a hypothecation, home loan is a mortgage  C) Gold loan is a mortgage, car loan is a pledge, home loan is a hypothecation  D) All three are hypothecations because the borrower continues to use the assets
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Gold loan is a pledge, car loan is a hypothecation, home loan is a mortgage**

**Mortgage** = security over **immovable** property. **Pledge** = security over **movable** property where **possession passes to the lender** (the bank holds the gold). **Hypothecation** = security over **movable** property where **possession stays with the borrower** (he keeps driving the car).

- **A) Gold hypothecation, car pledge** - wrong; the two are transposed, since the lender physically holds the gold but not the car.
- **C) Gold mortgage, home hypothecation** - wrong; a mortgage can only be created over immovable property.
- **D) All three hypothecations** - wrong; the borrower does not retain possession of pledged gold, and a house is immovable.

</details>

**Q22.** Jignesh owes Rs 1,80,000 on a credit card at 42% annualised, Rs 4,50,000 on a personal loan at 15% and Rs 9,00,000 on a car loan at 10%. He wants to minimise the total interest he pays. Which repayment strategy should he follow?
A) Snowball - clear the Rs 1,80,000 card first because it is the smallest balance  B) Avalanche - attack the 42% credit card first because it carries the highest rate  C) Blizzard - clear the car loan first because it is the largest exposure  D) Repay all three in equal proportions each month
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Avalanche - attack the 42% credit card first because it carries the highest rate**

The **avalanche** method targets the **highest interest rate first** while paying minimums on the rest. It is mathematically optimal — it minimises total interest paid.

- **A) Snowball** - wrong as stated; snowball targets the smallest *balance* regardless of rate and costs more interest overall, even though it happens to pick the same loan here for the wrong reason.
- **C) Blizzard - clear the car loan first** - wrong; blizzard is a hybrid that clears small balances for momentum and then switches to the highest rate — it never begins with the largest, cheapest loan.
- **D) Equal proportions** - wrong; spreading repayment evenly leaves the 42% balance outstanding longest relative to its cost.

</details>

- **D) Only above 150 clients** - wrong; the **150-client threshold** is when an **individual** RIA must convert to a **non-individual** registration; it has nothing to do with whether registration is needed at all.

**Q81.** Over one year, Bhavesh's broker-adviser moved his Rs 40,00,000 equity holding across nine different large-cap schemes, each time earning fresh commission, with no change in the underlying strategy or in Bhavesh's risk profile. This conduct is best labelled:
A) Mis-selling  B) Churning  C) Front-running  D) Unauthorised transactions
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Churning**

**Churning is excessive trading, switching or turnover in a client's portfolio to generate brokerage or fresh commission.** The give-away sign is **a great deal of activity with no change in the underlying strategy**. Each individual scheme may be perfectly sound; it is the **frequency** that is the offence — and every switch is also a taxable event for the client.

- **A) Mis-selling** - wrong; mis-selling is about recommending the **wrong product** for the client's needs, and a single transaction can amount to it.
- **C) Front-running** - wrong; that means dealing **ahead of a client's known impending order**.
- **D) Unauthorised transactions** - wrong; that requires trades the client never approved, which is not what the facts describe.

**Q82.** Which provision establishes the fiduciary duty of a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser?
A) Regulation 3 of the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013  B) Regulation 15(1) of the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013  C) Section 12A of the SEBI Act, 1992  D) Regulation 4 of the PFUTP Regulations, 2003
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Regulation 15(1) of the SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013**

Regulation 15(1) states that an investment adviser **"shall act in a fiduciary capacity towards its clients and shall disclose all conflicts of interest as and when they arise"**. Its two limbs are the **duty of loyalty** (whose side are you on?) and the **duty of care** (did you do the work?).

- **A) Regulation 3** - wrong; that is the **registration** requirement — no person may act as, or hold himself out as, an investment adviser without a SEBI certificate.
- **C) Section 12A** - wrong; that prohibits manipulative and deceptive devices, insider trading and fraudulent takeovers.
- **D) PFUTP Regulation 4** - wrong; that lists the deemed **fraudulent and unfair trade practices**.

</details>

**Q83.** An adviser learns that a large client will place a substantial buy order in a mid-cap stock the next morning, and buys the stock for his own account that afternoon. This is:
A) Insider trading under the PIT Regulations, 2015  B) Front-running, dealt with under the PFUTP Regulations, 2003  C) Permitted, because no unpublished price-sensitive information about the company was used  D) Churning of the client's account
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Front-running, dealt with under the PFUTP Regulations, 2003**

**Front-running is dealing ahead of a client's known impending order** to profit from the price move that order will cause. It is a deemed fraudulent and unfair trade practice under **Regulation 4 of the PFUTP Regulations, 2003**, attracting a penalty under Section 15HA. The memory hook is: **front-run an ORDER, insider-trade on NEWS.**

- **A) Insider trading** - wrong; PIT applies to **unpublished price-sensitive information about the company itself**, not to knowledge of an order book.
- **C) Permitted** - wrong; the absence of UPSI takes it out of PIT but leaves it squarely inside PFUTP, and it also breaches the adviser's fiduciary duty.
- **D) Churning** - wrong; churning is excessive trading **in the client's own account**.

**Q84.** A SEBI-registered IA's financial year ends on 31 March 2026. Its annual compliance audit report, containing adverse observations, is dated 12 August 2026. By when must the adverse findings and the action taken on them be reported?
A) 12 September 2026  B) 30 September 2026  C) 31 October 2026  D) 31 March 2027
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 12 September 2026**

The audit itself must be **completed within six months of the financial year end** (by 30 September 2026). Separately, **adverse findings together with the action taken, duly approved by the management, must be reported within one month of the date of the audit report** — 12 August plus one month gives **12 September 2026**.

- **B) 30 September 2026** - wrong; that is the deadline for **completing the audit**, not for reporting the action taken on its findings.
- **C) 31 October 2026** - wrong; that is the outer backstop date, but the one-month clock running from the 12 August report expires earlier and governs here.
- **D) 31 March 2027** - wrong; no reporting obligation runs to the next financial year end.

</details>

**Q85.** Under the Code of Conduct for Investment Advisers, what is the correct order of preference for handling a conflict of interest?

**Correct: B) Avoid it, then manage it, and disclose what cannot be avoided**

The Code requires an adviser to **avoid conflicts as far as possible**, and where a conflict is unavoidable, to **ensure fair treatment of clients and disclose it**. Regulation 15(1) reinforces this by requiring disclosure of all conflicts **as and when they arise**. The hierarchy is **avoid, manage, disclose** — in that order.

- **A) Disclose first** - wrong; this inverts the hierarchy and treats disclosure as a substitute for avoidance.
- **C) Disclose only** - wrong; disclosure alone is the weakest response and is not what the Code requires.

### Case 1: The Vaghela household in Ahmedabad

Jignesh Vaghela (52) runs a ceramic-tile trading firm in Ahmedabad and draws Rs 2,60,000 a month from the business. His wife Nirali (49) earns Rs 70,000 a month from a coaching centre she owns. The household spends Rs 2,05,000 a month, of which Rs 62,000 are EMIs on a shop loan and a car loan. Their adviser recommends an emergency fund equal to six months of total household expenses.

**Q91.** What is the household's monthly savings ratio?
A) 37.88%  B) 48.08%  C) 60.98%  D) 62.12%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 37.88%**

Gross monthly income = 2,60,000 + 70,000 = **Rs 3,30,000**. Savings = 3,30,000 − 2,05,000 = **Rs 1,25,000**. Savings ratio = Savings ÷ Gross income = 1,25,000 ÷ 3,30,000 = **37.88%**.

- **B) 48.08%** - wrong; this divides savings by Jignesh's income alone (1,25,000 ÷ 2,60,000), ignoring Nirali's earnings.
- **C) 60.98%** - wrong; this divides savings by expenses (1,25,000 ÷ 2,05,000). The denominator of the savings ratio is income.
- **D) 62.12%** - wrong; this is the *expenses* ratio, 2,05,000 ÷ 3,30,000. Savings ratio and expenses ratio must sum to 100%.

**Q92.** What is the household's solvency ratio?
A) 0.24  B) 0.32  C) 0.76  D) 1.24
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 0.76**

Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities = 3,25,00,000 − 78,00,000 = **Rs 2,47,00,000**. Solvency ratio = Net worth ÷ Total assets = 2,47,00,000 ÷ 3,25,00,000 = **0.76**. A ratio above 0.5 is healthy, so the household is comfortably solvent.

- **A) 0.24** - wrong; this is the *leverage* ratio, Liabilities ÷ Assets = 78,00,000 ÷ 3,25,00,000. Solvency and leverage must sum to 1.
- **B) 0.32** - wrong; this is Liabilities ÷ Net worth = 78,00,000 ÷ 2,47,00,000, which is a debt-to-equity measure, not the solvency ratio.
- **D) 1.24** - wrong; this adds the leverage ratio to 1 instead of subtracting it. A solvency ratio can never exceed 1.

</details>

**Q93.** What is the household's liquidity ratio?
A) 2.73 months  B) 4.88 months  C) 6.99 months  D) 7.80 months
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 4.88 months**

Liquid assets = savings bank 5,60,000 + liquid fund 4,40,000 = **Rs 10,00,000**. The gold ETF is excluded because the family has earmarked it as a long-term holding. Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets ÷ **Monthly** expenses = 10,00,000 ÷ 2,05,000 = **4.88 months**, which sits inside the 3–6 month norm but below the six months the adviser wants.

- **A) 2.73 months** - wrong; this counts only the savings bank balance (5,60,000 ÷ 2,05,000) and drops the liquid fund, which is a genuine emergency asset.
- **C) 6.99 months** - wrong; this divides by non-EMI expenses of 1,43,000 only. EMIs still have to be paid in an emergency, so total expenses are the correct denominator.

**Q94.** What is the household's debt-to-income ratio?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 18.79%**

Debt-to-income ratio = Total EMIs ÷ Gross income = 62,000 ÷ 3,30,000 = **18.79%**. Comfortably below the 35–40% prudential ceiling, so there is headroom for further borrowing.

- **B) 23.85%** - wrong; this uses Jignesh's income alone (62,000 ÷ 2,60,000).

- **D) 62.12%** - wrong; this is the expenses ratio, not the debt servicing ratio.

**Q95.** How much more must the household set aside to reach the emergency fund the adviser recommends?

Six months of total household expenses = 6 × 2,05,000 = **Rs 12,30,000**. Liquid assets already held = **Rs 10,00,000**. Shortfall = 12,30,000 − 10,00,000 = **Rs 2,30,000**.

**Q99.** Using the approximation formula, what is the yield to maturity on the bonds?

Under the SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, 2020 the minimum client investment in a PMS is **Rs 50 lakh**. Under the SEBI (AIF) Regulations, 2012 the minimum investment in an AIF is **Rs 1 crore**. The ladder to memorise is mutual fund Rs 100–5,000, PMS Rs 50 lakh, AIF Rs 1 crore.

**Q107.** What performance fee is payable for year 1?

- **C) Rs 7,20,000** - wrong; this charges 20% of the whole Rs 36,00,000 gain and ignores the hurdle entirely.

**Q108.** What performance fee is payable for year 2?

- **B) Rs 2,40,000** - wrong; this is the year-3 fee.
- **C) Rs 3,00,000** - wrong; this charges 20% of the hurdle amount in a year in which the portfolio lost value.

**Q109.** What performance fee is payable for year 3 on a high watermark basis?

The high watermark is **Rs 1.86 crore**. The year-3 value is Rs 1.98 crore, so the fee-bearing gain is only the amount above the previous peak: 1,98,00,000 − 1,86,00,000 = **Rs 12,00,000**. Fee = 20% × 12,00,000 = **Rs 2,40,000**.

**Q110.** Into which AIF category does the leveraged long-short hedge fund fall, and how is it taxed?

**Q113.** May Bhavesh's wife distribute regular-plan mutual fund schemes to his advisory clients?

**Q114.** What is the maximum annual fee Bhavesh may charge the Vaghela family under the assets-under-advice mode?

**Q115.** Which regulation does the recommendation to the retired schoolteacher breach?
A) None, because she signed a letter accepting the risk  B) Regulation 17, because the advice is not appropriate to the client's assessed risk profile  C) None, because an AIF is a privately placed product outside the scope of investment advice  D) Regulation 16 only, because the risk profile was never communicated to her

**Q116.** What is the current weight of equity in the portfolio?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 63.04%**

Current total = 1,45,00,000 + 62,00,000 + 23,00,000 = **Rs 2,30,00,000**. Equity weight = 1,45,00,000 ÷ 2,30,00,000 = **63.04%**.

- **A) 60.00%** - wrong; this is the target weight, not the actual weight after drift.
- **B) 62.50%** - wrong; no combination of the given figures produces this.
- **D) 72.50%** - wrong; this divides the current equity value by the *old* total of Rs 2,00,00,000. The denominator must be the current portfolio value.

**Q117.** Under the family's threshold rebalancing policy, is a rebalance triggered by the equity drift?
A) Yes, because equity is 3.04 percentage points above target, which breaches the band  B) Yes, because the portfolio value has risen 15%, which breaches the band  C) No, because equity's deviation of 3.04 percentage points is inside the plus or minus 5 percentage point band  D) No, because threshold rebalancing is applied only to the debt allocation
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) No, because equity's deviation of 3.04 percentage points is inside the plus or minus 5 percentage point band**

Equity is at 63.04% against a 60% target, a deviation of **3.04 percentage points**. The band permits up to 5 percentage points either side, i.e. 55%–65%. New target equity = 40% × 2,30,00,000 = **Rs 92,00,000**. Current equity = Rs 1,45,00,000. Equity to sell = 1,45,00,000 − 92,00,000 = **Rs 53,00,000**, moved into debt, whose target rises to 50% × 2,30,00,000 = Rs 1,15,00,000.

**Q120.** How should the move from 60:30:10 to 40:50:10 be classified?
A) A tactical asset allocation shift, because it is a temporary deviation to be reversed later  B) A strategic asset allocation change, because it is driven by a change in the client's circumstances  C) A breach of the Investment Policy Statement, because it moves outside the tolerance band  D) A routine rebalancing trade, because the total portfolio value is unchanged
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) A strategic asset allocation change, because it is driven by a change in the client's circumstances**

The test is simple: **if the reason for the change is something that happened in the client's life, it is strategic; if it is something that happened in the market, it is tactical.** Jignesh's horizon has shortened from eight years to three and a liquidity event is coming — a life event. The IPS is formally amended and the new weights become the long-term policy mix.

- **A) Tactical** - wrong; tactical allocation is a **temporary** deviation exploiting a perceived market mispricing, funded from the strategic weights and then reversed. Nothing here is temporary.
- **C) A breach of the IPS** - wrong; a documented, deliberate revision of the IPS is not a breach. A breach is an unauthorised move outside the bands while the IPS still stands.
- **D) Routine rebalancing** - wrong; rebalancing restores the *existing* targets. Here the targets themselves are being changed.

</details>

### Case 7: Jignesh's retirement plan and a loan against property

### Case 8: Ranking three portfolio managers

Jignesh's family office is evaluating three discretionary managers over an identical five-year period. The risk-free rate is 6.5% per annum, the market returned 13% per annum with a standard deviation of 15% and a beta of 1.00 by definition. The three portfolios recorded:

- **Portfolio Amreli** — return 16.4%, standard deviation 18%, beta 1.15
- **Portfolio Bhuj** — return 13.8%, standard deviation 11%, beta 0.85
- **Portfolio Chandkheda** — return 18.2%, standard deviation 24%, beta 1.40

The family office is also modelling a combined mandate of 65% in Amreli and 35% in Bhuj; the correlation between the two portfolios' returns is 0.35.

**Q126.** By how much does Portfolio Amreli's Sharpe ratio exceed the market's Sharpe ratio?
A) 0.0333  B) 0.1167  C) 0.4333  D) 0.5500
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) 0.1167**

Step 1 — Amreli's Sharpe = (16.4 − 6.5) ÷ 18 = 9.9 ÷ 18 = **0.5500**.
Step 2 — market Sharpe = (13.0 − 6.5) ÷ 15 = 6.5 ÷ 15 = **0.4333**.
Excess = 0.5500 − 0.4333 = **0.1167**. Amreli earned more return per unit of total risk than the index did.

- **A) 0.0333** - wrong; this subtracts using a market Sharpe wrongly computed as 6.5 ÷ 12.5 or similar; the market's denominator is its 15% standard deviation.
- **C) 0.4333** - wrong; this is the market's Sharpe ratio itself.
- **D) 0.5500** - wrong; this is Amreli's Sharpe ratio, not the excess over the market.

</details>

**Q127.** Which portfolio has the highest Treynor ratio, and what is it?
A) Amreli, 8.61%  B) Bhuj, 8.59%  C) Chandkheda, 8.36%  D) Chandkheda, 11.70%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) Amreli, 8.61%**

Treynor = (Rp − Rf) ÷ beta.
Amreli = (16.4 − 6.5) ÷ 1.15 = 9.9 ÷ 1.15 = **8.61%**
Bhuj = (13.8 − 6.5) ÷ 0.85 = 7.3 ÷ 0.85 = **8.59%**
Chandkheda = (18.2 − 6.5) ÷ 1.40 = 11.7 ÷ 1.40 = **8.36%**
**Amreli wins, narrowly.** Note that it did *not* win on Sharpe — the two measures can disagree because one divides by total risk and the other only by systematic risk.

- **B) Bhuj, 8.59%** - wrong; the value is correct for Bhuj but it is second, not first.
- **C) Chandkheda, 8.36%** - wrong; the value is correct for Chandkheda but it is the lowest of the three.
- **D) Chandkheda, 11.70%** - wrong; 11.70 is Chandkheda's excess return in percentage points, before dividing by its beta of 1.40.

</details>

**Q128.** Which portfolio delivered the highest Jensen's alpha, and what is it?
A) Amreli, 2.43%  B) Bhuj, 1.78%  C) Chandkheda, 2.60%  D) Chandkheda, 5.20%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Chandkheda, 2.60%**

Alpha = Rp − [Rf + beta × (Rm − Rf)], with the market risk premium = 13.0 − 6.5 = **6.5%**.
Amreli = 16.4 − [6.5 + 1.15 × 6.5] = 16.4 − 13.975 = **2.43%**
Bhuj = 13.8 − [6.5 + 0.85 × 6.5] = 13.8 − 12.025 = **1.78%**
Chandkheda = 18.2 − [6.5 + 1.40 × 6.5] = 18.2 − 15.60 = **2.60%**
**Chandkheda has the highest alpha** even though it had the worst Sharpe and the worst Treynor — alpha rewards raw excess return over the CAPM prediction and is not scaled by the risk taken.

- **A) Amreli, 2.43%** - wrong; the value is correct for Amreli but it ranks second.
- **B) Bhuj, 1.78%** - wrong; the value is correct for Bhuj but it ranks third.
- **D) Chandkheda, 5.20%** - wrong; this is 18.2 − 13.0, the excess over the market return, which ignores beta and is not Jensen's alpha.

</details>

**Q129.** For the 65:35 combination of Amreli and Bhuj, by how much does the portfolio's standard deviation fall below the weighted average of the two standard deviations?
A) 2.01 percentage points  B) 4.46 percentage points  C) 13.54 percentage points  D) 15.55 percentage points
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A) 2.01 percentage points**

Step 1 — portfolio standard deviation:
σp = √(0.65² × 0.18² + 0.35² × 0.11² + 2 × 0.65 × 0.35 × 0.18 × 0.11 × 0.35)
 = √(0.013689 + 0.001482 + 0.003153) = √0.018324 = **13.54%**
Step 2 — weighted average of the two standard deviations = 0.65 × 18 + 0.35 × 11 = 11.70 + 3.85 = **15.55%**
Reduction = 15.55 − 13.54 = **2.01 percentage points**, obtained for free because the correlation of 0.35 is below 1.

- **B) 4.46 percentage points** - wrong; this compares the portfolio's 13.54% with Amreli's own 18% standard deviation rather than with the weighted average.
- **C) 13.54 percentage points** - wrong; this is the portfolio standard deviation itself, not the reduction.
- **D) 15.55 percentage points** - wrong; this is the weighted average standard deviation, not the reduction.

</details>

**Q130.** Ranked from best to worst on the Sharpe ratio, what is the correct order?
A) Amreli, Bhuj, Chandkheda  B) Bhuj, Amreli, Chandkheda  C) Chandkheda, Amreli, Bhuj  D) Chandkheda, Bhuj, Amreli

**Correct: B) Bhuj, Amreli, Chandkheda**

Bhuj = (13.8 − 6.5) ÷ 11 = 7.3 ÷ 11 = **0.6636**

Sharpe is the right yardstick here because the family office would hold one manager as a standalone portfolio, so total risk is what the client actually bears.

- **A) Amreli, Bhuj, Chandkheda** - wrong; this is close but reverses the top two. Bhuj's much lower volatility outweighs its lower return.
- **C) Chandkheda, Amreli, Bhuj** - wrong; this ranks by raw return and ignores risk entirely.

### Case 9: A bond and a hedge on a single stock

Jignesh is offered a corporate bond of Rs 1,000 face value carrying a coupon of 7.8% paid annually, with exactly 5 years to maturity, redeemable at par. The market's required yield on comparable paper is 8.6%. The dealer quotes the bond's modified duration as 4.05, and the treasury desk expects yields on this segment to rise by 60 basis points over the next quarter.

Jignesh separately holds 400 shares of Mundra Logistics Ltd bought at Rs 1,390 a share; the stock trades at Rs 1,420 today. He is weighing two hedges over the same expiry: **(i)** selling one futures contract of 400 shares at Rs 1,448, or **(ii)** buying one put option contract of 400 shares with a strike of Rs 1,400 at a premium of Rs 38 a share. Assume the stock settles at Rs 1,375 on the expiry date and that in each case the shares themselves are still held.

**Q131.** What is the fair price of the bond at the required yield of 8.6%?
A) Rs 960.00  B) Rs 968.56  C) Rs 1,000.00  D) Rs 1,032.11
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 968.56**

Step 1 — discount factor: (1.086)^5 = **1.510589**, so 1 ÷ 1.510589 = **0.661989**.
Step 2 — annuity factor for the coupons = (1 − 0.661989) ÷ 0.086 = 0.338011 ÷ 0.086 = **3.930359**.
PV of coupons = 78 × 3.930359 = **Rs 306.57**; PV of redemption = 1,000 × 0.661989 = **Rs 661.99**.
Price = 306.57 + 661.99 = **Rs 968.56**. Coupon (7.8%) is below the required yield (8.6%), so the bond correctly prices at a **discount**.

- **A) Rs 960.00** - wrong; this approximates the discount as 5 years × the 0.8 percentage point yield gap × Rs 1,000, which ignores discounting altogether.
- **C) Rs 1,000.00** - wrong; the bond would price at par only if the coupon equalled the required yield.
- **D) Rs 1,032.11** - wrong; this swaps the coupon and the yield (8.6% coupon discounted at 7.8%), which produces a premium instead of a discount.

</details>

**Q132.** What is the current yield on the bond at that price?
A) 7.56%  B) 7.80%  C) 8.05%  D) 8.60%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) 8.05%**

Step 1 — the price from the valuation is **Rs 968.56**.
Step 2 — current yield = Annual coupon ÷ Market price = 78 ÷ 968.56 = **8.05%**.
For a discount bond the ordering Coupon (7.80%) < Current yield (8.05%) < YTM (8.60%) must hold, and it does — a useful check on the price computed in the previous question.

- **A) 7.56%** - wrong; this divides the coupon by Rs 1,032.11, the price produced by swapping coupon and yield.
- **B) 7.80%** - wrong; this is the coupon rate, which is measured on face value. Current yield uses market price.
- **D) 8.60%** - wrong; this is the required yield, that is the YTM, which also captures the pull to par at redemption.

</details>

**Q133.** If yields rise by 60 basis points as the treasury desk expects, what will the bond be worth?
A) Rs 921.49  B) Rs 945.02  C) Rs 968.56  D) Rs 992.09
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Rs 945.02**

Step 1 — percentage price change ≈ −Modified duration × Δy = −4.05 × 0.0060 = **−2.43%**.
Step 2 — new price = 968.56 × (1 − 0.0243) = 968.56 − 23.54 = **Rs 945.02**.

- **A) Rs 921.49** - wrong; this uses a yield change of 120 basis points. Sixty basis points is 0.60%, that is 0.0060 in decimal.
- **C) Rs 968.56** - wrong; this is the price before the yield change.
- **D) Rs 992.09** - wrong; this adds 2.43% instead of subtracting it. Prices move **inversely** to yields.

</details>

**Q134.** Under hedge (i), what is the combined profit or loss on the shares and the futures position at expiry?
A) Profit of Rs 18,000  B) Profit of Rs 23,200  C) Profit of Rs 29,200  D) Loss of Rs 6,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B) Profit of Rs 23,200**

Step 1 — the shares: bought at Rs 1,390, settle at Rs 1,375, so (1,375 − 1,390) × 400 = **−Rs 6,000**.
Step 2 — the short futures: sold at Rs 1,448, settle at Rs 1,375, so (1,448 − 1,375) × 400 = **+Rs 29,200**.
Combined = 29,200 − 6,000 = **profit of Rs 23,200**. The futures were sold above spot, so the basis of Rs 28 a share was itself locked in as gain.

- **A) Profit of Rs 18,000** - wrong; this measures the futures gain from today's spot of Rs 1,420 rather than the Rs 1,448 at which the contract was actually sold, and then omits the loss on the shares.
- **C) Profit of Rs 29,200** - wrong; this is the futures leg alone and ignores the Rs 6,000 loss on the underlying shares.
- **D) Loss of Rs 6,000** - wrong; this is the unhedged position on the shares alone, which is what the hedge was put on to avoid.

</details>

**Q135.** Under hedge (ii), what is the combined profit or loss on the shares and the put option at expiry?
A) Loss of Rs 5,200  B) Loss of Rs 6,000  C) Loss of Rs 11,200  D) Profit of Rs 10,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C) Loss of Rs 11,200**

Step 1 — the put: intrinsic value at expiry = max(1,400 − 1,375, 0) = **Rs 25** a share, so gross payoff = 25 × 400 = **Rs 10,000**. Premium paid = 38 × 400 = **Rs 15,200**. Net on the option = 10,000 − 15,200 = **−Rs 5,200**.
Step 2 — the shares: (1,375 − 1,390) × 400 = **−Rs 6,000**.
Combined = −5,200 − 6,000 = **loss of Rs 11,200**. The put finished in the money but not profitable — the break-even spot for the long put was 1,400 − 38 = Rs 1,362, and the stock never got there. In-the-money is not the same as profitable.

- **A) Loss of Rs 5,200** - wrong; this is the option leg alone and omits the loss on the shares.
- **B) Loss of Rs 6,000** - wrong; this is the shares alone and omits the cost of the option.
- **D) Profit of Rs 10,000** - wrong; this takes the put's gross intrinsic payoff and forgets both the Rs 15,200 premium and the share loss.

</details>

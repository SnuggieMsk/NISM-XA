## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy: Recall (Q1–Q35)

**Q1.** Which decision explains the largest share of a portfolio's return variability?
A) Security selection  B) Asset allocation  C) Market timing  D) Broker selection
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — **Asset allocation** dominates; the empirical studies put it around **90%** of return variability over time.
A) Security selection explains only a small residual. C) Timing is a small and usually negative contributor after costs. D) Broker selection affects cost, not the fundamental risk-return profile.
</details>

**Q2.** Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986) found that asset allocation policy explained approximately:
A) 46% of the variation in quarterly returns  B) 60% of the variation in quarterly returns  C) 93.6% of the variation in quarterly returns  D) 25% of the variation in quarterly returns
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Studying **91 large US pension plans over 1974–1983**, they attributed **93.6%** of the variation in quarterly returns to asset allocation policy.
A), B) and D) all understate the finding, which is famous precisely because the number is so high.
</details>

**Q3.** Ibbotson and Kaplan (2000) concluded that asset allocation explains roughly:
A) 90% over time, 40% across funds, 100% of the return level  B) 100% of everything  C) 40% over time and 90% across funds  D) 50% of all three
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Their paper separated three distinct questions and produced the **90 / 40 / 100** answer.
B) Overstates it; selection and timing do matter at the margin. C) Reverses the two figures — variability **over time** is the high number. D) Invents a single figure that appears in none of the studies.
</details>

**Q4.** The correlation coefficient between two assets must lie between:
A) 0 and 1  B) −1 and +1  C) −100 and +100  D) There is no limit
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Correlation is covariance standardised, and is always bounded by **−1 and +1**.
A) Negative correlation is possible and valuable. C) Confuses correlation with a percentage. D) Covariance is unbounded; correlation is not.
</details>

**Q5.** If two assets have a correlation of exactly +1, the diversification benefit is:
A) Maximum  B) Nil — portfolio risk equals the weighted average  C) Negative  D) Doubled
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — At **ρ = +1** the risk formula collapses to a straight weighted average; nothing is saved.
A) Maximum benefit occurs at ρ = −1. C) Diversification never increases risk above the weighted average. D) Has no meaning here.
</details>

**Q6.** At which correlation can portfolio risk theoretically be reduced to zero?
A) ρ = 0  B) ρ = +1  C) ρ = −1  D) ρ = +0.5
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Only at **perfect negative correlation** can the two risk contributions exactly cancel, with the right weights.
A) Zero correlation gives a large but not complete reduction. B) Gives none at all. D) Gives a modest reduction.
</details>

</details>

**Q8.** During a severe market crisis, correlations between risky assets tend to:
A) Fall towards −1  B) Rise towards +1  C) Stay exactly the same  D) Become undefined
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — In 2008 and in March 2020 almost everything risky fell together: **diversification fails when it is most needed**.

</details>

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium: Application (Q36–Q70)

**Q36.** Which of these does NOT belong in an Investment Policy Statement?

**Correct: B)** — Anything that must be rewritten every month has no place in a document meant to survive market cycles.

**Q37.** The Nifty falls 25% in three months. The IPS should be:
A) Rewritten immediately to cut equity  B) Left unchanged — this is what it was written for  C) Discarded  D) Rewritten to raise equity
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — An IPS is revised on **life events**, not market events. Rewriting the plan while it is being tested is the most expensive mistake in personal finance.

</details>

**Q38.** When a client's ability and willingness to take risk conflict, the adviser should generally:

**Correct: B)** — **The lower governs.** The adviser may educate but must never override.

</details>

**QC2.** Her ₹30,00,000 unlisted stake breaches the 10% single-security limit by how much, and what is the deeper problem?
A) By ₹18,00,000; her income comes from the same business  B) By ₹30,00,000; unlisted shares are always prohibited  C) By ₹12,00,000; the stake is simply too large  D) There is no breach
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — The cap is 10% × ₹1,20,00,000 = **₹12,00,000**, so the excess is 30,00,000 − 12,00,000 = **₹18,00,000**. The deeper problem is that her **professional income and her largest holding depend on the same business** — human capital and financial capital are exposed to a single event. The stake is also **illiquid**, so it cannot be trimmed quickly and must be unwound over years.
B) Unlisted shares are not prohibited; the IPS simply caps them. C) ₹12,00,000 is the permitted amount, not the excess. D) At 25% of the corpus the limit is clearly breached.
</details>

**QC4.** Given her year-end sleeve values, is the equity band breached, and what trade restores the target?
A) No breach; no action needed  B) Breached at 60.11%; sell ₹7,48,000 of equity  C) Breached at 60.11%; sell ₹13,00,000 of equity  D) Breached at 62.50%; sell ₹7,48,000 of equity
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Total = 88,00,000 + 45,00,000 + 13,40,000 = **₹1,46,40,000**. Equity weight = 88,00,000 ÷ 1,46,40,000 = **60.11%**, a drift of **5.11 percentage points** — just **outside** the ±5 point band. Target equity = 55% × 1,46,40,000 = **₹80,52,000**, so **sell ₹7,48,000**. That is redeployed as ₹6,24,000 to debt (to reach ₹51,24,000) and ₹1,24,000 to gold (to reach ₹14,64,000).
A) The drift is 5.11 points, which exceeds the ±5 point band. C) Sells far more than the target requires — ₹13,00,000 would push equity below 55%. D) Miscalculates the weight: 88,00,000 ÷ 1,46,40,000 is 60.11%, not 62.50%.
</details>

**QC5.** Her portfolio returned 22.0% against a 20.55% benchmark. If the ₹7,48,000 sale would realise a long-term gain of ₹2,48,000 with no exemption used, what should the adviser recommend?
A) Sell and pay the ₹15,375 of tax, since there is no alternative  B) Direct her ₹18,00,000 annual surplus into debt and gold instead, rebalancing with no sale, no tax and no exit load  C) Skip rebalancing entirely, since she outperformed  D) Sell the unlisted stake first to fund the rebalancing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Rebalancing with **new money** is the cheapest method available. Her ₹18,00,000 annual surplus comfortably covers the ₹7,48,000 needed on the underweight side, so the target weights are restored with **no selling, no capital-gains tax and no exit load**. *(For reference, selling would cost 12.5% × (2,48,000 − 1,25,000) = 12.5% × ₹1,23,000 = **₹15,375**, plus any exit load and impact cost.)*

**The wider lesson:** her **+1.45 percentage points** of outperformance is small relative to the decisions that really mattered this year — holding 55% equity in a 32% equity market, and controlling the ₹18,00,000 concentration excess. **Allocation and concentration dominate; selection is the residual.**

A) There is a cheaper alternative, and an adviser who ignores it is leaving money on the table. C) A good year is exactly when equity drifts overweight and risk rises above the agreed level. D) The unlisted stake is illiquid and must be unwound gradually over several tax years, not used as a source of ready cash.
</details>

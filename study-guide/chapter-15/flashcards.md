# Chapter 15: Portfolio Construction Process — Flashcards

Cover the answer, recall it, then check. **The IPS constituents, the RRTTLLU framework and the strategic-vs-tactical distinction are near-certain exam marks.**

**Q1.** Which of the three investment decisions — asset allocation, security selection, market timing — dominates a portfolio's outcome, and what is the empirical evidence?
> **A:** **Asset allocation**, by a wide margin. **Brinson, Hood & Beebower (1986)** studied **91 US pension plans over 1974–1983** and found asset allocation policy explained **93.6% of the variation in quarterly returns**; **Brinson, Singer & Beebower (1991)** confirmed it at about **91.5%**.

**Q2.** What did Ibbotson and Kaplan (2000) add, and what is the classic misreading of Brinson?
> **A:** They separated three questions: asset allocation explains about **90%** of return variability **over time**, about **40%** of the variation **across funds**, and about **100%** of the **level** of long-run return. The misreading is treating "93.6%" as **93.6% of the return** — it is **93.6% of the variability (variance)** of returns.

**Q3.** What is the range of the correlation coefficient, how does gold behave, and what happens to correlations in a crisis?
> **A:** **ρ runs from −1 to +1**: at **+1** there is **no diversification benefit**; at **−1** risk can be driven to **zero**. **Gold's correlation with Indian equity is low to sometimes negative** — a **safe haven** hedging inflation and rupee depreciation (typically **5–10%** of a portfolio). In a crisis (2008, March 2020) **correlations rise towards +1** — **diversification fails exactly when it is most needed**.

**Q4.** A ₹10 lakh portfolio is 60% equity (σ = 18%) and 40% debt (σ = 5%), with ρ = 0.15. What is portfolio risk versus the weighted average?
> **A:** σ_p = √(0.36×324 + 0.16×25 + 2×0.6×0.4×18×5×0.15) = √(116.64 + 4 + 6.48) = √127.12 = **11.27%**. The weighted average is 0.6×18 + 0.4×5 = **12.80%** — diversification saved **1.53 percentage points of risk for free**.

**Q5.** What is an Investment Policy Statement, and what is the first step of the portfolio construction process?
> **A:** The IPS is the **written, jointly agreed and signed document** governing the client–adviser relationship — objectives, constraints, guidelines, target allocation, benchmark and review process. The first step is always **understanding the client**; capital market expectations are formed **after** the IPS, never before.

**Q6.** What is the single greatest practical value of an IPS, and name four other benefits.
> **A:** **Discipline in a crisis** — rules written in calm conditions are what stop a client abandoning the plan in a crash. Others: a **yardstick (benchmark)** for honest evaluation; **continuity** if the adviser changes; **fewer disputes** because both parties signed; and a **compliance record** of risk profiling and suitability.

**Q7.** List the main constituents of an IPS.
> **A:** Client background; purpose and scope; **duties and responsibilities**; **investment objectives**; **investment constraints**; **investment guidelines and exposure limits**; the **strategic asset allocation with bands**; the **benchmark and evaluation process**; the **rebalancing policy**; the **review procedure**; and appendices (fees, risk profile, holdings).

**Q8.** What does RRTTLLU stand for?
> **A:** **R**eturn and **R**isk (the two **objectives**), then **T**ime horizon, **T**axes, **L**iquidity, **L**egal & regulatory and **U**nique circumstances (the five **constraints**).

**Q9.** Which events require an IPS to be revised — and which do not?
> **A:** **Life events** do: marriage, divorce, a birth, a death, job loss, inheritance, illness, or a goal changing. A **market fall does NOT** — that is precisely the situation the IPS was written for.

**Q10.** Distinguish required from desired return, and compute the required return for a retiree with ₹1 crore withdrawing ₹4 lakh a year, with 6% inflation and 1% costs.
> **A:** The **required return** is what the client **must** earn for essential goals — a **need**; the **desired return** is a **want** that can be abandoned. Spending rate = 4,00,000 ÷ 1,00,00,000 = **4%**. Additive approximation = 4 + 6 + 1 = **11%**; exact = (1.04 × 1.06 × 1.01) − 1 = **11.34%**. **Costs are added to the target, never ignored.**

**Q11.** Distinguish ability to take risk from willingness to take risk.
> **A:** **Ability (capacity)** is **objective and financial** — wealth relative to goals, time horizon, income stability, liabilities, dependants. **Willingness (tolerance)** is **subjective and psychological** — personality and past experience of loss.

**Q12.** What does an adviser do when ability and willingness conflict?
> **A:** **The lower of the two governs.** The adviser may **educate** but must **never override**. The two errors are not symmetric: overriding low **willingness** produces a client who panics and sells; overriding low **ability** produces a client who is ruined.

**Q13.** Name the five investment constraints.
> **A:** **Liquidity**, **time horizon**, **tax**, **legal & regulatory**, and **unique circumstances and preferences**.

**Q14.** What does the liquidity constraint mean, what is the baseline, and which Indian holdings score badly on it?
> **A:** Convertibility to cash **quickly at a fair price** — not "safety". Baseline: an **emergency fund of 3–6 months' expenses** (6–12 if income is irregular). Poor liquidity: **physical property**, **unlisted shares**, **PPF** (15 years), **ELSS** (**3-year lock-in**), **NPS** (until 60), **SGBs** (8-year tenor, exit from year 5).

**Q15.** How does the time horizon affect risk capacity, and what is the trap?
> **A:** A **longer horizon raises the ability to take risk**, because there is time to recover. The trap: it is the **goal's** horizon, not the investor's **age**, that decides — a 30-year-old buying a flat in two years should hold **almost no equity** for that money.

**Q16.** State the current Indian tax treatment of listed equity and of debt mutual funds bought on or after 1 April 2023.
> **A:** Listed equity/equity funds: **STCG 20%** if held ≤ 12 months; **LTCG 12.5%** on gains above **₹1,25,000** a year if held > 12 months. Debt funds bought on or after **1 April 2023** are taxed at the investor's **slab rate**, with no long-term benefit.

**Q17.** Give four examples of a legal or regulatory constraint, and distinguish it from a unique preference.
> **A:** Charitable **trusts** limited to modes permitted by **Section 11(5)** of the Income-tax Act; **PFRDA** capping NPS Active Choice equity at **75%** (tapering after 50) and alternatives at **5%**; **FEMA** barring NRIs from newly opening PPF; **insider-trading rules** on employees of listed firms. A **legal constraint is imposed from outside** regardless of the client's wishes; a **unique preference is the client's own choice** and can be relaxed by them.

**Q18.** What is the purpose of exposure limits, and at which four levels are they set?
> **A:** To control **concentration risk**. Limits are set on a **single security/issuer**, a **single sector**, a **single entity or group**, and a **single asset class** — and must be written into the IPS **before** a position becomes a favourite.

**Q19.** State SEBI's key exposure limits for mutual funds.
> **A:** **10% of NAV** in the equity of any one company (index and sector funds exempt); **10% of NAV** in the debt of a single issuer, extendable to **12%** with trustee and board approval; **20% of NAV** to a single sector; and no more than **10% of a company's paid-up voting capital** across all schemes. **G-Secs and T-bills are exempt.**

**Q20.** Why must exposure limits be applied to the client's whole balance sheet?
> **A:** Because **human capital is exposure too**. An employee holding large ESOPs has their **salary and their savings tied to the same single event** — if the employer fails, both disappear in the same week.

**Q21.** What do the three letters of ESG cover, which pillar has mattered most in India, and what are the six ESG approaches?
> **A:** **E**nvironmental (emissions, energy, waste), **S**ocial (labour, safety, human rights, data privacy), **G**overnance (board independence, pay, **related-party transactions**, promoter pledging). **Governance** has mattered most — almost every large Indian investor loss was a governance failure. The six approaches: **negative/exclusionary screening, positive/best-in-class screening, ESG integration, thematic investing, impact investing, and active ownership/stewardship**.

**Q22.** What is BRSR, and what is SEBI's minimum-investment rule for an ESG mutual fund scheme?
> **A:** The **Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report**, which SEBI requires from the **top 1,000 listed companies by market capitalisation**. An ESG scheme must invest a **minimum of 80% of total assets** in securities following its stated ESG strategy, and each ESG scheme of a fund house must follow a **distinct strategy**.

**Q23.** How does ethical investing differ from ESG investing, and what must the adviser do about an ethical screen?
> **A:** **ESG** treats the factors as **financially material risks**; **ethical investing puts values first** and explicitly **accepts a possible return sacrifice**, usually by **excluding "sin" sectors** — tobacco, alcohol, gambling, weapons, adult entertainment. The adviser must **implement it, record it in the IPS under unique needs, and disclose the consequences**: narrower universe, sector bias, higher tracking error.

**Q24.** What does Shariah-compliant investing prohibit?
> **A:** **Riba (interest)** — so conventional banks, NBFCs and insurers are excluded — plus alcohol, pork, gambling and tobacco businesses. It applies **financial screens** on debt and interest income, and requires **purification** (donating the impermissible portion of income). Indian implementations include the **Nifty50 Shariah** and **BSE 500 Shariah** indices.

**Q25.** Why must every goal be inflated, and what is the correct order of financial planning?
> **A:** A goal in today's rupees is not what will be needed: ₹15 lakh of education cost in **14 years at 8% education inflation** becomes 15,00,000 × (1.08)¹⁴ = **about ₹44 lakh**. Order: **emergency fund → health cover → term life cover → repay costly debt → then invest**.

**Q26.** Define net worth and cash flow, and give two ratio benchmarks.
> **A:** **Net worth = Assets − Liabilities** (a **stock**, at a point in time). **Cash flow = Inflows − Outflows** (a **flow**, over a period). **Basic liquidity ratio** = liquid assets ÷ monthly expenses, target **3–6**; **debt-to-income** = EMIs ÷ income, below about **40%**.

**Q27.** Describe the Barnewall two-way psychographic model.
> **A:** **Passive investors** acquired wealth **passively** — inheritance, or a salaried professional career — and are **more risk-averse, security first**. **Active investors** earned wealth by their **own risk-taking** (entrepreneurs) and are **more risk-tolerant and want control**. It classifies by **how they got the money**.

**Q28.** What are the two axes of the Bailard, Biehl and Kaiser model, and the five types?
> **A:** Axes: **confident ↔ anxious** (level of confidence) and **careful ↔ impetuous** (method of action). Types: **Adventurer**, **Celebrity**, **Individualist**, **Guardian**, **Straight Arrow**.

**Q29.** In the BB&K model, who is hardest to advise, who is easiest, and who most needs an adviser?
> **A:** **Hardest — the Adventurer** (confident + impetuous; big concentrated bets; dislikes advice). **Easiest — the Individualist** (confident + careful; does their own research; rational). **Most needs one — the Celebrity** (anxious + impetuous; follows fashions and tips; has no views of their own).

**Q30.** Name the four life-cycle phases and the risk capacity of each.
> **A:** **Accumulation** (25–40, **highest** capacity, equity-heavy); **Consolidation** (40–55, moderate to high, growth plus preservation); **Spending** (retirement, lower but **not zero** — still needs equity to beat inflation); **Gifting** (estate planning, driven by the **beneficiary's** horizon).

**Q31.** Why does the recommended equity weight fall with age, and what is the exception?
> **A:** Because **human capital** — a long, bond-like stream of future salary — **disappears**. A young investor already holds a huge bond-like asset outside the portfolio, so the portfolio tilts to equity to balance it. **Exception:** someone whose income is itself **equity-like** (a business owner, a commission earner, an ESOP-heavy employee) should hold **less** equity than "100 − age" suggests.

**Q32.** How are asset-class returns forecast, and what are the main forecasting problems?
> **A:** For **debt**, the **current yield to maturity** is the best estimate of a hold-to-maturity return. For **equity**: **dividend yield + expected inflation + real earnings growth ± the expected change in valuation (P/E)**. Problems: **regime change**, a **short reliable data history** (the Nifty 50 dates from 1996), **survivorship bias**, **recency and overconfidence**, and **stale prices on illiquid assets**, which **understate** true volatility and correlation.

**Q33.** What do the seven letters of SAMURAI stand for, and why does a peer-group benchmark fail it?
> **A:** A valid benchmark is **S**pecified in advance, **A**ppropriate, **M**easurable, **U**nambiguous, **R**eflective of current investment opinions, **A**ccountable and **I**nvestable. A peer-group benchmark fails the **"I"** — you cannot passively hold "the median fund" — and suffers **survivorship bias**.

**Q34.** What are SEBI's two key rules on mutual fund benchmarks?
> **A:** Benchmarking must be against the **Total Return Index (TRI)**, which includes dividends — mandated from **1 February 2018**. And schemes must disclose a **two-tier benchmark**: **Tier 1**, a broad index reflecting the scheme's **category**; **Tier 2**, an optional index reflecting the manager's **style or strategy**.

**Q35.** A client's SAA is 60% equity, 30% debt, 10% gold. Nifty 500 TRI returned 18%, the bond index 7.5% and gold 12%. What is the benchmark return?
> **A:** (0.60 × 18) + (0.30 × 7.5) + (0.10 × 12) = 10.80 + 2.25 + 1.20 = **14.25%**. A **blended benchmark weighted by the strategic asset allocation** is the only fair yardstick for a multi-asset portfolio.

**Q36.** Distinguish strategic from tactical asset allocation, and give the cleanest test.
> **A:** **SAA** is the **long-term policy mix** (5 years+) set by the **client's** goals and constraints; it changes rarely and explains most of return variability. **TAA** is a **short-term deviation within the IPS bands** driven by **market** views, and it must be reversed. **The test:** a change in the **client's life** ⇒ **strategic**; a change in the **market** ⇒ **tactical**. Moving **outside the band is neither — it is a breach of the IPS**.

**Q37.** Contrast buy-and-hold, constant mix and CPPI, and compute a CPPI allocation.
> **A:** **Buy and hold** — never rebalance, weights drift; best in trending markets. **Constant mix** — rebalance to fixed weights, **buying the falling asset**; best in **oscillating** markets. **CPPI** — Equity = **m × (Assets − Floor)**, so it **sells the falling asset**; best in **trending** markets. With floor ₹80 lakh, m = 3 and assets ₹1 crore: cushion ₹20 lakh, **equity = ₹60 lakh**.

**Q38.** State SEBI's cap definitions and explain the core-satellite approach.
> **A:** By **full market capitalisation**: **large cap = 1st–100th**, **mid cap = 101st–250th**, **small cap = 251st onwards**; **AMFI** publishes the binding list every **six months**. **Core-satellite:** a **core of 70–80%** in low-cost broad index and large/flexi-cap funds delivers the market return reliably, while **satellites of 20–30%** in mid-cap, thematic or international funds chase alpha — small enough that a failure is survivable.

**Q39.** What are the three debt-portfolio decisions, and what does a ₹20 lakh portfolio of modified duration 6 lose if yields rise 0.50%?
> **A:** **Duration** (interest-rate risk), **credit quality** (default risk) and **liquidity**. Price change ≈ **− modified duration × change in yield** = −6 × 0.50 = **−3.0%** = **₹60,000**. At duration 2 it would be **₹20,000** — the **duration decision matters more than the rate move**.

**Q40.** Distinguish laddering, bullet, barbell and immunisation.
> **A:** **Ladder** — maturities spread **evenly**, giving regular natural liquidity. **Bullet** — maturities concentrated at **one date**, to fund a known liability. **Barbell** — **short and long only**, nothing in between. **Immunisation** — **match portfolio duration to the horizon** so **price risk and reinvestment risk offset**.

**Q41.** Give the equity limits for the main hybrid categories, and the two real advantages of a hybrid fund.
> **A:** **Conservative hybrid 10–25%** equity; **aggressive hybrid 65–80%** equity and **20–35%** debt; **multi-asset — at least three asset classes with a minimum 10% each**. Advantages: **internal rebalancing is not a taxable event for the investor**, and it **removes behavioural risk** because the client never sees the equity sleeve fall in isolation. *(A scheme is taxed as equity only if it holds a minimum of **65%** in Indian equities — the tax boundary drives the product design.)*

**Q42.** What is rebalancing, what is its primary purpose, and what are the three methods?
> **A:** **Restoring the portfolio to its strategic target weights** after drift. Its primary purpose is **risk control**; any return benefit is a **by-product**. It is **inherently contrarian** — sell the winner, buy the loser. Methods: **calendar** (fixed dates — simple, ignores what happens between them); **percentage-of-portfolio / threshold** (act when a class breaches its **tolerance band**); and a **combination** — check on the calendar, act only if outside the band.

**Q43.** A 60:40 portfolio of ₹40 lakh sees equity return +30% and debt +7%. What is the new equity weight, and what trade restores the target?
> **A:** Equity = 24 × 1.30 = **₹31,20,000**; debt = 16 × 1.07 = **₹17,12,000**; total = **₹48,32,000**; equity weight = **64.57%**. Target equity = 60% × 48,32,000 = ₹28,99,200, so **sell ₹2,20,800 of equity** and buy debt. *(With a ±5 percentage-point band, the 4.57 point drift would NOT trigger a trade.)*

**Q44.** In which markets does rebalancing add value, and in which does it cost value?
> **A:** It **adds** value — the "rebalancing bonus" — in **volatile, mean-reverting (oscillating)** markets, because it sells high and buys low. It **costs** value in **strongly trending** markets, because it keeps trimming an asset that never stops rising. **Rebalancing is not a money machine; its dependable benefit is risk control.**

**Q45.** List the difficulties of rebalancing, and how a good adviser reduces its cost.
> **A:** **Difficulties:** transaction costs (brokerage, STT, stamp duty, exit loads, impact cost); **taxes**, since every sale is a taxable event; **behavioural resistance**; **underperformance in trending markets**; **illiquid and locked assets** (PPF, EPF, NPS, ELSS, SGBs, property) that cannot be rebalanced; and choosing the right band. **Cheapest fix:** direct **new contributions and SIPs into the underweight class** and take **withdrawals from the overweight class** — no selling, no tax, no exit load. Also rebalance **inside NPS/EPF**, use **hybrid funds**, and **harvest gains within the ₹1.25 lakh LTCG exemption**.

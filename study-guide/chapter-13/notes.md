# Chapter 13: Overview of Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs) — Short Notes

> **Module 4 · Investment Through Managed Portfolio (23 marks across Chapters 11–13).**
> This chapter is almost pure **numbers and categories**. If you can place any fund into **Category I, II or III** and recite the **₹1 crore / ₹25 lakh / ₹20 crore / 1000 investors / 2.5%-₹5 crore vs 5%-₹10 crore** set, you have most of the marks. The single sharpest trap in the whole module is **pass-through taxation for Categories I and II but NOT Category III**.

---

## 13.1 What "Alternative Investments" Are

**An alternative investment is anything outside the three traditional asset classes — listed equity, debt and cash.**

Typical alternatives: **private equity, venture capital, hedge funds, real estate, infrastructure, commodities, structured products, distressed assets, art and collectibles.**

### How alternatives differ from traditional investments

| Feature | Traditional (equity/debt/cash) | Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| **Liquidity** | High — daily exit | **Low** — multi-year lock-in |
| **Minimum ticket** | ₹100–₹5,000 | **₹1 crore** (AIF) |
| **Investor base** | Retail and everybody | **Sophisticated / HNI** only |
| **Regulation & disclosure** | Heavy, public, standardised | **Lighter, private, negotiated** |
| **Valuation** | Daily market price | **Periodic, often by an independent valuer** |
| **Fees** | Capped TER | **Fixed + performance/carry** |
| **Strategy** | Long-only, simple | **Complex — leverage, derivatives, unlisted deals** |
| **Correlation with markets** | High | **Typically lower** |

> 🧠 **The one-line definition to remember:** an alternative investment is *illiquid, expensive, lightly disclosed, sophisticated-investor-only* — and that is precisely why SEBI sets a **₹1 crore minimum**: it is a filter, not a fee.

---

## 13.2 Role of Alternative Investments in Portfolio Management

Why would anyone accept a 7–10 year lock-in?

| Reason | The logic |
|---|---|
| **Diversification** | Returns are driven by deal-specific factors, not the Nifty. **Low correlation** with listed markets lowers total portfolio risk. |
| **Illiquidity premium** | The investor is *paid* for giving up liquidity — an extra expected return for accepting a lock-in. |
| **Access to a bigger opportunity set** | Most Indian companies are **unlisted**. Listed equity is only a slice of the economy; PE/VC reaches the rest. |
| **Higher return potential** | Early-stage and special-situation deals can compound far faster than an index — with a far higher chance of total loss. |
| **Inflation hedge** | Real assets — infrastructure, real estate — carry revenue that reprices with inflation. |
| **Manager skill (alpha)** | Complex strategies (Category III) aim to earn returns independent of market direction. |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** the benefit of an alternative is **diversification through low correlation**, *not* "higher return with lower risk". Alternatives are **higher risk**; they improve the *portfolio's* risk-return trade-off, not their own.

**Where they sit in a portfolio:** alternatives are a **satellite**, added on top of a completed **core** of equity, debt and cash — never a substitute for the core, and never funded out of money the client may need.

## 13.3 Evolution and Growth of AIFs in India

| Phase | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Pre-2012** | Venture capital funds registered under the **SEBI (Venture Capital Funds) Regulations, 1996**. Many other private pools — PE, real estate, hedge-style funds — operated with **no dedicated framework**. |
| **May 2012** | SEBI notified the **SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012**, bringing *every* privately pooled vehicle under one umbrella and creating **Categories I, II and III**. |
| **After 2012** | The 1996 VCF Regulations were **repealed**; funds already registered under them continue until wound up, but **all new funds must register under the AIF Regulations**. |
| **Since** | Commitments raised have grown from a small base to **several lakh crore**, driven by rising HNI wealth, a maturing start-up ecosystem, family offices and institutional allocations. **Category II is by far the largest** category by commitments. |

> 🧠 **The four-word history:** *"1996 VCF → 2012 AIF."* The AIF Regulations replaced a narrow venture-capital rulebook with a broad, three-category one.

## 13.4 ⭐ What an AIF Is — the SEBI Definition

**An AIF is a privately pooled investment vehicle, established in India as a trust, company, LLP or body corporate, which collects funds from sophisticated investors — Indian or foreign — and invests them according to a defined investment policy for the benefit of those investors.**

Break that definition into its five load-bearing words:

| Word | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Privately pooled** | Money may be raised **only by private placement** — never by a public advertisement or public issue. |
| **Established in India** | An offshore fund is not an AIF. |
| **Trust / company / LLP / body corporate** | Most Indian AIFs are structured as **trusts**. |
| **Defined investment policy** | Stated up front in the **placement memorandum** and binding on the manager. |
| **For the benefit of investors** | A fiduciary standard. |

**What is NOT an AIF:** mutual funds (SEBI MF Regulations, 1996), collective investment schemes (SEBI CIS Regulations, 1999), and any other vehicle already regulated as a fund-management activity. Also excluded: **family trusts, ESOP and employee-welfare trusts, gratuity trusts, holding companies and securitisation trusts** — pools that are not really commercial investment funds.

### The three parties

| Party | Role |
|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | The person(s) who **set up** the AIF (promoter of a company, designated partner of an LLP). |
| **Manager** | Appointed by the AIF to **manage its investments**. |
| **Trustee** | For a trust structure, holds the assets for the investors' benefit. |

## 13.5 ⭐⭐ The Three Categories

### Category I — the "socially and economically desirable" bucket

Funds that invest in **start-ups, early-stage ventures, SMEs, social ventures, infrastructure, or other sectors that the government or regulators consider socially or economically desirable.** Because these areas are policy priorities, Category I funds are the ones on which the government or SEBI **may grant incentives or concessions**.

**The four named sub-categories:**

| Sub-category | Invests in |
|---|---|
| **Venture Capital Fund** (includes **Angel Funds**) | Early-stage, unlisted, high-growth ventures |
| **SME Fund** | Small and medium enterprises, including SME-exchange listings |
| **Social Venture Fund** | Social ventures — enterprises with a stated social objective |
| **Infrastructure Fund** | Infrastructure companies, projects and SPVs |

### Category II — the residual bucket

**Funds that do not fall in Category I or Category III, and which do not undertake leverage or borrowing other than to meet day-to-day operational requirements.**

Typical members: **private equity funds, debt funds, real estate funds, distressed-asset funds, fund-of-funds.** No specific government incentives.

> 🧠 **Category II is defined by subtraction.** "Not I, not III, and no leverage." If a fund invests in ordinary unlisted companies and does not trade derivatives, it lands here by default.

### Category III — the complex-strategy bucket

**Funds that employ diverse or complex trading strategies and may employ leverage, including through investment in listed or unlisted derivatives.**

Typical members: **hedge funds, PIPE funds** (private investment in public equity). No government incentives. **Only Category III may use leverage.**

## 13.6 The Eight Fund Types You Must Be Able to Place

| Fund type | Category | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Venture Capital Fund** | **I** | Equity in **early-stage, unlisted** high-growth ventures; long horizon; exits by IPO or strategic sale |
| **Angel Fund** | **I** (sub-category of VCF) | Very early "seed" capital raised from **angel investors**; special relaxed rules |
| **Private Equity Fund** | **II** | Larger, **later-stage** unlisted companies — growth capital, buyouts, mezzanine |
| **Debt Fund** | **II** | **Debt or debt securities** of listed/unlisted investee companies |
| **Infrastructure Fund** | **I** | Roads, power, ports, telecom — long-gestation projects and SPVs |
| **SME Fund** | **I** | Small and medium enterprises |
| **Hedge Fund** | **III** | Complex, leveraged, derivative-based strategies; **may be open-ended** |
| **Social Venture Fund** | **I** | Social ventures; investors may accept **"muted" (below-market) returns** |

> 🧠 **Memory hook — "VASI in I":** **V**enture capital, **A**ngel, **S**ME + **S**ocial venture, **I**nfrastructure are **Category I**. **PE and Debt are II. Hedge is III.**

### A few defining details

**Venture Capital Fund.** Invests in unlisted early-stage ventures. Very high failure rate at the individual deal level — returns come from a small number of large winners. Illiquid; the exit route (IPO, strategic sale, buyback) is decided at the time of entry.

**Private Equity Fund.** Invests across stages — **seed, early/start-up, expansion (second and third stage), mezzanine, and buyouts/M&A.** The manager typically receives **carried interest of up to about 20% of profits**, payable only after a **minimum rate of return (hurdle)** is achieved; the remaining ~80% goes to investors. Risk rises the earlier the stage.

**Debt Fund.** A **Category II** AIF investing in debt securities of investee companies. Because an AIF is a *pooled investment vehicle*, it invests in **debt instruments** — it cannot be used to carry on a lending business with investors' money. Debt funds receive **no special concessions** from the regulator.

**Social Venture Fund.** May **accept grants** and may **make grants** to social ventures; investors expressly accept that returns may be **muted** because the objective is measurable social impact alongside a financial return.

**Hedge Fund.** In India a hedge fund *is* a **Category III AIF**. May be **open-ended or close-ended**, may use **leverage** with investor consent and within the SEBI-specified limit, and is the **only category taxed at the fund level**.

### Angel funds — the special rules

Angel funds are a **sub-category of venture capital funds** (therefore **Category I**) that raise money from **angel investors**.

| Item | Angel fund rule |
|---|---|
| **Who is an angel investor** | An **individual with net tangible assets of at least ₹2 crore** (excluding the principal residence) *plus* early-stage investment experience / serial-entrepreneur experience / senior-management experience; **or a body corporate**; **or a registered AIF/VCF** |
| **Minimum corpus** | **₹10 crore** (against ₹20 crore for other AIFs) |
| **Maximum investors per scheme** | **49** (against 1000) |
| **Minimum investment per angel investor** | **₹25 lakh**, which may be drawn down over a **maximum of 5 years** — the ₹1 crore minimum does **not** apply |
| **Sponsor/manager continuing interest** | Lower of **2.5% of corpus or ₹50 lakh** |
| **Investee company must be** | Incorporated within **3 years**, turnover under **₹25 crore**, and not part of an industrial group with turnover above **₹300 crore** |
| **Size of each investment** | Between **₹50 lakh and ₹5 crore** |
| **Lock-in on each investment** | **3 years** |
| **Listing** | Angel funds **shall not be listed** |
| **Scheme document** | A **scheme memorandum** is filed with SEBI and comments incorporated before launch |

> ⚠️ Angel-fund thresholds are the ones SEBI has amended most often over the years. **Answer with the workbook figures above** — they are what the examination tests.

## 13.7 ⭐⭐ SEBI Requirements on AIFs — the Number Set

### Money

| Requirement | Figure |
|---|---|
| **Minimum corpus per scheme** | **₹20 crore** (**₹10 crore** for an angel fund) |
| **Minimum investment per investor** | **₹1 crore** |
| **Minimum for employees / directors of the AIF or of the Manager** | **₹25 lakh** |
| **Minimum for an angel investor** | **₹25 lakh**, over a maximum of 5 years |
| **Maximum investors per scheme** | **1000** (**49** for an angel fund scheme) |
| **Minimum tradable lot if units are listed** | **₹1 crore** |

**Why ₹25 lakh for employees and directors?** They already know the strategy, the manager and the risks from the inside — the ₹1 crore filter exists to keep the uninformed out, and insiders are not uninformed. It also lets the investment team invest alongside clients.

**Why 1000 investors?** The AIF Regulations permit fund-raising **only by private placement**. A cap on investor numbers is what keeps a "private" placement genuinely private rather than a disguised public issue.

### ⭐ Sponsor / Manager continuing interest ("skin in the game")

**The sponsor or manager must invest its own money in the fund and keep it there.**

| Category | Continuing interest |
|---|---|
| **Category I and II** | Lower of **2.5% of corpus** or **₹5 crore** |
| **Category III** | Lower of **5% of corpus** or **₹10 crore** |
| **Angel fund** | Lower of **2.5% of corpus** or **₹50 lakh** |

This interest **may not be met by waiving management fees** — it must be real money at risk.

> 🧠 **Why Category III is double:** Category III can use **leverage** and complex strategies, so the manager can lose investors' money faster. SEBI therefore doubles the alignment requirement — **5% / ₹10 crore instead of 2.5% / ₹5 crore.**

**Worked example.** A Category II fund with a corpus of **₹300 crore**: 2.5% × 300 = **₹7.5 crore**, but the rule is the **lower** of that and ₹5 crore → the sponsor commits **₹5 crore**.
A Category II fund with a corpus of **₹120 crore**: 2.5% × 120 = **₹3 crore**, lower than ₹5 crore → the sponsor commits **₹3 crore**.
The same ₹300 crore corpus in a **Category III** fund: 5% × 300 = ₹15 crore vs ₹10 crore → the sponsor commits **₹10 crore**.

### ⭐ Structure and tenure

| Category | Open or close-ended | Minimum tenure |
|---|---|---|
| **Category I** | **Close-ended** | **3 years** |
| **Category II** | **Close-ended** | **3 years** |
| **Category III** | **Open OR close-ended** | No minimum tenure if open-ended |

- The tenure of a close-ended fund may be **extended by up to 2 years** with the consent of **two-thirds of unit holders by value** of their investment.
- If that consent is not obtained, the fund must **fully liquidate within one year** of the end of its tenure.
- Units of a **close-ended** AIF **may be listed** on a stock exchange, with a **minimum tradable lot of ₹1 crore** — and only after the final close of the scheme. **Angel funds may not be listed.**

> ⚠️ **Listing is not liquidity.** A ₹1 crore minimum lot on an exchange with almost no buyers is a legal right to sell, not a practical one. Never tell a client an AIF is liquid because it is listed.

### Fund-raising and documents

- Funds are raised **only through private placement** using a **placement memorandum** containing all material information an investor needs.
- The placement memorandum must be **filed with SEBI at least 30 days before the launch** of the scheme, and SEBI's comments incorporated before launch.
- Every AIF must set up a **procedure to resolve disputes** between investors, the fund, the manager and the sponsor.

### Eligibility of the fund house

- The **constitution document** (trust deed / MoA / LLP agreement) must permit the activity of an AIF.
- The **sponsor, manager and key personnel** must be **fit and proper persons**.
- At least **one key person must have at least 5 years' experience** in asset/portfolio management, advisory, securities trading or dealing in other financial assets.
- Adequate **infrastructure** must exist to manage the fund.

### Registration fees (payable to SEBI)

| Item | Fee |
|---|---|
| **Application fee** | **₹1 lakh** |
| **Category I registration** | **₹5 lakh** |
| **Angel fund registration** | **₹2 lakh** |
| **Category II registration** | **₹10 lakh** |
| **Category III registration** | **₹15 lakh** |

> 🧠 The registration fee **rises with the freedom the category enjoys**: I (₹5 lakh) → II (₹10 lakh) → III (₹15 lakh), with the small angel fund cheapest at ₹2 lakh.

### Investment conditions

| Condition | Category I & II | Category III |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximum in one investee company** | **25% of investable funds** | **10% of investable funds** |
| **Leverage / borrowing** | **Not permitted**, except temporary funding — not more than **30 days**, on not more than **4 occasions a year**, and not more than **10% of investable funds** | **Permitted**, with investor consent, disclosure in the placement memorandum, and within the SEBI-specified limit |
| **Overseas investment** | Permitted, subject to RBI/SEBI conditions | Permitted, subject to RBI/SEBI conditions |
| **Investment in associates** | Requires approval of **75% of investors by value** | Same |
| **Un-invested money** | May be parked in liquid mutual funds, bank deposits, T-bills and other high-quality liquid assets | Same |

### Valuation and reporting

| Category | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Category I and II** | Investments valued by an **independent valuer at least once every six months** |
| **Category III — close-ended** | **NAV disclosed to investors at least once every quarter** |
| **Category III — open-ended** | **NAV disclosed to investors at least once every month** |

> 🧠 **Why the difference?** Categories I and II hold **unlisted** assets with no market price, so a valuer is needed — and twice a year is as often as it is meaningful. Category III trades **listed** securities and derivatives that price daily, so frequent NAV disclosure is both possible and necessary.

## 13.8 ⭐⭐ Taxation — the Highest-Yield Fact in the Chapter

| | **Category I & II** | **Category III** |
|---|---|---|
| **Pass-through status** | **Yes** — granted under the Income Tax Act | **No** |
| **Who pays the tax** | **The investor**, on his share of the income | **The fund**, before distributing |
| **Character of income** | **Preserved** — capital gains stay capital gains, interest stays interest, dividend stays dividend | Lost — the investor receives income already taxed |
| **Rate applied** | The **investor's own** applicable rate | Generally the **maximum marginal rate** at the fund level |
| **Exception** | **Business income** of the fund is taxed **at the fund level**, not passed through | — |
| **TDS** | The fund deducts tax at source on income credited or paid to resident investors | — |

**What "pass-through" actually means.** The fund is treated as **transparent**: income is deemed to arise directly to the investor, **as if he had made the investment himself**. There is no tax at the fund level, so there is no double taxation, and a low-slab investor pays a low rate while a high-slab investor pays a high one.

> ⚠️ **The exam trap, stated plainly:** *pass-through applies to Category I and Category II — never to Category III.* A hedge fund (Category III) is taxed **at the fund level**, so every investor effectively bears the **same** rate regardless of his own slab. This makes Category III **relatively less tax-efficient for an investor in a low tax bracket** — an important suitability point.

> 🧠 **Memory hook:** *"One and Two pass it through; Three pays for you."*

## 13.9 ⭐ Comparison of the Three Categories

| Feature | **Category I** | **Category II** | **Category III** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core idea** | Socially/economically desirable sectors | Everything else, no leverage | Complex trading strategies |
| **Examples** | VC, angel, SME, social venture, infrastructure | PE, debt, real estate, distressed, fund-of-funds | Hedge funds, PIPE funds |
| **Structure** | **Close-ended** | **Close-ended** | **Open or close-ended** |
| **Minimum tenure** | **3 years** | **3 years** | None if open-ended |
| **Leverage** | **No** (temporary borrowing only) | **No** (temporary borrowing only) | **Yes**, within SEBI limits |
| **Concessions from government/SEBI** | **May be given** | **None** | **None** |
| **Sponsor continuing interest** | Lower of **2.5% / ₹5 crore** | Lower of **2.5% / ₹5 crore** | Lower of **5% / ₹10 crore** |
| **Max in one investee company** | **25%** of investable funds | **25%** | **10%** |
| **Valuation / NAV** | Independent valuer **every 6 months** | Every 6 months | NAV **quarterly** (close) / **monthly** (open) |
| **Registration fee** | **₹5 lakh** (angel ₹2 lakh) | **₹10 lakh** | **₹15 lakh** |
| **Taxation** | **Pass-through** | **Pass-through** | **Taxed at the fund level** |

### AIF vs PMS vs mutual fund

| | **Mutual fund** | **PMS** | **AIF** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Regulation** | SEBI (MF) Regulations, **1996** | SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, **2020** | SEBI (AIF) Regulations, **2012** |
| **Minimum investment** | ₹100–₹5,000 | **₹50 lakh** | **₹1 crore** |
| **Pooling** | **Pooled** — investor holds units | **Not pooled** — securities in the client's own name | **Pooled** — investor holds units |
| **Offered to** | The public | Individually | **Private placement only**, max 1000 per scheme |
| **Liquidity** | High (open-ended) | Moderate | **Low** — multi-year lock-in |
| **Leverage** | Not permitted | Not permitted | **Category III only** |
| **Disclosure** | Daily NAV, public | To the client | To investors, **private** |
| **Customisation** | None | **Possible** | None — a common pool |

> 🧠 **The three minimums in one line: ₹100 → ₹50 lakh → ₹1 crore.** Mutual fund, PMS, AIF. Getting these three the right way round answers a surprising number of questions across Chapters 11, 12 and 13.

## 13.10 Suitability and Enablers

### Who an AIF suits

| Suitable when the client has | Because |
|---|---|
| A **large investible surplus** — well above ₹1 crore of *spare* money | The ₹1 crore is a **commitment**, drawn down over time by capital calls |
| A **long horizon** — typically **7–10 years** | Close-ended, minimum 3-year tenure, extendable by 2 more |
| **No need for liquidity** from this money | There is effectively no exit before maturity |
| **High risk tolerance** and the ability to absorb a total loss on a deal | Unlisted, concentrated, sometimes leveraged |
| An **already complete core portfolio** | Alternatives are a **satellite**, never the core |
| The **sophistication** to understand the strategy, the fee structure and the valuation basis | Disclosure is lighter than in a mutual fund |

### Who it does NOT suit

- Anyone who may need the money back — **there is no redemption on demand**
- Anyone seeking **regular income** or **capital protection**
- Anyone whose entire portfolio is small enough that a ₹1 crore commitment would be a concentrated bet
- Anyone for whom the **fee load** (fixed management fee + carry, plus set-up costs) would swallow the extra return

### Enablers that have driven AIF growth in India

- A clear, single **regulatory framework** since 2012 and the credibility that brings
- **Pass-through taxation** for Categories I and II
- Rapid growth in **HNI and UHNI wealth**, and the rise of **family offices**
- A maturing **start-up and unlisted-company ecosystem** needing risk capital
- The ability to **list close-ended units** and the move to holding **units in demat form**
- **GIFT City / IFSC** structures making it easier to pool domestic and offshore money
- Growing professional distribution and advice, including **fee-only investment advisers**

> ⚠️ **Adviser's duty:** an AIF pays no commission to a SEBI-registered investment adviser — the IA is paid by the client. So the recommendation must rest **only on suitability**: horizon, liquidity need, risk capacity and the client's existing asset allocation. Recommending an AIF to a client who cannot tolerate a decade-long lock-in is a **suitability failure**, however good the fund.

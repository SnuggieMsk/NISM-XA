## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy: Recall (Q1–Q35)

**Q1.** Portfolio performance is properly defined by:
A) The return alone  B) The number of holdings  C) The return AND the risk taken to earn it  D) The size of the fund
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Performance is a **two-dimensional** quantity. Two funds that both returned 18% did not perform equally if one took twice the risk.
A) A return figure on its own is meaningless. B) Holding count is a crude proxy for diversification, not performance. D) Fund size affects capacity, not risk-adjusted performance.
</details>

**Q2.** The Holding Period Return formula is:
A) (End − Begin + Income) ÷ Begin  B) (End − Begin) ÷ End  C) (End ÷ Begin)^(1/n) − 1  D) (End − Begin) ÷ n
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — HPR captures **price change plus income**, divided by the **opening** value.
B) Divides by the ending value — wrong base, and omits income. C) Is the **CAGR** formula. D) Is a simple average of the rupee gain, not a return.
</details>

**Q3.** The Time-Weighted Rate of Return is computed by:
A) Taking the IRR of all cash flows  B) Averaging monthly returns arithmetically  C) Dividing profit by average capital  D) Geometrically chaining the returns of each sub-period between cash flows
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Break the period at every cash flow, compute a clean return for each slice, then multiply: **(1+R₁)(1+R₂)… − 1**.
A) Describes **MWRR**. B) An arithmetic average overstates compounded performance. C) Is a rough money-weighted approximation, not TWRR.
</details>

**Q4.** The Money-Weighted Rate of Return is best described as:
A) The simple average return  B) The IRR of the portfolio's cash flows  C) The benchmark return  D) The return after tax
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — MWRR is the discount rate that equates all inflows and outflows, so periods with more money invested dominate the answer.
A) An average of period returns is a time-weighted idea. C) The benchmark is a separate comparison. D) Tax is an entirely different adjustment.
</details>

**Q5.** SEBI requires portfolio managers to disclose performance using:
A) MWRR, gross of fees  B) Arithmetic mean returns  C) TWRR, net of all fees and expenses  D) Whichever measure the manager prefers
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — TWRR neutralises client cash flows and net-of-fee reporting shows what clients actually kept.
A) MWRR would reflect the client's timing, not the manager's skill, and gross figures overstate outcomes. B) Arithmetic means inflate volatile records. D) Discretion is precisely what the rule removes.
</details>

**Q6.** TWRR is preferred for judging a manager because it:
A) Neutralises client cash flows, which the manager does not control  B) Always produces a higher number  C) Is simpler to compute  D) Includes the effect of tax
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — The manager decides **what to buy**; the client decides **when to add or withdraw**. Grading the manager on the client's timing measures the wrong person.
B) TWRR can be higher or lower than MWRR. C) It is generally *harder* — it needs a valuation at every cash flow. D) TWRR is a pre-tax measure.
</details>

**Q7.** The relationship between arithmetic and geometric mean returns is:
A) GMR is always greater than AMR  B) They are always equal  C) There is no fixed relationship  D) AMR ≥ GMR, with equality only when all returns are identical
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Percentage gains and losses are not symmetric, so simply averaging them overstates the compounded outcome.
A) Reverses the inequality. B) True only in the special case of zero volatility. C) The inequality is mathematically guaranteed.
</details>

**Q8.** The gap between AMR and GMR widens as:
A) The fee level falls  B) The volatility of returns rises  C) The holding period shortens  D) The benchmark changes
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Approximately **GMR ≈ AMR − σ²/2**, so the gap grows with the *square* of volatility. The gap **is** the cost of volatility.
A) Fees reduce both means equally. C) Period length does not drive the gap; dispersion does. D) The benchmark is irrelevant to a fund's own two means.
</details>

**Q9.** CAGR is calculated as:
A) (End − Begin) ÷ Begin  B) Sum of annual returns ÷ n  C) (End ÷ Begin)^(1/n) − 1  D) (End − Begin) ÷ n
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — CAGR is the geometric mean expressed from two endpoints, where **n is the number of years**.
A) Is the total (holding-period) return, not annualised. B) Is the arithmetic mean. D) Is a rupee-per-year figure, not a rate.
</details>

**Q10.** Net return is gross return minus:
A) All fees, expenses, transaction costs and GST  B) Tax only  C) Inflation  D) The benchmark return
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Management fee/TER, performance fee, brokerage, STT, custody, **18% GST** on fees, and exit load.
B) Tax is the *next* step — post-tax return. C) Deducting inflation gives the **real** return. D) Deducting the benchmark gives the **active** return.
</details>

**Q11.** Post-tax return is:
A) The same for every investor  B) Always higher than pre-tax return  C) Published by every mutual fund  D) Investor-specific, depending on slab, holding period and exemptions
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Tax depends on the individual's circumstances, which is exactly why published returns are always pre-tax.
A) Two investors in different slabs keep different amounts. B) Tax can only reduce a gain. C) A fund cannot compute one post-tax figure for everybody.
</details>

**Q12.** To annualise a return earned over part of a year, you should:
A) Multiply it by the number of periods  B) Compound it: (1 + r)^periods − 1  C) Divide it by the number of periods  D) Take its square root
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Returns compound; they do not add. A 4% quarterly return annualises to **16.99%**, not 16%.
A) Simple multiplication ignores compounding. C) Dividing de-annualises. D) The square root rule applies to **risk**, not return.
</details>

**Q13.** To annualise a standard deviation you:
A) Multiply by the number of periods  B) Divide by the number of periods  C) Multiply by the square root of the number of periods  D) Leave it unchanged
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — **σ_annual = σ_periodic × √periods**, because *variances* add over time and σ is the square root of variance.
A) Would massively overstate risk. B) Wrong direction entirely. D) A monthly σ is not an annual σ.
</details>

**Q14.** Cash drag refers to:
A) The return lost by holding uninvested cash in a rising market  B) The fee charged on cash balances  C) A delay in paying redemptions  D) The cost of borrowing
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Cash earns a money-market rate rather than the asset-class return, so it dilutes performance when markets rise.
B) Fees are a separate cost. C) That is a liquidity or operational issue. D) That is the cost of leverage.
</details>

**Q15.** Jensen's alpha is:
A) Rp − Rm  B) (Rp − Rf) ÷ σp  C) Rp ÷ Rm  D) Rp − [Rf + β(Rm − Rf)]
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Alpha is the excess over the **CAPM-required** return for the beta actually taken.
A) Simple excess over the benchmark ignores the risk taken — the single most common error in this chapter. B) Is the Sharpe ratio. C) Is a meaningless ratio of two returns.
</details>

**Q16.** Beta measures:
A) Total risk  B) Sensitivity to market movements — systematic risk  C) Downside risk  D) Liquidity risk
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — β = Cov(i,m) ÷ Var(m). A beta of 1.2 implies a 12% move for a 10% market move.
A) Total risk is standard deviation. C) That is downside deviation or semi-variance. D) Beta is blind to liquidity.
</details>

**Q17.** Portfolio return is:
A) The simple (unweighted) average of component returns  B) The highest component return  C) The weighted average of component returns  D) Always below the weighted average
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — **Rp = Σ wᵢRᵢ**, using market-value weights at the start of the period.
A) Ignores how much money is in each holding. B) No portfolio earns its best holding's return. D) That statement is true of **risk**, not return.
</details>

**Q18.** Total risk is measured by:
A) Standard deviation  B) Beta  C) Tracking error  D) Downside deviation
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Standard deviation captures the full dispersion of returns, both systematic and unsystematic.
B) Captures only systematic risk. C) Captures only benchmark-relative risk. D) Captures only shortfalls below a target.
</details>

**Q19.** Downside deviation counts:
A) All deviations from the mean  B) Only positive returns  C) Only the single worst year  D) Only returns falling below the target (MAR)
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Shortfalls below the MAR are squared and summed, but **every period still counts in the denominator n**.
A) That is standard deviation. B) Upside is exactly what downside deviation excludes. C) That would be a maximum-loss measure, not a deviation.
</details>

**Q20.** Tracking error is:
A) A dealing or settlement error  B) The standard deviation of the active return  C) The simple return gap versus the benchmark  D) The fund's beta
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — It measures how *variable* the portfolio-minus-benchmark gap is.
A) Nothing to do with operations. C) That is **tracking difference** — a closely related but distinct measure. D) Beta measures market sensitivity, not benchmark-relative variability.
</details>

**Q21.** Systematic risk is:
A) Company-specific and diversifiable  B) Removed by holding about 30 stocks  C) Market-wide, non-diversifiable, and measured by beta  D) Never compensated by the market
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Its drivers are economy-wide: RBI policy, inflation, GDP, currency, crude, global sentiment.
A) Describes unsystematic risk. B) Diversification removes only the specific component. D) Systematic risk is precisely the risk that **is** rewarded.
</details>

**Q22.** Unsystematic risk can be reduced by:
A) Diversification across securities that do not move together  B) Using leverage  C) Increasing portfolio beta  D) Nothing — it is unavoidable
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Most specific risk disappears within roughly **20–30 well-spread** holdings; what matters is that they are genuinely different.
B) Leverage magnifies all risk. C) Raising beta adds systematic risk. D) That is true of systematic risk, not specific risk.
</details>

**Q23.** Which risk does the market reward investors for bearing?
A) Unsystematic risk  B) Both equally  C) Neither  D) Systematic risk
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — The market pays a premium only for risk that **cannot be avoided**.
A) Specific risk can be removed at almost zero cost by diversifying, so no rational investor would pay a premium for it. B) They are treated very differently. C) The equity risk premium exists precisely as compensation for systematic risk.
</details>

**Q24.** The Sharpe ratio is:
A) (Rp − Rf) ÷ βp  B) (Rp − Rf) ÷ σp  C) (Rp − Rb) ÷ tracking error  D) Rf + (Sharpe × σm)
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Excess return per unit of **total** risk.

**Q25.** The Treynor ratio is:

**Q26.** The denominator of the Sortino ratio is:

**Correct: A)** — Sortino penalises only the volatility investors actually dislike.

**Q27.** The Information Ratio equals:
A) alpha ÷ beta  B) (Rp − Rf) ÷ σp  C) Rp ÷ Rb  D) Active return ÷ tracking error
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — It measures both the **size** and the **consistency** of benchmark-beating performance.

</details>

**Q28.** M² expresses risk-adjusted performance as:
A) A pure ratio  B) A percentage return at the market's level of risk  C) A beta  D) A rupee amount

**Correct: B)** — **M² = Rf + (Sharpe × σm)**, which a client can compare directly with the index return.

**Q29.** The beta of the market index is, by definition:

**Q30.** A Total Return Index differs from a Price Return Index because it:

**Q35.** An Indian investor's rupee return on a foreign investment is:

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium: Application (Q36–Q70)

**Q36.** An investor buys 500 shares at ₹400, receives a ₹8 per share dividend, and sells at ₹436. The HPR is:
A) 9.0%  B) 10.0%  C) 11.0%  D) 22.0%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Begin = 500 × ₹400 = **₹2,00,000**. End = 500 × ₹436 = **₹2,18,000**. Income = 500 × ₹8 = **₹4,000**. HPR = (2,18,000 − 2,00,000 + 4,000) ÷ 2,00,000 = 22,000 ÷ 2,00,000 = **11.0%**.
A) Omits the dividend (18,000 ÷ 2,00,000). B) Corresponds to a ₹20,000 gain — neither the ₹18,000 price gain nor the ₹22,000 total. D) Is the rupee gain of ₹22,000 misread as a percentage of ₹1 lakh.
</details>

**Q37.** An investment returns 8% over six months. Annualised, that is:
A) 16.00%  B) 16.64%  C) 8.00%  D) 4.00%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — (1.08)² − 1 = 1.1664 − 1 = **16.64%**.
A) Simply doubles the return, ignoring compounding. C) Is the un-annualised six-month figure. D) Halves it — the wrong direction entirely.
</details>

**Q38.** A portfolio returns +12% in the first half-year and −5% in the second. The TWRR for the year is:
A) 7.00%  B) 6.00%  C) 17.00%  D) 6.40%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — TWRR chains geometrically: (1.12 × 0.95) − 1 = 1.064 − 1 = **6.40%**.
A) Adds the two returns (12 − 5), ignoring compounding. B) Is an arbitrary rounding. C) Adds the absolute sizes of the moves.
</details>

**Q39.** A client transferred a large sum into her portfolio one week before a strong market rally. Relative to TWRR, her MWRR will be:
A) Higher, because more money was invested during the strong period  B) Lower  C) Identical  D) Impossible to determine

**Correct: A)** — MWRR weights each period by the money invested in it. Good client timing lifts MWRR above TWRR; bad timing pushes it below.
B) Would be the case had she added money before a *fall*. C) They coincide only when there are **no** cash flows. D) The direction follows logically from the timing.
</details>

**Q40.** A fund's annual returns are 18%, −4%, 12% and 6%. Its arithmetic mean return is:

**Q41.** A fund returns +20% in year 1 and −10% in year 2. Its geometric mean return is:
A) 5.00%  B) 4.50%  C) 3.92%  D) 8.00%

</details>

**Q42.** AMR equals GMR only when:
A) Returns are all positive  B) The period is exactly one year  C) Fees are zero  D) Every period's return is identical (zero volatility)
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — With no dispersion there is nothing for compounding to erode, so the two means coincide.

**Q43.** ₹4,00,000 grows to ₹8,00,000 over 6 years. The CAGR is:

**Q48.** A fund holds 15% in cash earning 5% and 85% in equities returning 16%. The cash drag is:

**Q50.** A fund returned 14% when Rf was 6%, the market returned 12%, and the fund's beta was 0.90. Jensen's alpha is:

</details>

**Q51.** Why is (Rp − Rm) not an acceptable measure of alpha?

**Correct: C)** — A fund with a beta of 1.5 in a rising market beats the index automatically. Alpha must subtract the **beta-adjusted required** return; the two figures coincide only when β = 1.

</details>

### Case: The Deshpande Family Portfolio Review

Mr and Mrs Deshpande hold a **₹2,00,00,000** portfolio managed under an advisory mandate. Their adviser presents the following one-year review against the agreed customised benchmark. The **risk-free rate is 6.0%**.

| Measure | **Portfolio** | **Benchmark** |
|---|---|---|
| Return | **15.2%** | **12.0%** |
| Standard deviation | **16.0%** | **14.0%** |
| Beta | **1.10** | 1.00 |
| Tracking error | **4.0%** | — |
| Downside deviation (MAR 6%) | 8.0% | — |

---

**QC1.** The Sharpe ratios of the portfolio and the benchmark are:
A) 0.58 and 0.43 — the portfolio is better  B) 0.95 and 0.86  C) 0.43 and 0.58 — the benchmark is better  D) Both 0.58
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Portfolio: (15.2 − 6.0) ÷ 16.0 = 9.2 ÷ 16.0 = **0.575 ≈ 0.58**. Benchmark: (12.0 − 6.0) ÷ 14.0 = 6.0 ÷ 14.0 = **0.429 ≈ 0.43**. Per unit of **total** risk, the portfolio delivered more.
B) Divides the raw returns by σ without subtracting Rf. C) Attaches the figures to the wrong side. D) The two ratios differ by 0.15, which is a material margin.
</details>

**QC2.** The portfolio's Jensen's alpha is:
A) +3.20%  B) +0.60%  C) +2.60%  D) +9.20%
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Required return = 6.0 + 1.10 × (12.0 − 6.0) = 6.0 + 6.6 = **12.60%**. Alpha = 15.2 − 12.6 = **+2.60%**. Of the 3.2 points of raw outperformance, **0.6 points were simply bought with a beta of 1.10**; only 2.6 points reflect the adviser's decisions.
A) Is Rp − Rb, which ignores the beta taken. B) Is the beta-bought portion alone. D) Is Rp − Rf, the raw excess over cash.
</details>

**QC3.** The portfolio's Information Ratio is:
A) 0.65  B) 3.20  C) 1.25  D) 0.80
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: D)** — Active return = 15.2 − 12.0 = **3.2%**; tracking error = **4.0%**; IR = 3.2 ÷ 4.0 = **0.80** — comfortably in the "very good" band, indicating outperformance achieved with modest benchmark-relative risk.
A) Is an arithmetic slip. B) Is the active return itself, undivided. C) Inverts the ratio (4.0 ÷ 3.2).
</details>

**QC4.** The portfolio's M² and M² alpha are:
A) 14.05% and +2.05 percentage points  B) 15.20% and +3.20 pp  C) 12.00% and 0.00 pp  D) 9.20% and −2.80 pp
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — Portfolio Sharpe = **0.575**. M² = Rf + (Sharpe × σ_benchmark) = 6.0 + (0.575 × 14.0) = 6.0 + 8.05 = **14.05%**. M² alpha = 14.05 − 12.00 = **+2.05 pp**. Translated into plain language for the clients: *"at the benchmark's own level of risk, your strategy would have earned 14.05% against the benchmark's 12.0%."*
B) Is the raw portfolio return, with no risk adjustment. C) Would require the portfolio's Sharpe to equal the benchmark's. D) Is the raw excess over Rf.
</details>

**QC5.** Taking the Treynor ratio and net selectivity together, the overall verdict is:
A) Treynor 6.00 versus 8.36 — the benchmark wins  B) Treynor 8.36 versus 6.00; the portfolio wins on both Sharpe and Treynor, and net selectivity is still about +2.34% — genuine, well-diversified skill  C) The measures conflict, so the portfolio must be badly diversified  D) Nothing can be concluded from one year of data alone
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Treynor: portfolio = (15.2 − 6.0) ÷ 1.10 = 9.2 ÷ 1.10 = **8.36**; benchmark = 6.0 ÷ 1.00 = **6.00**. The portfolio wins on **both** Sharpe and Treynor, which means it is **well diversified** — there is no hidden pile of specific risk for Sharpe to punish.

**The harder test — net selectivity:** charging for **total** risk instead of beta, the required return is 6.0 + (16.0 ÷ 14.0) × (12.0 − 6.0) = 6.0 + (1.1429 × 6.0) = 6.0 + 6.86 = **12.86%**. Net selectivity = 15.2 − 12.86 = **+2.34%** — only 0.26 points below the **+2.60%** Jensen's alpha, confirming that almost none of the outperformance was payment for undiversified risk.

**What the adviser should tell the clients:** the portfolio added roughly **2.3–2.6 percentage points of genuine value** with an Information Ratio of 0.80 and no diversification penalty. The one honest caveat is that **a single year is not a market cycle** — the same figures should be shown over three and five years, on rolling returns, before drawing firm conclusions.

A) Reverses the two Treynor figures. C) Agreement between Sharpe and Treynor is evidence of **good** diversification, not bad. D) Meaningful conclusions can be drawn from these figures; the caveat concerns their durability, not their validity.
</details>

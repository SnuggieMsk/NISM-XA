</details>

A) Paperwork is a formality that can be completed later  B) Every rule here answers one of three questions — prove who the investor is, prove the money is the investor's own, and make sure the right person gets it back — so identity, source of funds and succession must each be documented before, not after, the transaction  C) Nomination settles inheritance conclusively  D) Third-party payments are acceptable within the family
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Identity (**PAN, KYC, CKYC/KRA**), source of funds (**the anti-third-party rules**) and succession (**nomination, transmission, assignment**) are the three axes on which every rule in the chapter turns.
A) Incomplete documentation invalidates transactions and destroys an adviser's defence. C) A nominee is a **trustee**, not an owner — except a beneficial nominee under Section 39 of the Insurance Act. D) The only family exception is for a **minor**, capped at **₹50,000 per transaction**.
</details>

## 🧩 Worked Caselet — Exam-Style Practice

> **Attempt all five sub-questions before checking any answer.** The case moves through four different operational situations in one family — read each fact carefully before deciding which rule applies.

### Case: The Rajagopalan Family, Chennai

**Mr S. Rajagopalan**, aged 62 and a retired bank manager, is an SEBI-registered Investment Adviser's long-standing client. Four things happen in his family during the same financial year:

| # | Event | Figures |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | His son **Karthik** takes up a permanent job in Singapore and becomes an NRI | Resident savings balance **₹6,80,000** |
| 2 | Karthik remits **USD 60,000** to India; the rupee is at **₹83** when he remits and **₹90** a year later. An NRE deposit pays **7%**; an FCNR(B) deposit pays **4.5%** | — |
| 3 | Mr Rajagopalan wants to gift money into the folio of his granddaughter **Aditi**, a minor, from his own account | Intended gift **₹75,000** in one payment |
| 4 | His unmarried sister dies leaving one mutual fund folio in her sole name with **no nomination** registered | Folio value **₹4,60,000** |

---

**QC1.** What must Karthik do with his resident savings account holding ₹6,80,000?
A) Close it and remit the balance abroad  B) Have it re-designated as an NRO account  C) Convert it directly into an NRE account  D) Continue operating it as a resident account
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — On becoming a person resident outside India under FEMA, a **resident savings account must be re-designated as an NRO account**. The ₹6,80,000 of Indian-source money sits there, is taxable, and is repatriable only within the **USD 1 million per financial year** ceiling.
A) Closure is unnecessary and would lose the account history. C) An **NRE** account may be credited only with **foreign earnings remitted from abroad**, so a rupee balance built up in India cannot simply become NRE. D) Continuing to run a resident account after becoming an NRI is a **FEMA contravention**.
</details>

**QC2.** Karthik puts the USD 60,000 into an NRE deposit at 7%. What does he get back in dollars after one year at ₹90?
A) About USD 64,200  B) About USD 62,700  C) About USD 59,207  D) Exactly USD 60,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Rupee deposit = 60,000 × 83 = **₹49,80,000**. Interest at 7% = 49,80,000 × 0.07 = **₹3,48,600**. Maturity value = **₹53,28,600**. Repatriated at ₹90: 53,28,600 ÷ 90 = **USD 59,206.67**, i.e., about **USD 59,207**. He has **lost about USD 793** in dollar terms despite earning 7% — because the rupee fell about 8.4%.
A) Assumes no currency movement at all. B) Is the FCNR outcome. D) Ignores both the interest and the exchange-rate move, which happen to be of similar size but in opposite directions.
</details>

**QC3.** Had he chosen the FCNR(B) deposit at 4.5% instead, what would he have received, and what is the lesson?
A) USD 59,207 — the two routes are identical  B) USD 62,700 — about USD 3,493 more, because FCNR carries no currency risk  C) USD 60,000 — FCNR pays no interest  D) USD 64,200 — FCNR always pays more
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — FCNR is held **in dollars**, so the rupee's fall is irrelevant: **USD 60,000 × 1.045 = USD 62,700**, which is **62,700 − 59,207 = about USD 3,493 more** than the NRE route.

**The lesson:** the NRE deposit paid a **higher nominal rate (7% versus 4.5%)** yet delivered a **worse dollar outcome**, because the **rupee depreciated by more than the 2.5 percentage-point rate advantage**. For an NRI whose liabilities are in a foreign currency, **the currency of the deposit matters more than the headline rate**. Had the rupee been stable, NRE would have won comfortably.
A) The outcomes differ by about USD 3,493. C) FCNR deposits certainly pay interest. D) FCNR does **not** always win — it wins when the rupee falls by more than the rate differential.
</details>

**QC4.** Can Mr Rajagopalan gift ₹75,000 in one payment from his own account into his minor granddaughter's folio?
A) Yes — grandparents may pay any amount for a minor  B) No — the third-party relaxation for a minor is capped at ₹50,000 per transaction  C) Yes, if he signs an indemnity  D) No — grandparents are never permitted to pay
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — The relaxation for a **parent, grandparent or related person paying for a MINOR** out of natural love and affection is capped at **₹50,000 per transaction**. A single ₹75,000 payment breaches it and will be rejected. The practical answer is to gift the money into **Aditi's own bank account or the guardian's account** first and invest from there, with a **Third Party Payment Declaration** where the relaxation is used.
A) The exception exists but is capped. C) An indemnity cannot raise a PMLA-based ceiling. D) Grandparents **are** an allowed payer for a minor, within the cap.
</details>

**QC5.** His sister's ₹4,60,000 folio has no nomination. What documentation route applies, and what would have changed at ₹6,00,000?
A) A succession certificate in both cases  B) The simplified route at ₹4,60,000 since it is below the ₹5 lakh threshold; legal evidence such as probate or a succession certificate would be needed at ₹6,00,000  C) The simplified route in both cases  D) The units lapse to the AMC in both cases
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — ₹4,60,000 falls **below AMFI's ₹5,00,000 per-folio small-claim threshold**, so the claimant can use the **simplified route — an indemnity bond, affidavits from all the legal heirs and NOCs from the non-claimant heirs** — together with the **death certificate**, the claimant's **KYC** and **bank proof**. At **₹6,00,000** the folio would cross the threshold and the AMC would insist on **legal evidence of entitlement — a succession certificate, probate of the will or a letter of administration**.

**The adviser's lesson:** the entire difficulty was avoidable. A **registered nomination** would have let the fund pay the nominee against a death certificate and KYC alone — no affidavits, no NOCs, no court. That is why an adviser should audit **every** client folio and demat account for a live nomination, and should remind clients that a **nominee is a trustee for the legal heirs, not the owner** — so nomination complements a will, it does not replace one.

A) The simplified route exists precisely to avoid this for smaller claims. C) ₹6,00,000 exceeds the threshold. D) An AMC never appropriates an investor's units; they belong to the estate.
</details>

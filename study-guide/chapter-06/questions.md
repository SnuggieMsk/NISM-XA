# Chapter 6: Securities Market Segments — 100 MCQ Question Bank

> Three tiers — 🟢 Easy (Recall), 🟡 Medium (Application), 🔴 Hard (Numerical & Scenario). **Corporate actions carry the most marks here** — especially bonus vs split and rights entitlement arithmetic. Remember the **25% negative marking**.

## 🟢 Tier 1 — Easy: Recall (Q1–Q35)

**Q1.** In an Initial Public Offer, the company:
A) Offers shares to the public for the first time and becomes listed  B) Buys back its own shares  C) Issues free shares to shareholders  D) Delists from the exchange
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — An **IPO** is the first offer of shares to the public, after which the company is listed.
B) Is a buy-back. C) Is a bonus issue. D) Is delisting — the opposite of an IPO.
</details>

**Q2.** In an Offer for Sale, the proceeds go to:
A) The company  B) The selling shareholders  C) SEBI  D) The stock exchange
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — An OFS involves **existing shares**, so the **selling shareholders** receive the money — **not the company**.
A) The company receives money only in a **fresh issue**. C) and D) are the regulator and the platform, not recipients.
</details>

**Q3.** A bonus issue:
A) Raises fresh capital for the company  B) Involves the capitalisation of reserves and raises no money  C) Reduces the number of shares  D) Is offered only to institutional investors
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Bonus shares are issued **free** by converting reserves into share capital — **no cash is raised**.
A) That is a rights issue. C) The share count **increases**. D) Bonus shares go to **all** existing shareholders.
</details>

**Q4.** A rights issue is offered to:
A) The general public  B) QIBs only  C) Existing shareholders in proportion to their holding  D) Employees only
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: C)** — Rights are offered **pro rata to existing shareholders**, usually at a discount to market price.
A) Describes a public issue. B) Describes a QIP. D) Describes an ESOP.
</details>

**Q5.** In a stock split, the face value of a share:
A) Increases  B) Is reduced  C) Stays the same  D) Becomes zero
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A split **reduces the face value** and proportionately increases the number of shares.
A) and D) are incorrect. C) Face value stays the same in a **bonus issue**, which is the key contrast.
</details>

**Q6.** In a bonus issue, the face value of each share:
A) Is reduced  B) Remains unchanged  C) Doubles  D) Becomes zero
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Face value is **unchanged** in a bonus; only the number of shares rises (funded from reserves).
A) That happens in a **stock split**. C) and D) are incorrect.
</details>

**Q7.** A Qualified Institutions Placement can be made to:
A) Retail investors  B) Qualified Institutional Buyers only  C) Employees only  D) Foreign individuals only
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A **QIP** is restricted to **QIBs** — mutual funds, banks, insurers, FPIs, pension funds.
A), C) and D) are excluded from a QIP.
</details>

## 🟡 Tier 2 — Medium: Application (Q36–Q70)

**Q36.** An investor holds 200 shares priced at ₹600. The company declares a 1:1 bonus. What is the position afterwards?
A) 400 shares at ₹300; value ₹1,20,000  B) 400 shares at ₹600; value ₹2,40,000  C) 200 shares at ₹300; value ₹60,000  D) 400 shares at ₹150; value ₹60,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — A 1:1 bonus doubles the shares to **400** and halves the price to **₹300**. Value = 400 × 300 = **₹1,20,000**, exactly the original 200 × 600 = ₹1,20,000.
B) Assumes the price does not adjust — bonus shares are not free wealth. C) Ignores the extra shares. D) Halves the value, which cannot happen from a bonus.
</details>

**Q37.** An investor holds 500 shares of ₹10 face value trading at ₹2,000. The company announces a 1:10 split. What results?
A) 5,000 shares at ₹200, face value ₹1  B) 5,000 shares at ₹2,000, face value ₹10  C) 500 shares at ₹200  D) 50 shares at ₹20,000
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — A 1:10 split turns each ₹10 share into ten ₹1 shares: **5,000 shares**, price **₹2,000 ÷ 10 = ₹200**, face value **₹1**. Value = 5,000 × 200 = ₹10,00,000, unchanged.
B) The price must adjust. C) The share count must rise. D) Reverses the split direction.
</details>

**Q38.** A company announces a rights issue of 1:4 at ₹150 when the market price is ₹250. An investor holds 800 shares. How many rights shares can she subscribe to, and at what total cost?

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: A)** — A **1:4** ratio means one new share for every four held: 800 ÷ 4 = **200 shares**. Cost = 200 × ₹150 = **₹30,000**.
B) Uses the market price instead of the rights price. C) Uses a 1:8 ratio. D) Assumes one-for-one. **The discount (₹150 vs ₹250) is why rights have value and can be sold if not taken up.**
</details>

**Q39.** A shareholder neither subscribes to nor renounces a rights entitlement. What happens?

**Correct: B)** — An unexercised, unrenounced right **lapses worthless**, and because others subscribed, her **proportionate holding is diluted**.
A) Rights must be paid for; bonus shares are free. C) No compensation is due — the option was offered. D) Her percentage **falls**.
</details>

**QC3.** After the 1:5 split, what are Priya's share count, face value and price?

**QC4.** Priya is entitled to a 1:4 rights issue at ₹300 on her post-split holding. How many rights shares can she buy, and what is the theoretical ex-rights price?
A) 5,625 shares; TERP ₹213.33  B) 5,625 shares; TERP ₹230.66  C) 4,500 shares; TERP ₹300  D) 22,500 shares; TERP ₹256.67
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — Entitlement = 22,500 ÷ 4 = **5,625 rights shares** at ₹300.
**TERP** = (4 old shares × ₹213.33 + 1 new share × ₹300) ÷ 5 = (853.32 + 300) ÷ 5 = 1,153.32 ÷ 5 = **₹230.66**.

A) ₹213.33 is the **pre-rights** price — issuing new shares at a *different* price must move it. C) Miscounts the entitlement and simply quotes the rights price. D) Assumes a 1:1 entitlement.

> ⚠️ **Notice something unusual:** the TERP (₹230.66) is **higher** than the pre-rights price (₹213.33), because the rights price of ₹300 is **above** the market price. That is the reverse of a normal rights issue — and it means these rights have **no value**. See QC5.
</details>

**QC5.** Given that the rights price of ₹300 exceeds the post-split market price of ₹213.33, what should Priya do?
A) Subscribe fully, since rights are always beneficial  B) Decline the rights — she can buy the same shares more cheaply in the open market  C) Renounce the rights for a large premium  D) Sue the company
<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Correct: B)** — A rights issue is only attractive when the **rights price is below the market price**. Here she would pay **₹300** for a share worth **₹213.33** — an immediate loss of ₹86.67 per share. She should simply buy in the market if she wants more shares.
A) Rights are beneficial **only at a discount**. C) The rights have **no value** and cannot be renounced for a premium — nobody will pay for the right to overpay. D) There is nothing unlawful; badly priced rights issues simply fail and fall to the underwriters.

> ⚠️ **The lesson this caselet is built to teach:** rights issues carry value **only when priced below market**. In the earlier questions the rights price sat well below market and each right was worth ₹60–₹100; here the sequence of bonus and split pushed the market price down to ₹213.33, leaving the ₹300 rights price stranded above it. **Always compare the rights price with the CURRENT (post-adjustment) market price, not the original one.**
</details>
